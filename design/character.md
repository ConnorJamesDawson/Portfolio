# The MC — Character

Personality, voice, and progression. See `world-bible.md` for clan, bloodline
and setting; the act files for where each state sits in the story.

---

## Authored, not player-defined

**He is Geralt, not Link.** The player chooses how he expresses himself, never
who he is.

This is a prerequisite, not a preference. The design no longer supports a blank
protagonist:

- He **loses an argument to Haku** (Act 3). Losing requires holding a position.
- He **secretly agrees with Madara** (Act 8). That is interiority, and a silent
  protagonist has none the player isn't told about.
- **Guren's accusation** only lands on someone with an existing relationship to
  his family's guilt.

The earliest version of this project had a Shinobi Striker-style avatar at its
centre. That is no longer the game — one clan, one bloodline, one history. Any
character creator is now cosmetic.

---

## One trait, and everything derives from it

**Curiosity beats fear. Every time. It is a flaw as much as a virtue.**

Run the biography against it:

- Asks *why* crystal grows when nobody in the compound can answer him. *(Act 1)*
- Follows Kohaku's trail into a Kiri he has never seen at night. *(Act 1)*
- Experiments alone for five years and maims himself doing it. *(Act 2)*
- Teaches himself medicine. *(Act 4)*
- Reads seals off a dying man. *(Act 4)*
- Walks into a laboratory network that hollows people out. *(Act 5)*
- Listens to Madara's argument instead of dismissing it. *(Act 8)*

Every dangerous decision he makes is driven by wanting to know something.

**And it is precisely what makes him susceptible.** Oto is dangerous to him not
because he is weak but because they would let him find things out. One trait that
reliably overrides his self-preservation means no plot decision ever needs
justifying twice.

## The arc in one sentence

**A curious boy learns that wanting things gets people killed, spends fifteen
years doing useful work instead of wanting anything, and is then offered the
thing he stopped wanting — by the villain.**

That is why the Infinite Tsukuyomi works on him specifically. It is not merely
aimed at his losses. It is aimed at **the psychological defence he built to
survive them**: he stopped wanting so he would stop losing, and Madara offers
exactly the want he buried.

It is also why the refutation is *getting up* rather than winning an argument. He
cannot want it and keep working. He works.

## State by act

| Act | What he lets himself want |
|---|---|
| **1** | Everything. To know things, to graduate, to be worth his sister's attention. Wide open. |
| **2** | **Nothing.** Wanting cost him his family. The coldest he ever is — transactional, incurious about people, curious only about technique. |
| **3** | Haku shows him a person who wanted one thing enough to die for it and can defend the choice. He has no answer. |
| **4** | To be **useful.** Small, safe, institutional, revocable. The first want he permits himself, and he picks the least dangerous one available. |
| **5** | To **choose.** First genuine want with consequences attached — and it gets Tayuya killed. |
| **6** | To go home. Discovers home is not a thing he is allowed to want. |
| **7–8** | Nothing, again. And works anyway. |

### The constant

**His hands never stop.** Even at his worst in Act 2 he is fabricating. That is
the through-line, and it is what defeats Madara at the end — eight acts of a man
demonstrating a belief he would flatly deny holding.

### Stillness is his only stealth, and he does not know it

He has no infiltration training and never will — he was streamed to support and
never graduated. What he has is five years of finding where a thing is moving and
**not interfering with it**, which is the nearest an untrained child could come to
chakra suppression.

He does it under threat because it is the only thing he has ever been good at, not
because anybody taught him it was for this. It should never make him competent:

- It buys him **one** narrow escape, not repeated success.
- He is never told whether it worked. A guard stops, he goes still, the guard moves
  on — and the honest reading is that a tired man simply never looked over the edge
  of a culvert. Both explanations stay available.
- Where he hides matters more than how. **Every position with a good view is a
  position sentries are watching, because the view is the reason.** He survives by
  choosing places with no sightline and paying for it in what he cannot see.

Later this becomes the fortifier discipline — prepared ground, chosen positions,
winning by having been somewhere first. It starts here, as terror plus a bowl.

## Voice

- **Understates everything.** A craftsman describing his own work says *it
  holds*, not *it's beautiful*. He would describe the Konoha library as "useful."
- **Deflects emotion into mechanism.** Ask how he is and he tells you what he is
  working on. Not evasion — genuinely how he processes. Reads as cold to people
  who don't know him and as honest to people who do.
- **Answers the question asked, not the one implied.** Makes him hard to read and
  occasionally very funny.
- **He lies badly and omits superbly** — trained by Kohaku for years without
  knowing it. That is why he is a good spy in Act 5: not deception, just a
  lifetime of not saying things.
- **Apologises for things that aren't his fault and never for things that are.**

### Humour is mandatory

Sixty hours of unrelieved grimness is a tone failure — the same monotony risk
that governs the Act 1 prologue. His humour is **dry, technical, deadpan**, and
it comes out of the understatement rather than being bolted on.

**Absent in Act 2. Returns in Act 4.** Hospital banter, shop talk, the specific
gallows humour of people who work around death.

You never have to state that he is recovering. The jokes coming back says it.

## Making the arc playable

The personality should not live only in cutscenes. **Let the dialogue options
themselves be the arc** — the player experiences the character through the
interface rather than being told about him, and it costs no extra systems.

| Act | Option set |
|---|---|
| **1** | Many options, mostly questions. A child who asks things. |
| **2** | The list visibly shrinks. Two or three curt choices where there were five. The player feels him close. |
| **3** | The Haku conversation is the widest option set in the game to that point — the first time in years he argues for something. |
| **4** | Technical options return in volume. He will talk about work indefinitely and about himself never. |
| **5** | He has to **perform** a personality for the cover — the first time the player deliberately picks a self for him, which is thematically exact. |
| **6** | Almost nothing to say. |
| **7–8** | Minimal options, and the player knows precisely what he is thinking anyway. |

## Open questions

- ~~The body~~ **Resolved:** the arm is fully restored in Act 2 (slow, guided,
  months — the Hot Water medic completes what the boat medic started). The scar
  stays: wrist to elbow, three exits, the only record of the square he carries.
  The crowd wound is specific — he cannot be in a crowd facing one thing; markets
  remain his camouflage.
- **Name.** Ruri children are named after stones (see `world-bible.md`).
- **Whether he is voiced.** An authored character argues for it; a fan project's
  budget argues against. Text-only with strong writing is the safe answer.
- **How much the player can push him toward cruelty.** The design assumes a
  fixed moral floor. Whether there is a "use the stone-name" class of option —
  cruel, available, and permanently remembered — is undecided.
