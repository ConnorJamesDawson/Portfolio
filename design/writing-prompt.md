# Writing Prompt

For turning the design documents into prose fiction.

---

## How to use it

**1. Start with one scene, not an act.** Commission the Act 1 opening — the
workshop morning — at around 2,000 words. If the register is wrong, everything
built on top of it is wasted. Iterate on that one scene until the voice is right.

**2. Then commission act by act**, in scenes of 1,500–3,000 words. Do not ask for
a whole act in one pass; you will get compression and summary rather than
dramatisation.

**3. Supply the design documents with every request.** At minimum
`world-bible.md`, `character.md`, and the act file in question. Continuity across
a fifteen-year story will not survive being remembered rather than read.

**4. Keep a continuity ledger** and paste it in as the story grows. See the list
at the bottom.

## The prompt

Copy from here. Replace the bracketed section at the end with the specific
commission.

---

You are writing prose fiction — a Naruto fan novel. Not a summary, not an
outline, not a treatment. Scenes, in full, with dialogue and physical detail.

British English.

### The source material is a scaffold, not a script

The design documents supplied describe *functions*: "this establishes the
bargain," "this is the fortifier payoff," "this rhymes with Act 1." Your job is
to dramatise the function, not to execute the beat literally.

Cut, reorder, compress, and invent connective material freely where it serves
the scene. Serve the purpose; ignore the letter. A beat sheet followed
faithfully reads like a checklist.

### The authorial reasoning must never reach the page

The notes are full of analysis — parallels, rhymes, thematic labels, structural
observations. **None of it may appear as narration or dialogue.**

No character notices a theme. No narrator explains a parallel. No one reflects
on the significance of what just happened. If the reader spots the pattern they
spot it themselves, or not at all.

This is the single easiest way to ruin this story. Treat it as the first rule.

### POV and tense

**Third person limited, past tense, locked to the protagonist throughout.**

He deflects emotion into mechanism: asked how he is, he describes what he is
working on. So the narration **observes what he will not say**. It does not
analyse him, diagnose him, or announce his feelings.

Show him counting something. Show his hands. Let the reader do the rest.

Do not use first person. A narrator who thinks like him would either have to
break character to explain himself or withhold everything, and both are worse.

### Voice

- **Understatement.** A craftsman describing his own work says *it holds*, not
  *it's beautiful*. He would call the great library "useful."
- **He answers the question asked, not the one implied.** This makes him hard to
  read and occasionally funny.
- **He lies badly and omits superbly.**
- **He apologises for things that are not his fault and never for things that
  are.**
- **Dry, deadpan, technical humour** — arising from the understatement, never
  bolted on. Absent when he is at his worst; its return is how the reader knows
  he is recovering. Nobody says so.

### Canon discipline

- The protagonist is **instrumental but never decisive**. He never alters the
  outcome of a canon event. No canon character's fate changes because of him.
- **Canon characters must sound like themselves.** Zabuza is not wise. Naruto is
  not generic. If you cannot hear the character, give them fewer lines rather
  than approximate ones.
- Where the design documents flag a **deliberate liberty** or a **hard
  constraint**, respect it exactly. Those are load-bearing.
- Do not invent canon. If a detail is not in the documents and not established
  in the source, write around it.

### Pacing

Vary it. Not every scene is a set piece and not every scene is quiet. The
childhood act in particular must be **genuinely warm** — the reader can only feel
a loss proportional to what they were shown was worth keeping. If the early
material is bleak, the later material has nowhere to go.

### Do not

- State the theme, in narration or dialogue.
- Narrate a feeling the prose can imply.
- Write purple prose. The register is plain, precise, and unsentimental. Grimness
  comes from what is described, never from adjectives.
- Resolve ambiguity the design deliberately leaves open.
- Give the protagonist a wisdom or competence he has not earned in the text so
  far.
- End scenes on a summarising line. Stop earlier than feels comfortable.

### The commission

[Specify: which act, which beats, target length. For a first pass, use —
*"Write the opening scene of Act 1: the workshop morning, beats 1 and 2. Around
2,000 words. He is seven. Establish the family, the craft, his own small and
imperfect ability, and the sealed scroll. Warm. Nothing bad happens."*]

## Notes on the decisions above

**Third limited over first person.** The story is deeply interior — he secretly
agrees with the antagonist and tells no one — but the protagonist is
constitutionally unable to narrate his own feelings. First person forces the
narration either to break character or to withhold everything. Third limited
lets the prose see what he will not say.

**Past tense** carries a fifteen-year span more comfortably than present, and
keeps the register out of the breathless mode that present tense invites.

**Scene-length commissions.** Long requests produce summary. The failure is
recognisable: events reported in sequence rather than dramatised, and every
scene running at the same speed.
