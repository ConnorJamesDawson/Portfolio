# Chapter 1, Scene 4 — Four

*Act 1. A room. A boy, age twelve. POV: the boy. ~1,290 words.*

---

The door opened three times on an ordinary day.

Once for the water and the bowl. Once, later, for the bucket. Once at
the end, for the bowl going back, and after that it did not open again
and he could stop listening.

Three was ordinary. He had gone a long time on three.

Today it opened a fourth time before the bucket, and he was standing up
before he had decided to stand up, because four was not a number that
belonged to any day he knew.

---

He did not know what month it was.

He had tried to keep it once. He had marked the wall with a piece of
grit off the floor, and the marks had gone up past forty and then he had
been sick for a while — he did not know how long, that was the point of
it — and when he was not sick any more the marks were still there and
they were no longer worth anything, because there was no way to know how
many days had gone into the sick part. So the count was broken. He had
looked at it for a while and then rubbed it off with his sleeve.

After that he only counted forward from things.

*Eleven days since the last time the water came warm.*

*Two hundred and six since the man with the book.*

*Four openings today, and it is not time for the bucket.*

That was all he had. It worked as long as you did not ask it what the
date was. It was, he had decided, like being able to say how far you had
walked without being able to say where you were.

---

There were two of them.

The keeper he knew. The keeper had a name and he did not know it and had
never been given it; the keeper had a limp on the left and a smell of
cold iron and a way of coming in that meant *this is not about you, get
out of the road*, and in two years he had never once been unkind. He had
also never once been anything else.

The other man had a book.

He had seen him before. Two hundred and six days before, and some number
before that, and the number before that one had been lost in the sick
part.

"Up," said the keeper.

He was already up.

"Light," said the man with the book, and the keeper brought the lamp
round and put it on the shelf, and the room got smaller and yellower and
the corners came in.

---

The man with the book did the outside first.

He went round him the way a person goes round a table they are thinking
of buying. He looked at the hair and lifted a piece of it and let it
down. He turned his head to the light with two fingers on the jaw, one
side and then the other. He took his hand and turned it over and pressed
the flat of the thumb into the middle of the palm, and then bent each
finger back to where it stopped, and stopped where it stopped, without
any cruelty in it at all.

He looked at the teeth.

"Eating."

"He eats," said the keeper.

"All of it?"

"All of it."

"Mm." The man wrote something.

He had learned to be still for this. That was one of the things he had
worked out, and he had worked it out early and it had taken him a while
to be sure of it: *being difficult made the visit longer.* A boy who
flinched got handled twice. So he did not flinch, and the visits got
shorter, and after a while the man with the book stopped bracing his
grip before he took hold of a hand.

He had never been told to do that. He had never been told to do any of
it.

---

"Right," said the man with the book. "Arm."

He put his left arm out.

"The other one."

He put his right arm out, and turned the wrist so the underside of the
forearm was up, because that was where it was done and had been every
time.

"When you're ready."

He was ready. He had been ready since the fourth opening.

It came out of the forearm about the length of a hand — clean, white,
tapering to a point, no blood at it, because blood was a thing that
happened when it went wrong and it had not gone wrong for a long time.
It stood out of him at right angles like something that had been put
there rather than grown.

The man with the book took a strip of marked wood out of his sleeve and
laid it alongside.

"Hold."

He held.

The keeper looked at the door.

The man with the book watched the wood and not the boy, and after a
while he said, "Hm," and made a mark, and then said, "Keep holding,"
and made another mark a bit later, and then said, "Enough."

He took it back in.

The man wrote for longer than he had written before, and turned back a
page to look at something, and then wrote a bit more.

---

This was the part.

He stood with his arm still out because nobody had said to put it down,
and watched the top of the man's head, and did the thing he did, which
was to keep his face exactly as it had been for the whole of the last
four minutes and to make no noise of any kind.

He did not know what happened to the ones the book said no about.

He knew that there had been somebody along the passage, once, whose
bucket used to go out at the same time as his, because he could hear the
two doors in a rhythm — his, then a gap of about nine, then the other
one. And then one day the rhythm was his door and a gap and nothing, and
it had stayed nothing, and it was still nothing now, and it had been
nothing for — he had started that count and then stopped it, because it
was not a count that was going to be any use to him.

The man closed the book.

He closed it the way you close a book when the thing in it has come out
the way you expected. Not hard. One hand.

"He's fine," he said, to the keeper, and went out.

---

The keeper took the lamp off the shelf and the corners went away again,
and he said, "Sit down before you fall down," in the voice of a man
mentioning the weather, and went out after him, and the door was shut,
and the bolt went over.

He sat down.

His arm ached where it always ached afterwards, in the deep part, and
that was ordinary, and he put his hand round it and held on and let it
be ordinary.

*He's fine.*

He had not been sure. He was never sure. There was no way to be sure,
because the number on the wood was a number he had never been told the
meaning of — he only knew that it was the same wood every time, and that
the man laid it against him, and that whatever the wood said was the
thing he was.

He sat with his back against the wall and his arm in his lap and let it
go through him and did not make any noise while it did.

Then the bucket came, which made five.

*One,* he thought, and started again from the bucket.
