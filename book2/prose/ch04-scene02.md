# Chapter 4, Scene 2 — What He Took

*Act 1, month 4, the fifth, afternoon. Shioiri. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten.
POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,460 words.*

---

He worked out at about two in the afternoon that he was going to have to
eat something, because his hands had started doing a thing.

It was not shaking. He had been shaking on and off for two days and had
stopped noticing it. This was different: he had gone to put the slate
down on the flags after the fire and had missed the flag, not by much, by
about an inch, and had stood there for a moment looking at his own hand
with the professional interest of somebody whose entire trade was where a
hand was.

*Two days.*

*One cake on the morning of the third before school, and the water off the
runnel.*

*And an inch.*

He went down to the low house on the second course and opened the door he
had shut at three in the morning.

---

The crock had five flat cakes in it with the stone on top.

He ate one standing in the kitchen, badly, and had to stop halfway
through and put his hand on the table, and then finished it, and then
made himself wait about ten minutes before he had a second one, because
he had a general idea from somewhere that this was the correct approach
and no evidence for it whatsoever.

Then he wrapped the other three in the cloth off the board and put them
in his shirt.

*Three days if I am careful. Four if I am unpleasant about it.*

He filled the waterskin at the butt.

---

Then he stood in the middle of his own kitchen and worked out that he was
leaving.

It arrived in the flattest possible way and there was nothing in it he
could argue with, and he stood and let it go through in order.

*There is no food on this hill after the three in my shirt. Meno's crock
is the crock. Everything else is on a shelf in a house I have already
been through.*

*The nearest food is Ōura and Ōura is a day and a half.*

*There is no water problem. There is a fuel problem in eleven days and a
water problem in never.*

*And the fifth course is a house with a bed in it and no reason for me to
be in it.*

*I am not staying because I live here. I am staying because I do not know
where else there is.*

*That is not a plan. That is a place where I have stopped.*

He looked round the room.

*So: go to Ōura, and sell the tin, and then have some money, and then be
a person with money and no plan instead of a person with no money and no
plan. That is a smaller problem and it is the only one I can move.*

---

He went up to the workshop on the third to get the tin.

The tin was where it had been for two days: on the bench, open, with
eleven drops in it and about a dozen and a half of the rest of the
month's work, and one of his mother's on top because he had put it there
on the Tuesday morning before school and had not been back.

He put the lid on it.

Then he stood in front of the bench for a while, because of the frame.

---

It was where he had put it down on the night of the twenty-third, dished
side up, with the tapered fingers and the fillet at each root and the
rolled edge at the wrist.

*Take it.*

*It is nine weeks of a winter and it is the best thing anybody in this
family has made in my lifetime and I said that out loud to the man who
made it and it was true.*

He picked it up.

It was lighter than the old one. It sat further forward. The rolled edge
came under the web of his thumb exactly where he had been rubbing since
he was seven without knowing he was rubbing.

*And it is a Ruri frame that nobody else on the coast has got.*

He stood with it in both hands.

*Think about it properly.*

*I am going to walk into Ōura tomorrow with a tin of crystal and sell it,
which is a thing a boy from a crystal family does, and everybody within
four hundred yards of that counter has known my father for eleven years.
That is already a risk and I am taking it because I have to eat.*

*A frame is not a risk I have to take.*

*Anybody who has ever bought Ruri work knows what a Ruri frame looks
like. This one does not look like a Ruri frame. This one looks like a
Ruri frame that somebody has spent nine weeks improving, which is a thing
exactly one man on this coast could have done, and he is on a boat, and
the improvement is going to be the first thing anybody notices about the
work that comes out of it for the next thirty years.*

*If it turns up in a pawnshop in Ōura it is not a frame. It is a
signature.*

He put it down on the bench.

Then he opened the drawer at the back and took the old one out from under
the rag.

---

It was two hundred years old and beaten thin by somebody dead and it did
nothing at all.

The palm was flat and the fingers were the same thickness all the way out
and it had a spot on the heel where four generations of hands had worn
the copper down to about half, and there were about nine hundred of them
on that coast, and it was the most anonymous object in the building.

He put it inside his coat.

*It is worse and I am taking it because it is worse.*

*Enter that.*

He did not enter it. He had the ledger inside his shirt and he did not
get it out, and he was aware of not getting it out, and he did not
examine why.

---

He put Akagane's frame in the drawer at the back, under the rag, where
the old one had been.

He did it carefully — dished side down, so nothing could sit in the dish;
and he put the rag over it properly and not in a heap, and he shut the
drawer, and then he opened it again and looked at it, and shut it.

*Somebody will come here eventually. A house with a trade in it does not
stay shut for ever. Somebody will buy the hill or be given it or break
into it, and they will go through this bench, and they will find it.*

*And they will not know what it is. They will think it is a frame.*

*That is the whole of what happens to nine weeks.*

He went out and shut the workshop door.

---

He left at about four, up the shoulder path, with the tin under his arm
and the old frame inside his coat and three flat cakes and a waterskin
and a ledger and a corner of burnt paper and a drop with a fault in it.

And the grease tin.

He had gone back for that at the last minute, from the shed, where she
had told him to take it and use it, and it was flat and it was tin and it
weighed almost nothing and there was no argument in favour of it at all.

He put it in his coat pocket and did not cost it.

---

At the top of the shoulder he stopped, because he had stopped there every
day of his life going anywhere, and because if he did not stop there
deliberately this time he was going to find out later that he had walked
past it.

Six courses. Thirty-one roofs. Thirty of them shut and one of them open
at the top, third from the end, because he had gone in there this morning
and not shut it after him.

He looked at that for a while, and then went back — four hundred yards
up and along, which cost him twenty-five minutes and the last of the
light — and shut Take's door.

Then he came back to the shoulder and went north-east, on the top road,
towards Ōura.

*It is a day and a half.*

*I have three cakes and a tin.*

*And after Ōura I do not know.*

He walked until he could not see the path and then a bit further, and
slept under a wall about six miles on, in his coat, with the tin between
his knees.
