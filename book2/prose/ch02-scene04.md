# Chapter 2, Scene 4 — Four Seconds

*Act 1, month 3. Nakadō, the kitchen. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki.
~1,480 words.*

---

She waited until the box was away.

That was the first thing she had got right and she was proud of it
afterwards, in a bleak sort of way: she had wanted to say it on the
eleventh, in the middle of the counting, and she had made herself wait
four days until the box was back on the shelf and the table was a table
again, because you did not put a thing to somebody while their hands were
in the money.

Her father was in the back room with the door open.

That was on purpose too. He could hear everything from the back room. She
had established that at about six, in an entirely different context, and
had never once forgotten it.

"Ma. Can I say something. It's long."

Her mother said, "How long," and went on with the pot.

"About three minutes."

"Say it, then."

---

"Three days to Kayano if the road's dry," said Uzuki. "Four if it's not,
and it might not be, so call it four out and four back. I've been down to
the milestone at the fork and there's a proper road the whole way, it's
the Ōgi road and then the north one, and it's carted, so there's people
on it.

"I'd take the little cart and I'd take work with me. There's the Fusano
parcels sitting since the seventh because Iwasa's boy won't go past the
crossroads, and the man at the mill's got two for Kayano itself that
he's been sitting on because there's nobody going. I asked him. He'd pay
sixty for the two and he'd pay it up front if I asked him properly, and
that's the food for eight days with something over.

"So it doesn't cost us anything. That's the first thing and I want it
said first. It's not money. It's me not being here for nine days."

Her mother had stopped stirring.

"And I'm not asking a medic to come."

---

She had rehearsed this bit walking to Ōgi and back.

"If you ask a medic to come, what they hear is six days of walking and a
fee they won't get and a thing they can't fix, and they say no, and
they're right to say no. That's not a person being hard. That's a person
with one of them and a whole town wanting it.

"What I'd be asking for is an afternoon. Teach me the palm work — the
basic of it, the bones — and then I go away and they never see either of
us again. And that's a *good afternoon* for them, because they've got old
men's chests on their list they're doing three times a week for years,
and every one they teach out to somebody is one off the list."

She got to the end of it.

"I'd not be able to fix him," said Uzuki, because she had made herself
put that in. "Tazu says not in an afternoon and not in ten years, and I
believe him. I'd be able to do the nights. And the nights is where it
gets won and lost, and a man who sleeps four hours has got a chance."

She stopped.

The pot was going and the fire was going and there was a cart out on the
road somewhere behind the house.

"That's it," she said. "That's the whole thing."

---

"No," said her mother.

---

Uzuki stood in the middle of the kitchen floor.

She had, she discovered, prepared for six different arguments.

She had prepared for *it's too far*, and had the milestone. She had
prepared for *we can't afford it*, and had the sixty from the mill. She
had prepared for *they'll never teach you*, and had Tazu's whole thing
about the list, which was the best of it, which was the part she had been
looking forward to saying. She had prepared for *you're ten*, and for
*the road's not safe*, and for *and what if he gets worse while you're
gone*, which was the one she had been most frightened of and had an
answer for anyway.

She had not prepared for four seconds.

"You didn't — " she said.

"I did hear you."

"You didn't say anything about any of it. You didn't say the road's
wrong or the sixty's wrong or Tazu's wrong — "

"None of it's wrong," said her mother.

"Then — "

"None of it's wrong, Uzuki. It's a good plan. It's a better plan than
anything I've had." She put the spoon down on the rest, squarely, the way
she put everything down. "And the answer's no."

---

*It isn't about the road.*

She got that standing on the kitchen floor with her hands still half up
from where she had been counting things off on them, and it arrived
whole, the way the eighth count had arrived on the eighteenth try.

*She isn't arguing with it. She hasn't found a hole in it. She listened
to the whole of it and she didn't find a hole in it because she wasn't
looking, because it wasn't ever going to matter what was in it.*

*She'd decided before I opened my mouth.*

*So it's not about the road and it's not about the sixty and it's not
about whether they'd teach me.*

*It's something else and she isn't going to say what.*

"Why," said Uzuki.

"Because I said."

"That's not a reason."

"It's the one you're getting."

---

And that was the thing that did it — not the no. The no she could have
carried up the stairs. It was *it's the one you're getting*, said
flatly, over a pot, by her mother, who was the one in that house who
always gave you the reason even when the reason was awful; who had told
her about the money going out before Uzuki was seven, and about what
happened to Iwasa's first wife, and about why they did not talk to the
people at the end house.

Her mother did not do rules without reasons. Her father did that. Her
grandfather had done it, about a colour, with his hand on her head.

"You sound like Da," said Uzuki.

Her mother turned round.

"What?"

"You sound like Da when I ask about my hair." She heard her own voice go
up and could not get it back down. "*Because red's a rich colour.*
*Because I said.* That's — you don't *do* that, you're the one who — "

"Uzuki."

"— you're the one who *says* things — "

"That's *enough*."

---

It was not shouted. Her mother did not shout. It came out low and it
came out fast and it stopped the room, and Uzuki stopped in the middle of
her sentence with her mouth still open.

They stood there.

"Go and see if your father wants anything," said her mother.

"He heard all of it."

"I know he did."

"He's been lying there listening to the whole — "

"I *know* he has," said her mother.

And she picked the spoon back up and turned round to the pot, and Uzuki
looked at her back for a moment and then went out into the passage,
because there was nothing else in the room to do.

---

She did not go into the back room.

She stood in the passage instead, in the dark bit by the rack, with the
two bokken up on their pegs above her head, and she got her breathing
sorted out, because she was not going to go in there with a face on.

Through the doorway she could see the corner of the kitchen: the end of
the table, and the stove, and the shelf above it.

The tin was on the shelf.

And behind the tin, at the back, where things went that were not going
to be talked about, was the twist of cloth with the hundred in it that
she had brought back from the crossroads on a Thursday in the second
month, and which nobody had mentioned since, and which her mother had put
up there herself with her own hand while Uzuki watched.

*She put it high because a hundred's worth keeping safe.*

*She's keeping it. She's been keeping it since the second month and
she's not spending it on the roof and she's not spending it on the fire.*

*She's keeping it for something.*

Uzuki stood in the dark of the passage and worked it out, and got it
completely wrong, and felt — for about four minutes, before she went in
to her father — very much better.

*She's saving it,* she thought. *She's saving it for Kayano and she
doesn't want to say so in case it isn't enough.*

*That's why she wouldn't give me a reason.*

She wiped her face on her sleeve and went in to see whether he wanted
anything.
