# Chapter 7 — What It Cost

*Book two. Act one, month 6 (2nd) – month 7 (21st).
Ten scenes, 18,070 words.*




*Act 1, month 6, the second to the eleventh. Sakabe to Fukae. Tadayoshi
Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,980 words.*

---

The sheet said this:

> *12th. Fukae. The Kiribata, front room, before noon.*
> *Ask for Ōtsuka. He will ask you what the weather was at Sakabe.*
> *You will say: hard going on the Monday.*
> *No return required.*

Four lines, ruled off at the left, and the last line was not the same
kind of sentence as the first three.

*The first three are a man telling a boy where to go.*

*The fourth one is out of a book.*

He looked at it for a while on the second morning, sitting on a milestone
outside Sakabe with the parcel across his knees, and he could not get any
further with it than that, and he put it away.

*Required by whom.*

---

The parcel was oiled cloth over something the size of a book and about
twice the weight of one, tied with a waxed cord, and the knot was a knot
he did not know.

He learned it in about four minutes.

He did not untie it. He sat on the milestone and looked at it and turned
it over twice and followed the standing part round with his eye until he
had the whole of it, and then he could see how it went, and then he could
have put it back exactly as it was and nobody living would have known.

Then he did not do it.

---

He built the reason walking, over about a mile, in the order he built
things, because he did not trust *I ought not to* and had never once been
able to make it hold weight.

*One. What do I get.*

*I get to know what is in it. Which is a thing I cannot act on, because I
am not going to do anything about whatever it is, and which I cannot
stop knowing afterwards. So the whole of the gain is that a thing which
is currently not in my head is in my head.*

*That is not nothing. But it is not much.*

*Two. What does it cost.*

*If it is ever found out that I opened it — and it would be, in the end,
because eventually I will do it to a parcel tied by a man who is better
at knots than I am — then I am a boy who opens things.*

*And a boy who opens things does not get given things.*

*And being given things is the entire arrangement.*

He walked about a quarter of a mile on that.

*Three, and this is the one.*

*The man never told me what is in it. He did not say I am not going to
tell you, either. He simply did not tell me, and I did not notice at the
time that he had not.*

*Which means it is not about trust. It means the arrangement is built so
that the boy on the road does not have the thing in his head, so that
when somebody takes the boy on the road, the thing is not in a head that
can be opened.*

**He is not keeping it from me. He is keeping it off me.**

*And every single parcel after this one is the same arrangement, and it
is the only part of this that is looking after me, and it only works if I
never once find out.*

---

And then, right at the end of the mile, there was a fourth one, and it
arrived from somewhere he did not recognise, and he stopped in the middle
of a cart track to look at it.

*Four.*

*I do not want to know.*

He stood there.

*That is not a reason. That has no working in it at all. I cannot show
that to anybody.*

*I want it anyway.*

He wrote it down that night, because he wrote everything down, and he
wrote it exactly as it had arrived, without dressing it up, and he put a
small mark beside it of the kind Meno used to put beside a line in the
register that did not behave like the others.

*11/6. Anomaly. A preference, arrived at without arithmetic. Under
review.*

---

He had ten days for four days of walking, so he took ten.

Off the road and parallel to it, on the ridge side where the ground was
worse and the sightlines were better, coming down to the road only where
the country made him — a bridge, a cutting, a place where the ridge ran
out into somebody's crop and a boy in a crop is a boy in a crop.

He crossed the road eleven times in nine days and every single one of
them he did at a walk, in the open, in daylight, because he had worked
out in the second week of being on his own that a boy who crosses a road
carefully is a thing a carter remembers and a boy who crosses a road is
not a thing at all.

**Careful is a way of being seen.**

He entered exactly three towns.

He entered all three of them at the same two hours he always used: the
hour after first light, when the people out are the people going to work
and nobody has a reason to look at a child yet, and the hour before dark,
when everybody is tired and thinking about being indoors.

*Not the middle of the day. In the middle of the day everybody has time.*

He bought food in each of the three, once, and did not go back, because
buying food twice in one town is buying it from a man who has already had
a look at you, and the second look is where the questions live.

He slept in a byre, a stand of larch, a byre, the lee of a wall, a
charcoal burner's hut with nobody in it, a larch stand again, a ditch
that was better than it sounded, a byre, and, on the eleventh, in a
brake of dead bracken about forty minutes short of Fukae, so that he
could go in in the morning.

---

The byres cost him something and he knew what.

A byre is warm and dry and it has a roof, and the price of it is that a
byre belongs to somebody, and somebody comes to it, and they come to it
at the hour after first light, which is one of his two hours.

*So the byre buys me a night and takes a morning off me.*

He did it anyway, three times, because it was the sixth month and the
nights were still cold enough to be an argument, and he had learned in
month four what a cold night takes out of the following day.

*And I am writing that down as well, because in three months it will be
the eighth month and I will have forgotten how much I minded it.*

---

He ate: bread, twice. Cheese, once. A hot thing from a pot at Iwae for a
fortieth, which was the single best thing that happened in the nine days
and which he thought about for a long time afterwards. Bread again. Two
turnips out of a clamp, which was theft and which he entered as theft,
with the value, so that it could be paid later if it ever could be. Eggs
he found and did not take, because eggs get missed. Bread.

He had four ryō and a bit when he set out from Sakabe and he had three
and a half when he came in sight of Fukae, and he was aware, in a
distant way, that he did not know whether either of those was a lot.

*Nine a week at Sunaba, and this is nine days, and I have spent about a
half.*

*So a half is nine days of a boy eating badly.*

*And a quarter a day is what a man gets, and this man is going to hand me
ten of them for a walk I would have done for nothing, and I still cannot
make those two numbers stand next to each other.*

---

The parcel went inside his shirt, under the ledger, against his back,
which was the worst place for it in terms of comfort and the best in
terms of everything else, and he did not take it out at any point in nine
days except twice, to check the cloth for wet, and both times he put it
back without looking at the knot.

*Because looking at the knot is the beginning of the other thing.*

He noticed that too, and wrote it in the same entry.

*A rule you keep by not going near the edge of it is not a rule. It is a
distance.*

*I do not care. I will take a distance.*

---

On the ninth evening he sat in the bracken above Fukae with the town
laid out under him going orange, and did the whole thing through once,
the way he did every job for the rest of his life without ever once
calling it a rehearsal.

*Front room of the Kiribata. Before noon. Ask for Ōtsuka.*

*He asks about the weather at Sakabe. I say hard going on the Monday.*

*Then I give him the parcel.*

*Then I go.*

He turned it over.

*That is four things and none of them is difficult and I have nine days
of walking in my legs and I have not spoken more than nine words to
anybody since the first.*

*So why have I done it eleven times.*

He did not have an answer, and he was not in the habit of leaving a
question standing, so he made himself look at it properly, sitting in the
bracken with his arms round his knees.

*Because the walking is mine.*

*Nine days of ground and weather and a road I can see from above is nine
days of a thing where the only person who can get it wrong is me, and I
did not get it wrong, and I know I did not get it wrong, because I can
count the crossings and I can count the towns and I can tell you where I
slept and what it cost.*

*And a front room is four people I have never met, in a building, with
something in it I have to say out loud.*

*I have got no way of counting that at all.*

---

He looked at the town for a while longer.

Then he took the sheet out one more time, in the last of it, and read the
fourth line again, because it had been sitting at the bottom of his head
all week like grit in a boot.

> *No return required.*

*It means he does not send anything back. That is all it can mean.*

*And a man writing a note to a boy would put: nothing to bring back. Or:
that's all. Or he would put nothing at all, because if there were
something to bring back he would have said so.*

*Nobody says required unless there is a form somewhere with a box on it.*

He folded the sheet along the fold it already had.

*House style, then. Somebody in this trade writes like a clerk and
everybody has copied him.*

*Which is dull, and it is the answer, and I am going to stop looking at
it.*

He put it away.

It was the only question he raised in nine days and did not finish, and
he did not go back to it, and it did not go in the book, because a thing
you have decided is dull does not get an entry.

Then he lay down in the bracken with the parcel against his back and
slept until it was light.

*Act 1, month 6, the twenty-ninth. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV:
Uzuki. ~1,670 words.*

---

There were nine people, counting the priest and counting her.

She had it before the sake was out, because she always had it before the
sake was out, and she went round the yard once with her eyes and got
nine, and then went round again to see whether she wanted to make it
eight by leaving out the priest, and decided she did not, because he had
come and he had not been paid and both of those were true at once.

*Nine.*

*Iwasa and his wife. Ine. Kuriya. Fumi, which I did not expect and which
was kind. Tazu. The priest. Him. Her.*

*And me is ten. So nine and me.*

*Dad would have had forty.*

She put that one down and stood on it, because it had come out of
nowhere in the middle of counting and she was not going to have it in
her face in a yard.

---

It was in Shimoda's yard and not the shrine, because the shrine was for
the words and the words took about four minutes, and everything else
happened in a yard with a brick shed at the end of it and a mule looking
over the top half of the door.

Somebody had swept it. Somebody had put two planks on trestles and put
Iwasa's wife's cloth over them, which Uzuki knew was Iwasa's wife's cloth
because she had seen it on a line for four years.

The sake was in three cups and went round. There was rice. There was a
thing Ine had made that was mostly egg and was very good and there was
not much of it.

Her mother wore the grey and not anything new, and Uzuki had known she
would, and had known why, and had watched her put it on that morning
without saying one word about it in either direction, which was, she
thought afterwards, about the most grown-up thing she had ever done.

---

She counted the other thing as well.

She did not decide to. She had never once decided to; it ran on its own
and it had run on its own since she was about seven, and it ran in a yard
with nine people in it the same as it ran anywhere else.

*Tazu. Four seconds. When Mother turned round.*

*That's the third time this year, and the other two were both in the lane
and both when I was carrying.*

*Iwasa's wife looked at me for a long time and it was not that. That's
her working out whether I'm all right, which she's been doing since the
fourth month and which she has never once asked me about.*

*The priest, no.*

*Kuriya, no. Kuriya looks at the ground and at people's hands.*

*Ine, no.*

*Fumi, no, and Fumi has still not said anything to me, and she came, and
she walked forty minutes to come, and I do not know what to do with
that.*

*Him — *

She stopped that one before it finished, which she noticed at the time
and did not examine, and went and stood by the wall.

---

The thing she had come to do she did about halfway through, when the sake
had been round twice and the yard had got loud enough that a question
would be heard by everybody but would not be an event.

That was deliberate. She had thought about the timing for four days.

"Are we still going to Dad's?"

The yard did not go silent, because yards do not, but it did the thing
where about six conversations get quieter without stopping.

Shimoda had a cup in his hand and he put it down on the plank first,
which she noticed.

"Every month," he said. "Same as you've been doing. Sixth or thereabouts,
whenever the weather is."

"Right."

"And I'll come if you want me to and I'll not come if you don't." He said
it at ordinary volume, to her, in a yard with eight people listening.
"And you can change your mind about that as often as you like and you
don't have to tell me which way round it is this month. You can just say
at the gate."

*Oh.*

Ine said something to Iwasa's wife that Uzuki did not catch. Somebody's
eyes went, and she thought it was Iwasa's wife and she was not sure.

Her mother was looking at the cloth on the plank.

"Thank you," said Uzuki.

"Nothing to thank," said Shimoda, and picked his cup back up, and that
was the end of it, and the yard came back up to volume inside about four
seconds.

---

She stood by the wall afterwards and had it out with herself, properly,
because she had built the whole thing and it had come out and she was not
going to pretend she had not.

*I asked that in front of everybody on purpose.*

*I asked it in front of everybody because in front of everybody he has
to answer well, and if he answers badly then eight people have heard him
answer badly and it is on the record and I can use it.*

*And if he answers well then it is on the record that he answered well.*

*I knew both halves of that on Tuesday. I knew both halves of it and I
did it anyway, because I thought the first one was more likely.*

She looked at her feet.

*And he answered well. He answered better than well. He answered it the
way you would answer it if you had thought about it before today, which
means he has thought about it before today, which means he has been
sitting in that house working out how to be all right with a girl he does
not know.*

*And now I cannot un-ask it.*

*There are eight people in this yard who watched a man be decent to me
about my father's grave, and every single one of them is going to
remember it, and every single one of them is right.*

**You cannot put a thing on the record and then only keep the half you
wanted.**

---

The last hour was the part she was not ready for.

Ine got the priest onto something about a roof and the priest got
animated about it, and Iwasa said the thing he said about his brother
every single time there was sake anywhere near him, and Kuriya laughed,
which Uzuki had never heard and which was a bad noise and which she liked
enormously.

And at some point in the middle of it her mother said something.

Uzuki did not hear what it was. She was four feet away with a cup of
water and she was watching the mule and she heard the shape of it and not
the words, and Ine put her hand flat on the plank and laughed, and Iwasa
said "she's not wrong," and her mother put her hand up to her mouth for a
second the way she used to.

*She's not doing the thing.*

Uzuki stood very still with her cup.

*She's not doing the thing with her hands.*

For ten weeks her mother had sat at that table every night at the box and
done a thing with her hands that Uzuki had never given a name to and had
been watching without knowing she was watching: the left one flat, and
the right one on top of it, and then the right one going out to the pen
and the left one staying exactly where it was, over and over, for an
hour, every night.

*It's the counting hands.*

*She does it when she's counting and she does it when she's not counting
and she's been doing it since the fifth of the fourth month and she is
not doing it now.*

*She's been not doing it for about an hour.*

---

She stood by the wall with her cup and worked at it and it would not come
apart.

*Right. Say it.*

*My mother has not been frightened for about an hour, and I am watching
it happen, and I do not know what to do with it at all.*

*I could be glad.*

*I am not glad. I'm — *

She got as far as the word and stopped, and it took her about two more
years to be able to finish that sentence, and when she could finish it
the sentence was: *I am furious, and I am furious because it turns out
that a roof and a man with two fields buys it, and that means it was
always buyable, and my father could not buy it, and he worked until he
could not stand up.*

At ten, in a yard, with a cup of water, all she had was that something in
her chest had gone hot and that it was not a nice thing and that she was
not going to let it out in front of nine people.

So she drank the water and looked at the mule.

---

Later, when the planks were coming down, her mother came over to her by
the wall.

She did not say anything for a bit. Then she said:

"He's put the top of the door open for that animal every morning since
the fourteenth."

"I know."

"He gets up to do it. It's not on his way anywhere. He gets up and goes
out and does it and comes back in."

*She's telling me something.*

*She's telling me a thing she has decided is evidence.*

"He said he would," said Uzuki.

"Yes," said her mother. "He did."

And then she went to help Ine with the cloth, and Uzuki stood by the wall
in the dark with the empty cup, and the mule put his head over the top of
the door and looked at the yard, and there were eight people in it who
had all had a nice afternoon.

*Nine.*

*Nine and me.*

*Act 1, month 6, the twelfth. The Kiribata, Fukae. Tadayoshi Ruri, age
ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~2,000 words.*

---

The Kiribata had a front room with six tables in it and four of them had
somebody at one, and he came in at about ten in the morning and sat down
at the empty one nearest the door with his back to the wall.

*Two exits. Front, and the passage at the back that goes past the stair,
and I do not know where the passage goes, so it is one exit and a guess.*

*Four people. One at the window with a bowl. Two together on the far
side who are carters, because of the hands and the smell. One big one on
his own three tables down with a coat on that is better than this room.*

*Nobody has looked at me except the big one, and he looked for about a
second and a half, which is what everybody does to a child in a doorway.*

He put his hands on the table where they could be seen and waited, and
the parcel sat against his back under his shirt, and he had come at ten
because *before noon* meant any time before noon and ten was the hour
when a room like this is at its emptiest and its least interested.

*So. Wait until he comes in, get across the room, ask for him, and go.*

That was the plan and he had had nine days on it and it was a good plan
and it lasted about four minutes.

---

The innkeeper's wife came out from behind the counter with a cloth.

"You want something, love?"

"No."

She stopped, half turned, cloth in her hand.

*That was wrong.*

He knew it was wrong the instant it was out of him, and he knew exactly
why: *no* is what you say to a woman offering you something, and a boy in
a front room is not being offered something, he is being asked what he is
doing there, and *no* answers the surface of the question and refuses the
bottom of it.

"Then what are you doing in my front room?"

"Waiting."

"Waiting for who?"

---

And that was it.

He sat with his hands on the table and looked at a woman with a cloth in
her hand and there was one word that would end this and it was the one
word he had been told not to say.

*Ask for Ōtsuka.*

*In a room. Out loud. In front of two carters and a man at the window and
a woman who will remember it.*

*No.*

*And there is nothing else. There is not one other sentence.*

He went through them anyway, because he could not stop going through
them, and it took him under a second and a half and every one of them
came back the same:

*My mother — no.*

*A man — which man.*

*Somebody's sent me — sent you for what.*

*I'm looking for work — then why are you sitting down, and what have you
got under your shirt.*

**Every true thing I have got opens another question and I have not got
an answer to any of the second ones.**

*So make one up.*

He tried.

He genuinely tried, and it was the first time in his life he had made the
attempt, and what happened was that he got as far as an opening sound —
a breath, and the front of a word — and then there was nothing behind it
at all. Not a refusal. Not a scruple.

*Nothing came.*

*There is nothing there. There is not a mechanism. I have got a hand for
seals and a memory for hands and a book with forty-five entries in it and
I have not got the thing that everybody else in this room has, which is
the ability to open their mouth and put out a sentence that is not true.*

He sat with his hands on the table and said nothing.

---

The silence went on for about three seconds and it was the loudest thing
that had happened in that room all morning.

The two carters had stopped. The man at the window had turned round on
his bench.

"Whose are you?" said the innkeeper's wife, and it was louder now, and
it was not unkind, and that was the worst part of it — **she was not
being nasty, she was doing the thing that a person does about a child in
a building at ten in the morning who will not say whose he is.**

*And she is right to.*

*She is going to ask twice more and then she is going to send the girl
for somebody, and the somebody will be a man with a rank in this town,
and he will not hurt me, and he will take me to a room, and in the room
there will be a book, and somebody will write down a boy of about ten,
and it will go up at month end with everything else that gets written
down in this country.*

*Eight seconds. Maybe ten.*

*And I can see the whole of it and there is not one single thing I can
do about any of it, because everything I can do is a thing you do to
ground and this is a room.*

He had, in that moment, one clear thought, and he was ashamed of it for
about four years.

*I want a wall behind me and forty yards of open in front and I want
somebody to try to hit me, because I would know exactly what to do.*

---

"—and I said to his mother, I said, *he doesn't want the mill*, and did
she listen?"

The big man three tables down was already talking.

That was the first thing about it and it was the thing Tadayoshi came
back to afterwards for about a year.

**He did not start. He was already in the middle of it.**

Halfway through a sentence, at volume, to nobody — and the room turned
round to find a conversation that had apparently been going on for some
time and that they had all somehow missed the beginning of.

"Did she listen. Did she." He got up. He was a big man and he got up the
way big men get up, in stages, using the table. "*Twelve years* that
family has had that mill and there is not one of them can tell you what a
tail race is for. Not one."

"Ōtsuka," said the innkeeper's wife, in a completely different voice.

"And there he *is*," said the big man. "Sitting there. Look at him."

---

He came down the room and he did not come at Tadayoshi, exactly; he came
past the innkeeper's wife and turned as he came, so that when he arrived
he was standing between her and the table, with his back three-quarters
to her, still complaining.

"Sit *up*."

A hand came down on Tadayoshi's shoulder and took a good deal of his
coat with it and put him about a foot to the left and into the other
chair, the one at the far side of the table, and the movement turned him
towards the wall and away from the room.

"Nine days," said Ōtsuka, to the ceiling, to the carters, to everybody.
"His mother says a week. I said to her, I said, a week, from Sakabe, on
those legs? And here he is on the twelfth like I said and she'll tell
everybody it was a week." He sat down heavily on the bench beside him.
"What was the weather?"

*What?*

*That is — *

*That is it. That is the thing. He has put it in the middle of the mill.*

"Hard going on the Monday," said Tadayoshi.

"Course it was," said Ōtsuka, disgusted, to the room. "Course it was. And
she'll have him out again on Thursday. *Thursday.* Woman's never walked
past the shrine in her life."

One of the carters laughed.

"You want feeding," said the innkeeper's wife.

"He wants feeding, I want feeding, we all want feeding, and what I want
is somebody in that family to tell me what a tail race is *for*."

The room came back up.

---

It took Tadayoshi about forty seconds to work out what had happened.

He sat with his back to the room and his hands flat on the table and the
big man beside him going on about the mill to anybody who would take it,
and he was still, somewhere in the back of himself, waiting for the girl
to be sent for somebody.

Then he moved his shoulder.

*The parcel's gone.*

He did not look down and did not put his hand to it; he knew before he
had finished the thought that both of those were things you do not do,
and he had learned that from nobody, and it was one of about four things
he could do that nobody had taught him.

*It's gone.*

*It went when he moved me into the chair. His hand came down on my
shoulder and my coat and it went across my back on the way past and it
went into — *

He let his eyes go along the bench without moving his head. There was a
coat on it, folded, that had not been on it four minutes ago.

*There.*

---

And then the rest of it arrived, all at once, and it was the most
frightened he had been in the whole of the fifth and sixth months,
including the riverbed.

*He turned a room.*

*There were seven people in this room and every single one of them was
looking at me and there was a woman four feet away from me who was about
nine seconds from sending for somebody, and he stood up and started
talking in the middle of a sentence about a mill and now that has not
happened.*

*It has not happened. It is not that it stopped. It is that they have all
got a different morning now.*

He went back over it. He went back over it three times, and each time he
could name every physical thing that had occurred and could not, at any
point, find the place where the thing had been done.

*He never said a word to me until he was already sitting down.*

*He made himself the boring one. He made himself a man everybody in this
room has heard about a mill from before, so that nobody in this room is
going to think about him for one second after he goes out of that door.*

*And he did it as loudly as it is possible to do anything.*

*I have spent five months learning how not to be looked at. He did the
opposite of the whole of it, in about eleven seconds, and it worked
better.*

He sat with his hands on the table.

*I could not do that if I had a year.*

*I could not do it if somebody wrote it down for me and I learned it off
by heart, because it is not a thing you can write down. There is no
sequence. He looked at a room and he knew what it wanted and he gave it
that, and I have watched it happen from four feet away and I could not
tell you what he did.*

**And I did not see it happening. That is the part. I was in it and I did
not see it happening.**

---

Ōtsuka finished with the mill.

He did it the way he had started it — not finishing it, exactly, just
letting it run down into the room and be over — and then he put both
hands on his knees and looked at Tadayoshi for the first time, properly,
with an entirely different face on.

"Right," he said, quietly. "Through the back. Mind the step, it's not
where you think it is."

*Act 1, month 7, the second. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki.
~1,680 words.*

---

It took four journeys and Iwasa's cart for one of them, and the whole of
what they owned went up the lane in about three hours, and that was the
part nobody had warned her about.

*Three hours.*

*Fifteen years of two people and then me, and it's three hours and a cart
and a bit.*

*Hand on. What's different. Where does it hurt. And do those two agree.*

*I could put my hands on her and I would know in about forty seconds. Not
guess. Know. It's the one thing in the world I can actually do and it is
standing four feet away from me with a bit of kindling in it.*

---

So she went and did it.

She crossed the kitchen and reached out and took her mother's hands the
way she had taken them on the twenty-seventh, off her lap, at the table,
in the old house, when neither of them had said a word about it.

Her mother moved them.

It was not violent and it was not fast. She turned, in the same motion,
and put the kindling down on the shelf, and used the turn to take her
hands out of it, and it was so ordinary that a person standing in the
doorway would have seen a woman putting down a bit of wood.

Uzuki stood with her own hands out in front of her.

*She's never done that.*

*She has never once in my life moved her hands away from me.*

She put them down.

*Right.*

*Then I know.*

---

She sat outside on the step for a while afterwards, in the cold, before
anybody else was up, and she did the rest of it in order, because doing
it in order was the only thing she had that worked.

*One. There is nobody.*

*There were nine people in that yard on the twenty-ninth and every single
one of them watched him be decent to me about my father's grave, and they
were right, and they all remember it. If I go to Ine and say what I heard
through a wall, Ine will be kind to me and then Ine will be uncomfortable
and then Ine will tell my mother, because Ine is fond of my mother, and
that is exactly what a kind person does.*

*Two. The hundred is spent.*

*It went in the left column on the twenty-seventh of the sixth month and
I watched her do it. It is not a hundred any more. It is a house and a
range and meat twice in a fortnight and a room with a door on it, and
there is nothing to give back.*

*Three. The rent.*

*The rent is his. Kuriya has eleven widows and he does not move things
for anybody and he told me so at his own gate, and if we went back down
the lane the house is let by now anyway, because Kuriya does not leave a
house empty for three weeks.*

She got to the fourth one and sat with it for a long time, and the sun
came up on the far side of Iwasa's while she was doing it.

*Four.*

*She told me she wasn't doing it for me.*

*She told me on the twenty-seventh, at the table, with the box out, and
she said it in that flat voice and she said exactly why she was saying
it: because if she said it was for me then I'd owe her, and I'd carry it,
and in about four years I'd work out what she'd charged me for it.*

*So there's no debt.*

*She made sure of that. She sat down and made sure of it on purpose,
because she thought it was the kindest thing she had left, and it
probably was.*

*And it means that this morning I have got nothing to spend.*

*I cannot go in there and say you owe me. She took that off the table
before I was ten and she told me she was doing it and I said all right.*

She put her chin on her knees.

*And the other one I could have used, she used up before six o'clock this
morning on a bucket.*

---

She got the flour bag out that night with the door shut.

The first column was the ninety and she wrote twenty-nine in it, because
she had done twenty-nine, and it had been a bad one and she wrote it
anyway.

The second column was the money and there was nothing to put in it,
because Kuriya paid at month end and it was the twenty-first.

The third column said 1,679.

She looked at it for a while.

*Twenty-fourth of the sixth month I wrote seventeen hundred and four and
I took one off and it was — *

*It was a plan. That's what it was. It was a number I made up in the dark
in a kitchen because I was ten and I had nothing and I wanted there to be
a day at the end of it.*

She took one off.

1,678.

It went down onto the flour bag in the same hand as everything else and
it was not the same object at all, and she sat and looked at it and could
not have told anybody what had changed about it.

---

Then she turned the bag sideways and ruled a fourth column with the edge
of her thumbnail, the way her father had ruled a chit when there was no
rule in the house.

She sat with the pen over the top of it.

*Write what it is.*

*Everything on this bag has got a thing at the top of it. The ninety's
got a thing. The money's got a thing. I put the date at the top of the
second one on the fourteenth of the fifth month because Dad put the date
on everything.*

*So write what it is.*

She held the pen there for about four seconds.

Then she moved it down, past where a heading would have gone, and left
that part empty, and put one mark in the column and nothing else.

She looked at it.

Then she put the bag behind the brick, and did it from the door — down on
one knee, two fingers, in, brick back — and it took about two and a half,
and she lay down on the mat in her own room with the door shut, in the
house with the roof that did nothing when it rained.
