# Chapter 6, Scene 6 — The Last Hundred Yards

*Act 1, month 5, the nineteenth. Above Nezu. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten.
POV: Tadayoshi. ~2,180 words.*

---

He wrote the riverbed on the nineteenth, eleven days after, on a slope of
burnt grass above the town with his back to a wall that had once been
part of something.

It came to nine lines and it took him two hours.

He had the first four inside twenty minutes, because the first four were
where and when and how many and what they had been told, and those were
facts and facts went down the way facts went down.

The fifth line was the distances. That took forty minutes, and not
because he did not have them — he had walked them, he had them in paces
and he had converted them on the day — but because there is a difference
between having a number in your head and writing *ninety yards* on a page
under a name, and he did not find that out until the pen was on the
paper.

He wrote *Miyo* and then had to stop for a while.

The seventh line was the one he could not do. He built it four times. He
got as far as *the fourth one, four feet, one point, and I cannot
determine* and then sat with it, and the trouble was that the sentence
needed an end and every end he could put on it was a decision, and he was
not going to decide a thing like that in order to finish a line.

In the end he wrote it in two halves with a stroke between them, the way
Meno used to rule a column when the total was not yet known, and left the
right-hand half empty.

*It stays empty. It stays empty until there is something to put in it.
An empty half is honest. A guess is not.*

The ninth line was three words long.

Then he put the ledger inside his shirt and did not get up.

---

He did not get up for about five hours.

He was aware of that at the time. He was aware of it in the way you are
aware of weather, and at some point in the middle of the afternoon he
made a note of it — *five hours, sat, no cover, no watch kept, in the
open above a town where a boy is being looked for* — and did not do
anything about it, and made a second note that he had not done anything
about it.

*That is a fault. That is the biggest fault I have got.*

*I can find out what is going to happen to me and then not stand up.*

---

He saw the man from about four hundred yards.

He was coming up the cart track from the town at a walk, and he was
alone, and he did not stop and look and he did not go into cover, and he
did not once change his speed, and Tadayoshi watched all of that happen
and got up onto his feet and got his back into the corner of the wall and
had the tin out and open in his left hand.

*Ram. Boar. Ox. Snake. Ready and not made.*

*Grit under my feet and the wall behind me. That is the whole of what I
have got up here and it is not much.*

At forty yards the man stopped.

Then he sat down on the bank at the side of the track, with his knees up
and his hands where they could be seen, and looked out at the bay.

*He sat down.*

Tadayoshi stood with the tin in his hand for a long moment.

*Nobody sits down.*

*Nobody in the world sits down at forty yards from a thing they are
going to fight. You do not get up out of that in under a second and a
half and he knows it and he did it where I could see him do it.*

*He has just spent his only advantage to tell me something.*

---

"You went down that bed from the north end," said the man, to the bay.

Tadayoshi did not answer.

"You went down from the north end, and when it started you got into the
channel, because the footing in the channel's the worst in the bed and
it was the only cover there was, and it makes anybody who comes at you
come at a walk." He was not loud. He was about the right amount of loud
for forty yards, which meant he had done it before. "That's the right
answer. I don't think anybody taught you it."

*Nobody taught me it.*

*Do not say that.*

"You laid to seaward and you laid behind and you left the landward bank
open, which is not a mistake, it's an offer, and one of them took it, and
that's the one you got at fifty-four yards."

*He has been down there.*

*He has been down there and he has done it with his feet, the same as I
did, and he has got fifty-four out of it, which is the number, which is
my number.*

"Four of them," said the man. "And you're ten."

He turned his head and looked at Tadayoshi for the first time.

"I'm not going to ask you about the last hundred yards."

---

Tadayoshi stood in the corner of the wall with an open tin in his hand
and found that he had nothing at all.

He had built about nine things to say on the way up from Nezu, over three
days, against about four kinds of person, and none of them had a man
sitting on a bank in them, and none of them had *that*.

*He knows.*

*He has walked ninety yards of a coast road with a rule and he has
counted the points in a girl's back and he knows the thing I have been
carrying for eleven days and he has just told me he is not going to make
me say it.*

*That is a kindness.*

*Work out what it costs. Everything costs. Work out what it costs before
you take it.*

He could not work out what it cost. He stood there and could not do it,
and that was new, and it frightened him considerably more than the man
did.

And underneath all of that, low down and steady and not to be got rid of,
there was a thing he had not expected at all, and it was this:

*Ask me.*

*I want you to ask me.*

---

He said, "Which was it?"

The man did not pretend not to understand it.

"Say what you're asking."

"Was the one at four feet the last thing I did while I was still —" He
stopped. He had never said any of this out loud and there were no words
in him that were the right size for it, and so he used the ones he had.
"Was he before, or after."

The man looked at the bay for a while.

"I don't know," he said. "And you won't."

"You've been down there."

"I've been down there and I've been down four of those," he said, "in
nineteen years. Four fields that read like that one. Two of them were
men who'd had a fright and lost hold of themselves and did four minutes
of something they couldn't tell you about afterwards, and one was a man
who was exactly what he looked like, and one I never found out." He put
his hands on his knees. "There's no mark on the ground that tells you
which. There's no mark on a body that tells you which. It's not in the
evidence, son. It was never in the evidence. It's a thing about the
inside of your head and the inside of your head didn't leave any."

"Then how do I—"

"You don't."

*No.*

"And I'll tell you the other half," said the man, "because you'll get to
it yourself in about a year and it'll be worse if you get there on your
own. That difference is worth nothing to anybody but you. Not to the
four of them. Not to their people. Not to the office in Kiri that's going
to put the four of them on a sheet. Not to me. There is not one person
alive who is going to price it, ever, and you're going to carry it your
whole life anyway, and those two facts have got nothing to do with each
other."

---

Tadayoshi stood very still and did a thing that he did not understand
until he was about nineteen.

He filed it.

He took the man's sentence, which was the worst sentence anybody had ever
said to him, and he put it down in the place where he put findings, with
the date on it, in order, next to *a fight nobody survives still makes a
report*, and he felt the small, exact, disgusting satisfaction of having
got a true thing written down.

*Nineteenth. The question I cannot stop asking has no market value.*

*Confirmed by a man who has seen four.*

He noticed himself doing it. That was the part he came back to for years
— not the sentence, but that he had heard it, and put it in the book, and
had felt *better for the entry*.

---

"Now the useful thing," said the man, "and then I'll go, because I've
been sat on this bank eleven minutes and that's about my limit for a
piece of open hill."

"You haven't asked me anything."

"I've not asked you your name and I'm not going to and don't offer it."
He said that quite hard, and it was the only time. "Right. You did one
thing wrong down there, and it's the only one that matters, and it's not
the one you think."

*The staffing return.*

*He cannot know about the staffing return.*

"You left your work standing."

Tadayoshi opened his mouth and shut it.

"Four of them in the bed at the north end, eleven feet, tips up, a hand's
width. Straight as a wall. Still there on the fifteenth when I went
down." The man got up off the bank, unhurried, and brushed the back of
his coat. "They're not measuring the dead ones, son. A dead one tells
them a boy of that age can be killed and how fast. That's a page. Your
*work* is a book. How long it stands up. How wide it's set. Whether the
points are the same as each other, which they are, and what that says
about the man making them. Which way they're facing, so they know which
way you were looking. Whether there's anything in the material and there
is, there's a grain in it, and it goes the same way in all of them."

*Grain.*

*They can see the grain.*

"Somebody in Kiri's got all that on a sheet by now, and the next lot they
send will have read it on the boat." He looked up the hill and then down
it, once each, the way he had been doing every ninety seconds or so
without ever seeming to. "So. Next time you leave, take your work with
you."

"I can't."

"Say why."

"Because it doesn't go away." Tadayoshi heard his own voice do something
and did not stop. "The four fences I could have knocked down and it would
have taken me a minute and I didn't because I was — because of the state
I was in. But there's two hundred of them in a stand at the top end and I
did not put those there. I don't know they're there until afterwards.
There is no version of it where I go round and pick up two hundred
things I don't remember making."

The man stood in the track and thought about that for a while, and did
not say anything comforting, and did not say anything at all for long
enough that Tadayoshi understood he was being taken seriously for the
first time in his life by an adult who was not related to him.

"Then that's what you are," he said at last. "And you'd better be paid
for it, because you're going to pay for it either way."

---

He started down the track and then stopped.

"I'm at the yard behind the bell house at Sakabe on the first," he said.
"Not the town, the yard. From the first for three days and then I'm gone
north and you'll not see me again."

"Sakabe's shut."

"Sakabe's open on the twenty-eighth and I know that and you don't, and
that's the first thing I've given you and you can have it."

"What do you want?"

"I want a boy who can put eleven feet of that across a road," said the
man, "and I'm going to tell you exactly what for, on the first, out
loud, and then you can say no."

He went about twenty yards.

"You'll want to be off this hill," he said, without turning round. "You
can be seen from the bar."
