# Chapter 7, Scene 8 — The Chair

*Act 1, month 7, the twentieth. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV:
Uzuki. ~1,670 words.*

---

It had been a completely ordinary day and she went over it afterwards
about four hundred times looking for the part that was not, and there was
not one.

He had been out at the far field until about six. Her mother had done a
thing with the barley that had turned out well and had said so twice.
Uzuki had been up at Kuriya's in the afternoon — it was the twentieth,
which was a Tuesday, and Tuesday and Friday were the days — and had come
back with her hands still not right and had sat in the yard with the mule
until they were.

They had eaten. Somebody had said something about a gate again. Her
mother had laughed, once, briefly, at the beginning of it and not at the
end.

Uzuki had gone to bed at about the usual time and had said goodnight to
both of them and both of them had said it back.

---

She did the ninety at the wall.

She did the ninety at the wall every night now, because the brick was the
coldest thing she could reach without getting off the mat and because it
did not care and because it was one hundred per cent hers, and she had
been doing it there for eighteen nights.

Twenty-nine. Then thirty. Then twenty-nine again, which had annoyed her.

That night she got to about fifty and stopped, because it was going
badly, and lay on her back with her hands on her stomach and listened to
the shape of them talking through the wall, which she had stopped
noticing about a fortnight ago the way you stop noticing a stream.

Up and down. His, then hers, then his.

---

Then it went to one voice.

She noticed that the way you notice a clock stopping: not the sound, the
absence of the other half of it. His went on, and hers did not come, and
his went on, and hers did not come, and it was low and level and it went
on for a while, and there was no going up and down in it any more because
going up and down is what two people do.

Uzuki lay on her mat with her hands on her stomach.

*She's not answering.*

*Why isn't she answering.*

---

Then the chair went over.

It was a completely ordinary noise. It was the noise a chair makes in a
kitchen four feet the other side of a brick wall when it goes over
backwards on a stone floor, and she had heard chairs go over in her life
perhaps twenty times, and every single one of those had been Iwasa's boy
being an idiot or her father getting up too fast or the wind.

She sat up.

*That's the chair.*

And then — and this was the one, this was the thing, this was the thing
she carried for the rest of her life and could produce in one second at
any moment for thirty years — somebody stood it back up.

The little grate of it. The three-inch scrape as it came round the right
way. The knock of the legs coming down.

*Somebody's picked it up.*

*You don't pick a chair up in the middle of an argument.*

*You pick a chair up when the thing that knocked it over is finished.*

---

She sat on her mat in her own room in the dark with her back against the
brick.

There were two more sounds after that and she never got a word for either
of them, at ten or later, and she was not going to make one up.

One was short and had nothing in it and was not a word.

The other one was not a sound a piece of furniture makes.

And then his voice again, low and level, going on, the same as before,
and going on for what felt like a very long time and was probably about
forty seconds.

*Get up.*

*Get up. Get up and open the door and go in.*

She did not.

*Get UP.*

She had her hands flat on the brick behind her, which was where they had
been when she sat up, and she was not able to move them, and she was
completely aware that she was not able to move them, and there was no
part of her that did not know exactly what was happening on the other
side of that wall.

*I don't know what it is.*

*Yes I do.*

*I don't know what it's called.*

*I know what it is.*

---

And then it stopped.

That was the thing that finished it. That was the piece of information
that made it not something else.

*An argument doesn't stop.*

*An argument runs down. An argument gets slower and one of them says a
last thing and there's a gap and then somebody says a small thing and
then it's over, and Mum and Dad had about nine of them a year and I know
exactly what one sounds like from four feet away because I grew up four
feet away.*

*That stopped.*

*It stopped like a door.*

---

She did not go in.

She sat with her back on the brick and her hands on the brick and she did
not go in, and at some point in the next minute she understood that she
was not going to be able to make herself go in by telling herself to, and
so she did the only thing she had.

*Right.*

*I'll go at ninety.*

She put her palms flat.

*Little. Stop. Wait.*

*One.*

---

It was the worst ninety she ever did and she did it correctly.

*Little. Stop. Wait.*

*Two.*

*Three.*

The wait is the work. The wait had always been the work; Anzai had said
it in a kitchen in another village with a cup of water and she had thought
at the time that he was being clever, and he had not been being clever,
he had been describing the actual job, which is that you take your hands
off the going and you sit in the gap and you do not lean.

*Four.*

*I'll go at ninety.*

*Five.*

Every single gap was a place she could have got up.

She knew that at the time. That was not something she worked out
afterwards and put back into it; she knew it at eleven and at nineteen
and at thirty-four, sitting on a mat in the dark with her hands on a wall
in a house that did not leak, that the gaps were doors and that she was
walking past one of them every three seconds.

*Twelve.*

*I'll go at ninety.*

*Thirteen.*

---

At about forty it went quiet properly.

Not the stopping — the other quiet, the one after, where somebody is
moving about and the moving about is ordinary. Water. A pot. Somebody
putting something on a shelf.

*She's up.*

*She's up and she's doing the pot.*

*So it's — *

*So she's all right.*

She had that thought and it went through her like something hot and she
had to put both hands flat and hold still for a second, because she knew
what it was the instant she had it.

*That's not relief. Don't you dare call that relief.*

*That is you being let off.*

*Forty-one.*

*I'll go at ninety.*

*Forty-two.*

---

She did not stop at forty and she did not stop at sixty.

At sixty she wanted to push. That was the sixtieth the way the sixtieth
always was — the absolute certainty that nothing was happening and had
never happened and the whole thing was made up — except that this time
the thing she was certain of was not about her hands.

*It didn't happen.*

*A chair went over and somebody picked it up. People knock chairs over.*

*Push.*

*Don't push.*

*Sixty-one.*

*I'll go at ninety.*

She did not push. She had been taught by a man with a cup of water that
pushing undoes the hour, and she had never once pushed, and she did not
push then.

*Seventy.*

*Eighty.*

*Eighty-nine.*

*Ninety.*

---

She took her hands off the wall.

You cannot tell during. That was the other half of it, the half Anzai had
said four times: whatever you think is happening under your hands, you do
not know, and you will not know, and forty seconds after they come off
you will find out.

So she counted the forty seconds, because that was the job, and she sat
in the dark in her own room with her hands in her lap and counted to
forty.

And then she listened.

---

There was nothing to listen to.

The house was quiet in the ordinary way a house is quiet at night. There
was no sound from the kitchen. There was no sound from their room. There
was nothing anywhere that a person could have taken to anybody and said
*this, listen to this*.

She sat there for a long time with her hands in her lap.

*I said I'd go at ninety.*

*It's ninety.*

She did not move.

*It's ninety and I'm not going, and I knew at about six that I wasn't
going, and I did the other eighty-four anyway.*

She lay down eventually and pulled the blanket over and looked at the
ceiling of the room that was hers, with the door shut, in the house with
the roof that did nothing when it rained.

*I'm going to say something in the morning.*

*I'm going to get up and say something in the morning and I'm going to do
it before I've thought about it, because if I think about it I'll build a
reason not to and I'm good at those.*

*That's the plan. That's tomorrow.*

She lay in the dark with her eyes open for a while.

Then she reached out, without particularly deciding to, and put one hand
flat on the brick, and left it there.
