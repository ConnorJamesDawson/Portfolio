# Chapter 12, Scene 9 — Some Time Before

*Act 1, month 12, the seventeenth. The hall of the Kadono-dera. Nozaki
Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~3,000 words.*

---

Some time before.

---

The bowl did not come.

That had never happened. It had not happened once in a hundred and
thirty-five bowls across forty-five days, and it had not happened in the
eighteen days upstairs before that, and she had built the whole of her
head on it for six weeks.

She knew it was late at the point where the strip of light under the door
had moved past the place it was at when the bowl came, and she knew it was
not coming about twenty minutes after that.

In between those two she went up the four steps and put her hand on the
door.

It was locked.

---

She came back down and sat on the mat with her back against the wall and
did the only work available, which was to go back over the morning and
find where it had stopped being an ordinary morning.

*The bell went. The bell was right.*

*Feet at the ordinary time over the north end. Then nothing over the north
end at all, which is wrong, because the north end is four of them and they
are up before the bell and they walk about.*

*So they moved the north end before the bell.*

*And they have locked a door that has not been locked since the fourth day
of the eleventh month.*

She put her hands flat on her knees and got the rest of it in about four
seconds and it did not frighten her, and she noticed that it did not, and
put the noticing away with everything else.

*Today, then.*

---

They came at about ten.

It was Tokuji and two she did not know, and Tokuji came down the steps
first and stood at the bottom of them and did not look at her, which he
had not done since the second of the eleventh month, and one of the others
said, "Up you come," in the voice you use to a dog you are fond of.

She got up.

She got her sacking, because it was hers and it had four columns on it and
one of them had a date, and one of the men took it off her at the top of
the steps and looked at it for about a second and dropped it on the ground
without any malice at all, the way you drop a thing a child has picked up
on a walk.

She looked back at it once from about eight feet.

Then she did not look at it again, and forty years later she could still
have drawn the exact place on the ground where it fell, and the way the
edge of it had gone up against a stone.

---

The light was the thing.

She had not been outside since the second of the eleventh month, which was
forty-five days, and it was the middle of the morning in the twelfth month
under a white sky, and she came up four steps into it and it went through
her head like a hand.

She stopped on the top step with her eyes shut and one of the men waited,
which she noticed.

*He waited.*

*He is not in a hurry and he is not unkind and he waited for a child to be
able to see, and I have been in this building nine weeks and every single
person in it has been like this the whole time.*

She got her eyes open and walked across the ground between them and did
not need to be told which way.

---

The hall was dim after the yard and she came in with her eyes wrong again
and had to stand still for a second in the doorway, and somebody's hand
came round her upper arm from behind and moved her four feet forward and
stopped her.

She got the room in about the time it took the hand to do that, because
she had been doing rooms since the sixth month.

**Five men.**

Enkai at the table on the left with his book, not sitting, because he never
sat.

Two by the east door, one of whom was the one from the fifteenth with the
coat that had dried on him, and one who was not.

And two in the middle of the floor with her, one on each arm, and neither
of them holding hard, because there was no need and everybody in the room
knew it.

*Five and me.*

*Two doors, one shuttered window, and the window is not a way out for
anybody over about nine.*

She stood where they put her, facing the table, with her back to the south
door.

---

They talked about her for about two minutes and she heard all of it.

It was the correction. The man from the fifteenth had written something on
his sheet and there was a difference between his sheet and Enkai's book
and they were settling it standing up, in the ordinary way, the way two
men settle a difference about a weight.

She could not follow the numbers. She followed the shape of it, which was
that the buyer's man wanted something off and Enkai was giving him less
than he wanted and both of them were entirely comfortable.

Then Enkai said a thing she did not understand, about the second column
and the second end of it and what that end paid.

And the man on her left — one of the two on the floor, who had not said
anything at all up to that point and who she had never seen before that
morning and who had a good coat and a cold in his nose — said the end of a
sentence into the middle of it.

He said it flatly, and to the room, and in the tone of a man clearing up a
small point that was holding everybody up.

**"She doesn't have to be pure for that end of it."**

---

Then the door moved behind her.

She could not see it. What she saw was the light change on the floor in
front of her, a long shape of it opening across the boards from behind her
right shoulder, and every face in the room going to the door over the top
of her head.

And nobody straightened up.

That was the thing she got first and she got it before she got anything
else, because she had spent forty-five days learning a building off the
way people moved in it, and what a room does when somebody it respects
comes in is that it goes about half an inch taller, and this room did not
do anything.

Nobody stood up straighter. Nobody stopped. Enkai did not close the book.

*Nobody knocked.*

*Somebody has walked into a room with five men in it and did not knock,
and none of them has done anything about it, and that is because they have
not worked out yet what it is.*

She turned her head as far as the hand on her arm let her, which was
enough.

---

It was a boy.

He was her own size. That was the first thing and it was the only thing
that mattered for about a second and a half: he was ten or eleven, and he
was standing two paces inside the door with the light behind him and a
coat that was not enough for a mountain, and he had come in the way you
come into your own kitchen.

He was not looking at her. He was looking at the room, left to right,
and she knew exactly what he was doing because it was what she had been
doing four seconds earlier, and she had never once seen anybody else do it.

*He is counting the doors.*

And then the man on the left finished his sentence, because he had started
it before the door moved and a man finishes a sentence, and the last four
words of it went out into the room with a boy standing in it.

---

She said it.

She would not be able to say why, at eleven or at fifteen or at any point
in her life, and she went at it properly more than once and never got
anywhere, because there was nothing under it.

She had learned in the eighth month that asking is the thing you do not
do. She had held to it through a brick wall and a road and a hall floor
with a man and a book, and she had not asked one single person for one
single thing since the fourth of the ninth month, and she had built the
whole of herself on that, and she knew she had.

And she looked at a boy her own size in a doorway and it came out of her
with nothing under it at all.

**"Help."**

---

And then hell broke loose.

---

She lost the next part and never got it back.

That is the exact truth of it and she was clear about it always, and she
was clear about it in a way that annoyed people who wanted a better story:
**she did not see him cross the floor.** There was a boy in a doorway
eleven feet away, and there was a sound she did not identify, and then
there was no man on her left.

Her arms were free and she did not know when.

She was looking at the boards, at the man from the left side, who was
down, on his face, with his legs still doing something, and there was a
great deal happening at the back of his neck.

And when she got her head up the boy was standing in front of her.

---

Between her and the room.

Not beside her. In front of her, square, with his back to her and his
shoulders across the whole of the space where the two men on the floor had
been, and the second one of those was going down on her right at that
moment and she did not see how that had happened either.

She stood four feet behind a boy her own size and could not see past him.

Then he turned round.

---

He looked at her.

That is the whole of it and she gave that account of it for the rest of
her life and nobody ever found it interesting, and that was correct,
because there was nothing in it to find interesting.

He turned round and looked at her, from about four feet, for something
under a second — she counted it afterwards against her own pulse and got
*under one* and never got closer than that — and his face did nothing at
all. He did not speak. He did not check her over. He did not reach out. He
did not look at her eyes and he did not look at her hands; he looked at
her the way you look at a thing on a road that is not moving.

And then he turned back round and went into the room.

---

She had time to think one thing while it happened and she thought it
straight away and it was not about him.

*He is not going to.*

*I am not on it.*

---

After that she watched.

---

There is no version of what follows in which she is a child hiding her
face, and she never once claimed there was.

She stood on the hall floor of the Kadono-dera at about half past ten in
the morning on the seventeenth of the twelfth month with two dead men
inside four feet of her and she watched every single second of it with her
hands at her sides, and what she felt while she was doing it was not
horror and was not fear and was not gratitude.

It was the thing you feel watching somebody do a job properly.

She had felt it exactly once before, at Shimoda's, in the sixth month, in
a yard, watching a man go over a mule out loud — and that time it had been
the wrong way round and she had been the mule. This time she was standing
behind it.

*He does not go round anything.*

That was the first note she made and she made it inside about three
seconds, and she made it the way she had made *eleven trips is eight
trips*, which is to say she could not not.

*Everybody in this room is going somewhere and he is going through the
place they are going to be.*

---

The one who had said the sentence went second.

He had got about six feet and was turning, and he was turning to his left
because his right hand was inside his coat and across his body, which
meant his weight came onto his left foot and stayed there.

The boy went at him low.

That was the whole of what she got of him: a change of height, and a line
that went past the man's right side and under the arm that was busy, and
he did not slow down for any part of it.

What she got of the man was everything.

His left leg gave from the inside. It did not buckle at the knee — it went
sideways above it, the whole thing folding in toward the other one, and it
took his hip with it, and he came down on that side with his shoulder
first and his right hand still in his coat and the coat pulling up under
his arm as he fell.

He was not dead. He was on the floor of the hall making a noise and there
was a great deal coming out of the inside of his thigh in a way she had
never seen and had not known was possible from a leg, and it went on
coming, and he got his hand out of his coat at last and put it on it, and
it came through his fingers.

*Above the knee.*

*He has done the leg and gone. He has not finished him and he has not
stopped to.*

*He is not going to come back to him either. He does not need to. That man
is going to be dead in four minutes and there is no work left in him now
and the boy knew that when he did it.*

She looked at the man on the floor for a while, and then stopped, because
there was better work happening on the other side of the room.

---

The two by the east door came at him together, which was the only
sensible thing anybody in that hall did, and it did not work, and it did
not work for a reason she could see from where she was standing.

They did not arrive together.

The near one had four paces of clear floor. The far one had the table.

And the boy moved sideways — left, two steps, toward the wall — which put
the table's long edge between him and the far one and did nothing at all
about the near one, and she understood what that was for about half a
second before it happened.

*He fights one and then he fights one.*

The near one came in with a knife up, high, at about the height of his own
ear, which is where you hold a knife when you have decided the other
person is smaller than you.

The boy did not go back and he did not go sideways again. He went *in*,
under the arm, on the same side as the knife, close enough that the arm
came down across his shoulder instead of into him — and she saw the arm
come down and lie there for a moment like a thing put on a shelf.

His right hand went up under it, into the armpit, twice.

The man's knife hand opened, and the knife went on the boards, and he made
a sound that was mostly out of his nose.

And the far one arrived round the end of the table at exactly that point,
which was four paces too late, and the boy was already turned.

**And there is a light coming up.**

---

She had not noticed the light start.

She noticed it at about the point where the second of the two by the east
door went into the wall, because she could see his face, and the hall had
been dim when she came into it out of the yard and had been dim four
minutes ago and it was not dim now.

It was coming off the sword.

She could not have told anybody what she meant by that. The sword was a
short one and it was in his right hand and it had been in his right hand
since some point she had not seen, and it was making light, in the way
that a thing in a fire makes light, from inside itself and all along its
length, and the light was going up.

And with it there was a note.

*There is a sound.*

*There has been a sound for a while and I have been hearing it and calling
it something else.*

It was high and it was thin and it did not come from anywhere in
particular. It was in the room the way cold is in a room. And it had been
lower when he came through the door — she was certain of that afterwards
and could not prove it — and it had come up while she stood there, and it
was still coming up.

She stood on the boards with two dead men inside four feet of her, under a
note that was going up and a light that was getting worse to look at, and
what she thought, whole, was:

*That is not a sword.*

And then she thought the other thing, and it was not a question, and she
had no more curiosity in it than she had had about the step at the turn:

*I want to know what that is.*

---

Tokuji came in at the east door.

She saw the whole of that one and she saw it from beginning to end and it
took about two seconds and there was nothing to be done about any of it by
anybody.

He came in fast for a man with a hip. He came in three or four steps and
he was looking for her — that is the part, and she had it immediately and
has never once been able to put it down — he came into a room with that in
it and the first thing his head did was go to the middle of the floor
where she had been standing when they brought her up.

He did not look at the boy.

The boy came off the man at the east door and went through where Tokuji
was, and it was not a decision and there was nothing in it, and there was
no more in it than there had been in the wall or the table.

Tokuji went down about a yard inside the door.

---

She did not say anything.

She did not make a noise and she did not move and she did not stop
watching, and she stood there under a rising note with her hands at her
sides and put it where things went.

*That was Tokuji.*

*He asked me if I had eaten on the thirteenth of the tenth month.*

*He got a second sled on the twenty-fifth and he came down for two days in
the rain and he never said anything about the eleven men.*

*He said nobody knows and he kept it.*

*And he came in through that door for me.*

She stood with it.

*And it went on the same as the wall.*

She did not put it down anywhere, because there was nowhere to put it and
nothing to write with, and it stayed exactly where it was for eleven
years.

---

Enkai was last and he was the only one who did not try.

He had put the book down at some point and he was standing by the table
with his hands at his sides, about sixty, clerical, ink on the middle
finger of his right hand, and he watched the boy come across the floor at
him and did not move and did not speak.

She was eight feet away and she saw his face and there was nothing in it
she could read.

It took some time.

She watched all of it. She watched it from eight feet with the light off
the sword filling the whole of the hall by then, bright enough that she
could see the grain of the boards and the ink on his finger and every
single thing that happened to him, and the note above the building so high
by then that it had stopped being a sound and had become a thing pressing
on the inside of her ears.

And what she felt, standing there, was the thing she had wished on a road
in the ninth month and had not had a shape for.

**It was the thing she had wanted to do to Shimoda instead of running.**

She had never had a picture of it. She had had four hundred nights of a
feeling with no picture in it, and here it was, being done properly, eight
feet away, by somebody who was very good at it, and she stood and watched
it happen to a man who had put a number on her, and she was not sorry
about one second of it then and she was not sorry about it at nineteen.

---

And then it stopped.

Not gradually. There was nothing left standing in the hall, and the boy
was in the middle of the floor, and the note came down.

She heard it go. It came down through everything it had come up through,
the whole of it, in about four seconds, and it went down past where it had
been when he walked in and kept going and settled somewhere under the
room, and the light went out of the sword the way heat goes out of a
poker.

And the hall was dim again, the way it had been when she came in out of
the yard.

The boy stood in the middle of it with his arms down and did not move for
a long time.

She had no idea what she had just watched.
