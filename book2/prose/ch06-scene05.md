# Chapter 6, Scene 5 — The Top Field

*Act 1, month 5, the twenty-second. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV:
Uzuki. ~1,770 words.*

---

She had it down to four sentences and she had said them to the wall
eleven times, and she said them to Kuriya at the gate at about half past
six in the morning, standing up straight, without any of it going wrong.

"Autumn's four months. I want winter. I'll pay the four months in the
spring on top of the spring, so it's not less, it's later. My mother
doesn't know I'm here."

Kuriya listened to the whole of it. He was sixty-something and he had a
face that had spent a lot of time outdoors being disagreed with, and he
did not interrupt her once, and when she stopped he did not say anything
for long enough that she nearly said it all again.

"No," he said.

"Why."

"Because if I move it for you, I've moved it." He leaned on the gate.
"And then I've got a thing I do for widows. And I've got eleven of them."

*Eleven.*

*I hadn't got eleven.*

"And they all talk to each other and they all know within a fortnight,
and then I'm the man who moved it for the Nozakis and not for anybody
else, and I'd rather be the man who doesn't move it for anybody. It's a
worse thing to be and it's the same for everybody, and that's the only
thing I've got that's any good."

---

*Right.*

She stood there.

*Don't cry at him. If you cry at him he's got to be kind and then he's
got to get away from you, and then you never get to ask him anything
again as long as you live.*

*Ask him something. Quick. While you're still here.*

"What would make you say yes?"

He looked at her properly for the first time.

"Somebody in that house who earns," he said.

"I'm going to earn."

"I know you are," said Kuriya. "I've seen your mother's face and I've
seen yours and I know exactly which one of you is going to be up at
Iwasa's this morning."

"Then—"

"Somebody who earns," he said, "means somebody I can put on a paper, so
that when they don't pay I can go and stand in front of them. I can't
stand in front of you. There isn't a man in this village who'd stand in
front of you and there isn't a court in Hot Water that'd let me, and if I
put your name on a paper I'd be laughed off the road."

*Oh.*

*It isn't the money.*

*He doesn't care whether I can make it. He cares whether he can get it
off me if I don't.*

"So it doesn't matter how much I get," she said.

"It matters to you," said Kuriya, "and it doesn't matter to the paper."

---

Iwasa's top field was eleven minutes up and it faced the wrong way and
that was why it was still full of stones after forty years.

The stones came up every winter. That was the whole of it; the ground put
them up and you took them off and next winter it put more up, and it had
been doing that since before anybody, and it would go on doing it after,
and Iwasa put four or five people on it for nine days every year and paid
them by the day and never once got to the end of it.

There were six of them on it that morning. A woman called Fumi who was
about thirty and who did not speak to her at all; two men; Iwasa's
daughter-in-law; a boy of about twelve called Isaki, and her.

*Fumi's not being nasty. She doesn't know what to say.*

*That's what it is now. Everybody in this village has got a thing they
haven't said to me and they've all decided the safest thing is to say
nothing at all, and so I go about being not-spoken-to by about nine
people who are all being kind.*

*It's the exact opposite of nasty and it's worse.*

---

The work was this.

You walked the row bent over and you picked up anything bigger than your
fist and you put it in the basket, and when the basket was heavy you
carried it to the headland and tipped it on the wall, and then you walked
back and started again where you had left off, which you had marked with
your foot in the dirt and which you always got wrong by about a yard.

That was the whole job. It was not difficult and there was no skill in
it and she had thought, for about the first forty minutes, that this was
going to be fine.

By nine her hands were the least of it.

It was the small of her back. Nobody had told her it would be the small
of her back. The bending was fine, the bending was nothing, it was the
*coming back up* between the bending, forty times to the basket and then
the basket to the wall and then down again, and by the middle of the
morning there was a place about two inches wide across the bottom of her
spine that had stopped being part of her and had become a thing she was
carrying about.

She did not stop. She had a very clear rule about not stopping, which
she had made at about eight o'clock and which had got worse as it went
on: *if you stop before the man says stop, then you are a girl who was
brought out to a field, and if you don't, you are somebody who is
working.*

---

At the middle of the day Iwasa's daughter-in-law brought water out and
they sat on the wall, and Isaki, who had not said a single word to her
all morning, said:

"You're doing it wrong."

"I'm not."

"You are. You're going twice."

She was too tired to be angry and it came out as nothing at all, which
was probably what saved it.

"Going twice how."

"You pick it up and you put it in the basket and then you take the basket
to the wall." He was chewing something. "That's picking it up and putting
it down and picking it up again. You throw the little ones. Straight at
the wall, off the ground, as you come. Then the basket's only got the big
ones in it and you go to the wall about a third as much."

"You can't throw them that far."

"You can't. I can from the top half. From the bottom half you can't and
you carry them." He shrugged. "Everyone gets told. Nobody told you
because you're new and they think you know."

---

She tried it after the water.

It was hard for about eleven minutes and then it stopped being hard, and
then it was the only way she wanted to do it, and by the middle of the
afternoon she had it so that she was going along the row throwing with
her right and dropping the big ones in with her left without breaking
step, and the basket was going to the wall about every fourth time it had
been going before.

And her back stopped getting worse.

*It's the eighth count.*

She stood up in the middle of the row and laughed at nothing, which
nobody saw, because they were all bent over.

*It's exactly the eighth count. It's the same thing. It's not where you
put the effort in, it's where you take it OUT.*

*He'd have loved this. He'd have made a whole evening out of this.*

She got to the end of the row.

*And it doesn't matter, does it.*

*Because it's a day rate.*

---

Iwasa paid them on the headland at about six.

A fortieth. He put it in her hand and said "Same tomorrow if you want
it," and he said the same thing to Isaki, and Isaki had done about twice
what she had done, and Isaki got a thirtieth, because Iwasa knew his
face.

*I did nearly what he did after the water.*

*Doesn't matter. Nobody was watching the middle of the row. Iwasa was in
the bottom field the whole afternoon and what he's paying is what a girl
he doesn't know is worth, and what a girl he doesn't know is worth is a
fortieth, and it will be a fortieth tomorrow and the day after.*

*I got better at it in one afternoon and it was worth nothing.*

She turned that over on the way down and it kept coming out the same and
she made herself look at it, because she had decided some time in the
last three weeks that she was going to be somebody who looked at things.

*Right. So it was worth nothing.*

*I'm still doing it the new way tomorrow.*

*Because my back's better, and because I'll get to the end of the row,
and because I'd rather be good at it than not, and if that's stupid then
it's stupid and I'm doing it anyway.*

---

She did the ninety that night on the corner of the table with her mother
asleep on the other side of the wall.

Twenty-eight good. Down from thirty-one, and she knew why, and she wrote
the twenty-eight and did not write why, because the number was the number
and putting an excuse next to a number is how you end up with a column of
excuses.

Then she turned the flour bag over and put the first thing in the second
column.

*22nd. 1/40.*

She looked at it.

It was, without any question at all, the smallest thing she had ever
written down.

*Forty of those is one.*

*And one hundred and forty is what we're short in a month.*

*And there's nine days of it in the year and then the field's done and
there isn't any more until the thinning.*

She sat with the pen.

*Say it properly. Don't be soft about it and don't be dramatic about it.
Say the actual thing.*

*The actual thing is that I could work every single day that exists
between now and the autumn, and be good at all of them, and it would come
to about four and a half.*

*And the rent's three a month.*

She put the pen down and put the flour bag under the stone by the step,
and stood in the yard for a minute in the dark, which was bigger than it
had been on the fourteenth.

*Fine.*

*Four and a half is four and a half.*
