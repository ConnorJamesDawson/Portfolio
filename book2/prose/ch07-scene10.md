# Chapter 7, Scene 10 — I Heard The Chair

*Act 1, month 7, the twenty-first. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV:
Uzuki. ~1,860 words.*

---

Her mother was doing the fire and she was fine.

That was the first thing and it did most of it. Uzuki came out of her
room at about half past five with the whole of the night still in her and
found her mother crouched at the range with the door open and the kindling
going, doing exactly what she did every morning at half past five, in the
grey, with her sleeves down.

The chair was standing up.

It was standing where it always stood, at the end of the table under the
window, four square on the flags, and Uzuki looked at it for slightly too
long and then made herself stop.

There was nothing else. The pot was on the shelf. The floor had been done.
There was not one single object in that kitchen that was in a different
place from where it had been at eight o'clock the night before.

*There's nothing to see.*

*There's nothing anywhere in this room and I have looked at all of it.*

---

She said it before she had got two steps into the kitchen, because she had
promised herself at about one in the morning that she would say it before
she had time to build a reason not to, and she was going to keep one
promise even if it was that one.

"I heard the chair."

Her mother did not stop what she was doing.

That was the first thing. Uzuki noticed it and filed it and did not know
yet what she was filing.

"That was me," said Sae, to the fire. "I caught it with the bucket coming
past."

---

Uzuki stood in the middle of the kitchen floor.

*No you didn't.*

She had it in under a second and she had it completely and she could not
have explained one part of it to anybody who was not her.

*You gave me a reason.*

*You never do that. You give reasons for the true things. You gave me a
reason for the road and you gave me a reason for the hundred and you gave
me a reason for not doing it for me, and every single time you have said
a hard thing to me in my life you have said the hard thing and then told
me why you were saying it, and it is the whole of how you talk.*

*Nobody asked you how the chair went over.*

*I said I heard the chair. That is not a question. And you answered a
question that had not been asked and you put a bucket in it.*

*And you said it to the fire.*

She stood there.

*And you have never once in your life told me a thing that wasn't true.
Not about money and not about Dad and not about the road. You told me you
were never going to come and you told me you knew it while you were
saying it, and it was the worst thing anybody's ever said to me and you
said it because it was true.*

*You are the one thing in this house I can read.*

---

"Mum."

"Mm."

"Did he—"

"Uzuki."

It was her name and nothing else. It was the way her mother had said her
name four hundred times over ten years, at the end of a thing, when the
thing was finished: at the shrine, in the lane, in the kitchen at Kuriya's
rent, in the doorway on the fourth of the fourth month.

*That's the closing one.*

*That is the one she uses when a subject is over.*

*And it has never once been used on a lie.*

Her mother put another piece in and got up off her knees, and turned
round, and looked at her — properly, straight, the way she always did,
and there was nothing wrong with her face at all — and said:

"There's bread."

---

Uzuki looked at her.

She looked at the whole of her, in the grey light, with the fire going
behind her, and she did it thoroughly and without any shame about doing
it, because she had spent the night on a mat with her hands on a wall and
she was not going to be delicate about it now.

*Face, nothing.*

*Neck, nothing.*

*Hands, nothing — and she's had them in the kindling, so I'd see.*

*Sleeves are down.*

*Sleeves are always down. Her sleeves have been down every morning since
the fourth month because it's cold in this kitchen at half five and she
has never once rolled them up before the fire's going.*

*So the sleeves are not information.*

*I want them to be information and they are not.*

And underneath that, arriving with everything it brought:

*I could find out.*

She stood in the middle of a kitchen floor at half past five in the
morning and understood exactly what she had in her hands.

*Hand on. What's different. Where does it hurt. And do those two agree.*

*I could put my hands on her and I would know in about forty seconds. Not
guess. Know. It's the one thing in the world I can actually do and it is
standing four feet away from me with a bit of kindling in it.*

---

So she went and did it.

She crossed the kitchen and reached out and took her mother's hands the
way she had taken them on the twenty-seventh, off her lap, at the table,
in the old house, when neither of them had said a word about it.

Her mother moved them.

It was not violent and it was not fast. She turned, in the same motion,
and put the kindling down on the shelf, and used the turn to take her
hands out of it, and it was so ordinary that a person standing in the
doorway would have seen a woman putting down a bit of wood.

Uzuki stood with her own hands out in front of her.

*She's never done that.*

*She has never once in my life moved her hands away from me.*

She put them down.

*Right.*

*Then I know.*

---

She sat outside on the step for a while afterwards, in the cold, before
anybody else was up, and she did the rest of it in order, because doing
it in order was the only thing she had that worked.

*One. There is nobody.*

*There were nine people in that yard on the twenty-ninth and every single
one of them watched him be decent to me about my father's grave, and they
were right, and they all remember it. If I go to Ine and say what I heard
through a wall, Ine will be kind to me and then Ine will be uncomfortable
and then Ine will tell my mother, because Ine is fond of my mother, and
that is exactly what a kind person does.*

*Two. The hundred is spent.*

*It went in the left column on the twenty-seventh of the sixth month and
I watched her do it. It is not a hundred any more. It is a house and a
range and meat twice in a fortnight and a room with a door on it, and
there is nothing to give back.*

*Three. The rent.*

*The rent is his. Kuriya has eleven widows and he does not move things
for anybody and he told me so at his own gate, and if we went back down
the lane the house is let by now anyway, because Kuriya does not leave a
house empty for three weeks.*

She got to the fourth one and sat with it for a long time, and the sun
came up on the far side of Iwasa's while she was doing it.

*Four.*

*She told me she wasn't doing it for me.*

*She told me on the twenty-seventh, at the table, with the box out, and
she said it in that flat voice and she said exactly why she was saying
it: because if she said it was for me then I'd owe her, and I'd carry it,
and in about four years I'd work out what she'd charged me for it.*

*So there's no debt.*

*She made sure of that. She sat down and made sure of it on purpose,
because she thought it was the kindest thing she had left, and it
probably was.*

*And it means that this morning I have got nothing to spend.*

*I cannot go in there and say you owe me. She took that off the table
before I was ten and she told me she was doing it and I said all right.*

She put her chin on her knees.

*And the other one I could have used, she used up before six o'clock this
morning on a bucket.*

---

She got the flour bag out that night with the door shut.

The first column was the ninety and she wrote twenty-nine in it, because
she had done twenty-nine, and it had been a bad one and she wrote it
anyway.

The second column was the money and there was nothing to put in it,
because Kuriya paid at month end and it was the twenty-first.

The third column said 1,679.

She looked at it for a while.

*Twenty-fourth of the sixth month I wrote seventeen hundred and four and
I took one off and it was — *

*It was a plan. That's what it was. It was a number I made up in the dark
in a kitchen because I was ten and I had nothing and I wanted there to be
a day at the end of it.*

She took one off.

1,678.

It went down onto the flour bag in the same hand as everything else and
it was not the same object at all, and she sat and looked at it and could
not have told anybody what had changed about it.

---

Then she turned the bag sideways and ruled a fourth column with the edge
of her thumbnail, the way her father had ruled a chit when there was no
rule in the house.

She sat with the pen over the top of it.

*Write what it is.*

*Everything on this bag has got a thing at the top of it. The ninety's
got a thing. The money's got a thing. I put the date at the top of the
second one on the fourteenth of the fifth month because Dad put the date
on everything.*

*So write what it is.*

She held the pen there for about four seconds.

Then she moved it down, past where a heading would have gone, and left
that part empty, and put one mark in the column and nothing else.

She looked at it.

Then she put the bag behind the brick, and did it from the door — down on
one knee, two fingers, in, brick back — and it took about two and a half,
and she lay down on the mat in her own room with the door shut, in the
house with the roof that did nothing when it rained.
