# Chapter 4, Scene 6 — Three and a Half Hours

*Act 1, month 4, the third, two till dark. Anzai's back room, Kayano.
Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~2,080 words.*

---

"First thing," said Anzai. "I'm not teaching you a jutsu."

"But — "

"Not during."

"Sorry."

"There is a jutsu. It has a name and it takes about six years and you'd
need somebody standing over you for four of them, and you are not having
it, and if anybody ever tells you that they got it in an afternoon they
are lying to you or selling you something." He put a stool in the middle
of the floor. "What you're getting is what's underneath it. It's not a
technique. It's a *habit*, and it's the habit that everything else is
built on top of, and about one person in nine can do it at all."

"Which one in nine?"

"Not during," said Anzai. "Sit."

---

He did not start with her hands.

He started with her sitting on a stool for eleven minutes doing nothing
at all, which she had not expected and did not enjoy, and which turned
out afterwards to be the whole of it.

"Find it."

"Find what?"

"You know what. Everybody knows what. Find it and hold it and don't do
anything with it."

She found it.

It was where it always was, which was nowhere she could have pointed at,
somewhere behind and below, and she got hold of it the way she got hold
of it before the eighth count and held it there.

"Right," said Anzai. "Now don't spend it."

"I'm not."

"You're spending it. You've been spending it since I said find it. That's
what everybody does — they get hold of it and the getting hold of it is
already a push." He pulled a stool over and sat down in front of her.
"Hold it and let it sit. Don't push it anywhere. If it goes anywhere,
start again."

She started again eleven times.

---

At about the tenth minute she got a stretch of about four breaths where
it sat.

She knew she had got it because it was *easy*, suddenly and completely,
and it had gone from being a thing she was doing to a thing that was
happening, and she went, without meaning to, straight back to a flat bit
of ground behind a house with a barrow on it.

*That's the eighth.*

*That's exactly the eighth. That's the bit where you stop paying and
it goes anyway.*

Anzai said, "Hah."

She opened her eyes.

He was leaning back on the stool with his arms folded, looking at her the
way Kenji looked at a bead, which she would not have known, and which was
the way a person looks at a thing they were not expecting to have to
look at properly.

"Who taught you to hold back?"

"What?"

"Somebody's taught you to hold back. You're not doing it well and you're
doing it, and that's — " He made a small dismissive motion. "That's four
months I'm not going to have to spend. Who was it?"

"My father."

"He a medic?"

"He's a carrier. He does parcels. He taught me sword forms."

"Mm," said Anzai, and unfolded his arms, and that was the whole of it,
and it went past both of them like a cart on a road.

---

"Second thing. Hands."

He put his own hand out flat.

"You are not going to put anything into anybody for about two hours. What
you are going to do first is *take something out*, which is a stupid way
of saying it and it's the way everybody says it, so learn it. Hand on.
Nothing going anywhere. And you tell me what's different."

"Different from what?"

"From the other side."

---

Chiyo came and sat on a chair and put both forearms on the table with her
sleeves up, with the air of somebody who had done this before and had
opinions about it.

"Both hands. One on each. Don't press."

Uzuki put her hands on.

"Now shut your eyes and tell me the differences and don't be clever. Say
what's actually there."

"The left one's warmer."

"Good. That's the fire. What else?"

"The right one's — " She sat with it. "The right one's harder. Not the
bone. The bit on top, along here. It's like a rope in it."

"That's the one," said Anzai. "That's a cord and it's been there six days
and she's had a bag in that hand up a hill four times and she's not going
to tell you about it because she's sixteen. Now, both of you. Chiyo,
where does it hurt."

"It doesn't hurt."

"Chiyo."

"Under the shoulder," said Chiyo, "and it goes down to the elbow if I
carry anything and it's fine."

---

"There you are," said Anzai. "That's the entire diagnostic art and it
took ninety seconds and it's about three quarters of what a medic
actually does. *Hand on. What's different. Where does it hurt. And do
those two agree.*

"Because they mostly don't. Where it hurts and where the trouble is are
in different places about half the time, and if you go and put your hand
where it hurts you'll spend twenty minutes on an elbow that hasn't got
anything wrong with it and the patient will feel better for an hour
because you were kind at them, and you'll think you did something."

"How do you know which — "

"Not during."

---

The next hour was the worst hour of her life to that point and she would
not have swapped it for anything.

It went like this: hand on Chiyo's forearm, on the cord. Find it. Hold
it. Don't spend it. And then — the thing itself — **let about a
thimbleful go out of the hand and stop.**

The first time she gave Chiyo about a bucket.

Chiyo said a word that Anzai did not react to at all and pulled her arm
back.

"Sorry — "

"Again," said Anzai.

The second time was worse in a different way, which was that she was so
careful that nothing left her hand at all, and Chiyo said, "There's
nothing," and Anzai said, "No, there isn't. Again."

The eleventh time something went.

She felt it leave, which she had not expected — it was not like the
eighth count, where you found out afterwards; it was a small definite
going, like a coin off a palm — and Chiyo said, "Oh."

"There," said Anzai. "Now stop."

"I have stopped."

"You have not stopped, you've *trailed off*. Watch." He put two fingers
on the back of her hand. "You went, and then you kept going for about the
length of a breath at about a quarter, because you didn't decide to stop,
you ran out of intending. That's not stopping. That's finishing.

"Stopping is a thing you do. It's a separate action and it takes as much
as the going does, and if you don't learn it now you'll spend your whole
life leaking into people."

---

*Late. Not at the set, at the going.*

*And stop is a thing you do.*

She did it two hundred times.

She did not count them; Anzai counted them, out loud, in tens, in a
completely flat voice, for an hour and a bit, and somewhere around a
hundred and forty she stopped being able to feel her own hands and he
said, "Good, now you'll find out what it is without the hands telling
you," and did not let her stop.

At about a hundred and eighty she got four in a row.

At a hundred and ninety-one she was crying, which she noticed at about a
hundred and ninety-four, and Anzai did not say anything about it and did
not stop counting, and she got to two hundred.

"Right," he said. "Drink that. Sit still. Don't talk for four minutes."

---

"Third thing," said Anzai, "and it's the one you'll get wrong for the
next year.

"You saw me do twenty-five minutes on a boy's leg. You thought that was
twenty-five minutes of *doing*. It isn't. It's about ninety separate
goes, each of them about a thimble, with a stop on the end of every one,
and the gaps in between are where the work is.

"If you go in and hold it on somebody for twenty-five minutes you will
cook them, and you'll be on the floor in four, and you'll have done
nothing at all except frighten yourself.

"Little. Stop. Wait. Little. Stop. Wait. Ninety times. And it's *dull*,
and it's dull for you and it's dull for them, and by the sixtieth one
you'll be sure it isn't working and you'll want to push, and if you push
you undo the whole hour."

"How do I know if it's working?"

"You don't," said Anzai. "Not while you're doing it. Afterwards. Forty
seconds afterwards, when your hands come off."

---

At about half past four he let her do a whole one.

"Chiyo. Sit down. Uzuki. Under the shoulder, where she said, and check
the cord first and don't take my word for where it is."

She put her hand on and found it.

It had moved. That was the first thing and it stopped her — it was not
where it had been at half past two; it was about an inch and a half
further up and it was thicker, and she said so, and Anzai said, "Yes.
Because you've been on it for two hours with a bucket and a thimble. Go
on."

"I've made it worse."

"You've made it different. Go on."

So she went on.

---

Little. Stop. Wait.

She got to about thirty and her arms started shaking and Anzai said,
"Elbows on the table," without looking up from whatever he was doing, and
she put her elbows on the table and it was better.

At about forty Chiyo's shoulder dropped.

Not much. About half an inch, on that side, and it happened between one
go and the next, and Uzuki nearly said something and did not because he
had said not during.

Little. Stop. Wait.

At about sixty she was certain it was not working, exactly as she had
been told she would be, and she recognised the certainty as the thing he
had described, which did not make it any less certain, and she kept going
because it was a certainty with a label on it.

At seventy-one Anzai said, "That'll do."

She took her hands off.

---

Chiyo sat at the table with her arms in front of her and rolled the right
shoulder, once, and then again.

Then she said, "Huh."

"Well?" said Anzai.

"It's — " Chiyo rolled it a third time. "It's not gone."

"It's not going to have gone."

"No, I know. It's — " She lifted the arm out sideways and put it down
again. "That's the first time I've done that since Tuesday without the
elbow going."

Nobody said anything for a second.

"Right," said Anzai, and got up, and went to the window, and stood there
with his back to the room for a while looking out at nothing, the way he
had at two o'clock.

---

Uzuki sat at the table with her hands in her lap.

She could not feel them properly. There was something going on in both
forearms that was going to be extremely unpleasant tomorrow, and she was
so tired that the room had gone slightly wide at the edges, and she had
not eaten since one o'clock and did not want to.

*It worked.*

*I put my hands on a person and I did a thing and the thing happened and
she felt it and she said so and I did not imagine it.*

*Seventy-one goes. Two hours ago I gave her a bucket and she said a word.*

She looked at her own hands lying in her lap.

*These are — *

She could not finish it. She was too tired and there was not a word in
her for it, and what came out instead, sideways, unwanted, in the exact
voice it had been said in on a flat bit of ground behind a house:

*That's what it's meant to feel like?*

*That's nothing. That's less than the other way. I did LESS —*

*Yes.*

*Well why didn't you SAY —*

*I said it four hundred times.*

She put her head down on her forearms on Anzai's table for a moment,
which she had not asked permission to do.

Nobody made her get up.
