# Chapter 5, Scene 7 — Thirty-One Good

*Act 1, month 5, the eighth. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki.
~1,230 words.*

---

The box came out on the eleventh of the month and again on the
twenty-fourth and again on the second, which was three times in six
weeks, and the third time Uzuki sat down opposite her mother and did not
go away.

"I want to see it."

"There's nothing to see."

"Then it won't take long," said Uzuki.

Her mother looked at her for a moment and then turned the paper round.

---

She could read it.

That was the whole of the scene and it took her about ninety seconds and
she was never able to explain to anybody why it had been the worst part
of that year.

Six weeks ago she had stood in that kitchen with a piece of bread in her
hand and watched her mother's hand move coins from one side of a table to
the other, and had known that things had been added up and had not known
which side was the good side.

Now she looked at a sheet of paper and read it like a page.

*She's got it in two columns and the left one is money in and the right
one is money out, and the left one has three things in it and the right
one has eleven.*

*Left: the mill, four thousand ryō, that's the two big parcels from the second
month that he settled at month end. Iwasa, one and a half. And she's
written "H" and a hundred and drawn a line under it, and the line under
it means she's not counting it.*

*Right: the rent to Kuriya, which is the same every month and is the
biggest thing on the page by four times. Meal. Oil. The mule. Tazu.*

*Tazu's on there twice.*

She looked at that for a while.

"You've paid Tazu twice."

"He came eleven times," said her mother.

---

"Where's the carrying?"

"There isn't any carrying."

"There's the Ōgi round. There's the mill, and the cooper, and the
crossroads letters, and Fusano Friday — "

"Iwasa's boy has all of it," said her mother, "and he has had all of it
since the second month, and it is not coming back."

"He's fourteen and he drops things."

"He's fifteen now and he's got a barrow and a father." Her mother took the
paper back and squared it up. "Nobody in this village is going to put a
parcel into the hand of a widow and a girl of ten, Uzuki, and it is not
because they are unkind. It is because a parcel has to arrive."

---

Uzuki sat at the table.

*Four and a half in. Eleven things out. And the rent is the biggest thing
on the page.*

*And the hundred with a line under it, which she isn't counting, which
means she's decided it's not money, which means she's decided it's
something else.*

"How long," she said.

"What?"

"How long can we stay."

Her mother did not pretend not to understand it, which Uzuki was grateful
for and had not expected.

"Kuriya's said nothing," she said. "He'll say nothing till the autumn
because he was fond of your father and because it looks bad. And then
he'll say something, and he'll be perfectly nice about it, and that'll be
that."

"So the autumn."

"The autumn," said her mother.

---

"And then what?"

"And then I'll have thought of something."

"Have you thought of something?"

"Uzuki."

"Have you *thought* of something?"

Her mother put the box lid on.

"Shimoda came round on Tuesday," she said.

"Who?"

"Shimoda. He's got the two fields the other side of Iwasa's and the
brick shed. He came about the mule."

"What about the mule?"

"He'd buy it."

"We're not selling the mule," said Uzuki. "The mule's — how do we do
anything without the — "

"We don't do anything with it now," said her mother, and got up, and put
the box on the shelf above the stove, at the back, where things went.

---

Uzuki went out and sat on the wall.

She did the ninety at about four every afternoon and had done since the
seventh, which was thirty-two days, and she had it on the back of the
mill's chit and then on the back of the chit and then on a piece of a
flour bag when the chit ran out, in a column, in her own bad hand:

*90. Own left arm. 20 good.*

*90. Own left arm. 22 good.*

*90. 19 good — arms bad, did it after the yard.*

*90. 24.*

*90. 24.*

*90. 26.*

Thirty-two lines.

She got her sleeve up and put her right hand on her own left forearm and
found it and held it and did not spend it.

*Little.*

*Stop.*

*Wait.*

---

She got to about forty and stopped, which she had never done.

*There's nobody.*

It arrived like that, flatly, in the middle of the forty-first, and it
was not new — she had had it about nine times since the seventh and had
put it down each time and got on — and this time she put her hand in her
lap and looked at the yard.

*I learned this for one person.*

*I walked eleven days and sat in a nettle bank and cried on a man's table
and got given three and a half hours he hadn't got, for one person, and I
have been doing ninety a day for thirty-two days on my own arm.*

*My own arm hasn't got anything wrong with it.*

*Ma's got a back and she won't let me near it. Ine's four days away. The
mule's a mule.*

*There is nobody in this village who is going to let a girl of ten put
her hands on them, and the one person who was ever going to is under the
ground behind the shrine, and I am sitting on a wall in the cold doing
ninety repetitions of a thing that takes the edge off a chest, at nobody,
for the thirty-second day.*

She sat there for a bit.

Then she put her hand back on her forearm and did the forty-first.

---

*Because I'll be fifteen.*

*That's it. That's the whole of it and it's not much.*

*I'll be fifteen and then I'll be twenty, and there'll be somebody, and
if I stop now then when there is somebody I'll have a fifth of it and a
bad month behind me, and if I don't stop then I'll have the whole of it.*

*He said properly good by the autumn.*

*He said ninety a day and don't write the feelings, write the number,
because in four months you'll be certain you've got worse and you won't
have.*

*And it's been thirty-two days and I've gone from twenty to twenty-six
and I know that because it's written down.*

*So: the number.*

*Not what it's for. There isn't anything it's for.*

*The number.*

She did the rest of them.

It took her about an hour and a quarter because she was slow that day and
because her hands were cold, and at the end of it she sat on the wall in
the near dark and wrote on the piece of flour bag, in the column, in her
own bad hand:

*90. Own left arm. 31 good.*

Then she went in and did the fire.
