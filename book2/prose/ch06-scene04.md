# Chapter 6, Scene 4 — What A Boat Needs

*Act 1, month 5, the sixteenth. The north quay, Nezu. Tadayoshi Ruri,
age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~2,180 words.*

---

It took him three days to work out where to stand, which was two days
longer than it should have taken and he knew it while it was happening.

He spent the thirteenth on the top road doing the quay with his eyes and
getting nothing, because a quay from four hundred yards up a hill is a
picture and pictures do not have books in them. He spent the fourteenth
going along the front pretending to be somebody's errand and got as far
as knowing where the crane was and which of the four sheds was locked.

*Which is the same mistake. It is the boat again. I am looking for the
boat and the boat is not there and has not been there since the third of
last month, and I am going to go on looking for it because it is the
thing I want.*

*Stop looking for the thing you want.*

*The dues book is for dues, so she is not in it. The harbour office is
for the coast and I am not going in one of those again.*

*So: what is a boat.*

He sat on the wall on the evening of the fourteenth and did it properly,
in a list, the way Meno would have made him do it.

*A boat is a hull and six men and it cannot make anything.*

*It cannot make water. It cannot make food. It cannot make rope or tar
or charcoal, and it cannot make a man who knows the bar.*

*So every single one of those comes off this quay, out of a shed, off a
man, and every one of those men has a book, because a man who gives
things away without a book stops having things.*

*Six books, then. And I want the one that everybody has to use.*

**Everybody drinks.**

---

The victualler's yard was the third one up from the crane and it had a
cooperage at the back of it and about ninety casks in it and a man of
about sixty called Kihara who was doing nothing at all.

That was the whole of the intelligence and it was enough.

Tadayoshi went in on the morning of the sixteenth and asked for a day's
work.

He had thought about the sentence for a long time and had got it down to
nine words, and the nine words did what he had built them to do, which
was to fail.

"I'm not taking anybody on," said Kihara.

"All right."

He turned to go, slowly, and got about four feet.

"I'm not taking anybody on," said Kihara, "because I've not been paid
since the third of last month."

---

*There.*

*Do not turn round fast.*

He turned round at the speed of a boy who is disappointed and has
nowhere else to be.

**A man who is owed money will explain the whole of a transaction to
anybody who stands still.**

He had not known that until that morning. He had it inside about forty
seconds and he kept it for the rest of his life and he used it more often
than anything else he ever learned.

*Four questions.*

*I get four. Four is a boy being nosy. Six is a boy who came in here for
this.*

---

"Somebody's not paid you?"

"Somebody's not paid me," said Kihara, "since the third of last month,"
and he sat down on a cask, which was the thing Tadayoshi had been hoping
for and had not expected to get in under a minute.

*One.*

"Was it a lot?"

"Was it a lot." Kihara looked at him. "Eleven days of water for nine.
Four sacks of rice, a barrel of the salt pork that I'd been keeping,
because he wanted the good one, and two of oil. And they'd have had my
charcoal and all if they'd wanted a fire on that thing, and they didn't,
which I've thought about since."

*Do not ask about the fire.*

*Do not ask about the fire. It is the best thing he has said and it is
not what I came in for.*

"Eleven days," said Tadayoshi.

"Eleven days of water for nine men."

*Two.*

---

He stood in the yard with his hands where they always were and did the
first pass while the man went on.

*Nine.*

*Water for nine and rice for nine and they came back on — he has not
said when they came back.*

*Nine.*

Kihara said the crew of that boat was six. He said it in the middle of
something else, complaining about the master, who had been short with
him and had not looked at the docket and had said the office would settle
it, and Tadayoshi took the six out of the middle of the complaint and put
it next to the nine and stood very still.

*Six men sail it.*

*Nine were going to eat.*

*Three more people were expected on that boat than there are men who
work it.*

He did not do anything with that. He put it down whole, the way you put
down a thing you cannot lift yet.

*And I do not know if she is one of the three.*

*She might be one of the three. Or she might be a thing in the hold that
does not get counted, because you do not victual for cargo, and if she is
a thing in the hold then the three are three men with keys and the number
is nothing.*

*And if she IS one of the three —*

He made himself finish it.

*— then two other people were taken that week, and I am never going to
know their names.*

---

*Three.*

"When did they come back?"

"Fifteenth," said Kihara. "Of last month. Twelve days."

*Twelve.*

*He victualled it for eleven and it was out twelve.*

*So either the master got it wrong by a day, which men do, or they put in
somewhere and took water.*

*And if they put in somewhere and took water —*

He got there while Kihara was still talking about the master.

**Then there is another one of him.**

*Somewhere down that coast there is a yard with casks in it and a man of
sixty sitting on one of them who has not been paid since the third of
last month either, and he has got her in his book, in stores, and he does
not know it, and he will tell anybody who stands still.*

*I cannot find her.*

*I can find the people who were paid for her, and there is one of them at
every single place that boat stopped.*

He stood in a cooper's yard at Nezu on the sixteenth of the fifth month
and understood, for the first time, what he was actually going to be
doing for the next three years, and it did not feel like anything at all.

---

*Four.*

"What did they give you instead of money?"

Kihara got up off the cask.

"Come here," he said. "Come and look at this, because I've been trying
to get somebody to look at this for six weeks."

---

There was a spike on the wall of the counting house with about forty
things on it and the warrant was not on the spike; it was in a tin under
the spike, on its own, which told Tadayoshi more about the last six
weeks of Kihara's life than anything the man said.

He put it flat on the bench.

"Read that."

"I can read."

"I know you can read, everybody can read, read the *date*."

Tadayoshi read the date. Then he read the whole of it, twice, in about
forty seconds, in the order he read things, which was the top left and
then the bottom right and then the middle.

**STORES — SECOND OFFICE**

Under it a number, seven figures, with the first two set apart. Under
that a line for a sum and the sum written in a clerk's hand and not the
master's. Down the right-hand edge, sideways, a strip of smaller print
about what happened if you presented it late.

And in the bottom corner, over the fold, a second stamp, smaller than the
first, that was not a place and was not a word he knew: a mark, six
strokes, inside a square with the corners cut.

*I do not know that.*

*I have never seen that.*

"Four months, that takes," said Kihara. "Four months and then a man comes
round and gives you it in paper you take somewhere else. I've had that
stamp twice in nine years."

*Do not.*

*That is five.*

He asked it anyway, and he knew he was doing it while his mouth was
moving, and afterwards he wrote down that he had done it, because a rule
you break without recording it is a rule you have stopped having.

"When was the other one?"

"Three year back," said Kihara. "Same time of year, near enough. Same
stamp, same four months, same fellow at the end of it with the paper.
Different boat."

---

*Three years ago. On this coast. Same office.*

*So it is not a thing that happened to my family. It is a thing that
happens.*

He looked down at the warrant on the bench and did the only thing he
could do with it, which was to take it all.

He read the number four times until it was in. He read the strip down the
edge twice, the whole of it, because he did not know which part of it was
going to matter and would not know for years. He looked at the six
strokes inside the cut square for a long time, and closed his eyes, and
put them out again on the inside of his eyelids, and opened them and
checked, and got one of the strokes wrong the first time and did it
again.

*Do not copy it here.*

*Do not take out a book in a man's counting house. Take it out on the
wall by the crane in twenty minutes and put it down before it goes.*

"Thank you," he said.

"For what?"

"For letting me look at it."

Kihara laughed, once, without any pleasure in it, and put it back in the
tin.

"You're the first," he said.

---

He got up on the wall by the crane and had it down inside nine minutes,
all of it, the stamp and the number and the six strokes in the cut
square, and he did the strokes three times on the corner of the page
until the third one matched the thing in his head.

Then he sat with the ledger on his knees and did the arithmetic, and
watched it come apart in his hands.

*Eleven days of water is not a distance. It is a circuit.*

*Out on the third, back on the fifteenth, and Shioiri is somewhere in the
middle of it.*

*How far is Shioiri.*

He had that, and it took him a moment to find where he had got it, and
then he had that too: Kanaya's boats, and his father saying it at a
table, that the run down the coast and back was nine days with the
loading and about six without.

*Six, Sunaba to Shioiri and back. So three down.*

*And Nezu is a day north of Sunaba by sea, near enough, because Kihara's
day book has the Sunaba boats in it twice a week.*

*Four down, then. Four back up at the end.*

*That leaves four days in the middle for everything else, and half of
four is two, and two days' sail from an inlet that goes dry at low
water, in a direction I do not have.*

*Two days is a very long way.*

*And that is what I have got. She is somewhere inside two days' sail of a
place I already know, and two days of sail is a half a circle a hundred
miles across, and I have taken twelve days to get forty-one miles up a
road on my own feet.*

*And I have made all of it out of one number that a man of sixty gave me
because he was cross about a bill, and every step of it could be wrong,
and the first step is the worst — because if that boat put in somewhere
and took water, then the eleven days is not the voyage at all and the
whole of this comes down.*

He wrote it anyway, with the arithmetic shown, and put a stroke under the
line that said *three down* and wrote *unchecked* beside it.

**A number you can check is worth having wrong.**

He looked at it for a while.

Then he did the other thing, the thing he had not let himself do in the
yard, and put his hand flat on the outside of his coat over the ledger
and the grease tin and the drop.

*Nine.*

*Six sail it.*

*I want to know who the other two were.*
