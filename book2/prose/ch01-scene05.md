# Chapter 1, Scene 5 — The Eighth

*Act 1, month 2. Nakadō, behind the house. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV:
Uzuki. ~1,470 words.*

---

The third form had fourteen counts in it and she could do thirteen.

She had been able to do thirteen since the autumn. Thirteen was not the
problem. The problem was the eighth, which was a step, and it was not
even a difficult step to look at — you came off the back foot and you
went across, about a body's width, and you arrived with the sword
already where it had been going. Her father could do it without
appearing to move at all. He would be standing in one place and then he
would be standing a yard to the left of it with the wood at her collar
and an expression of great patience on his face.

"Again," he said.

"I've done it eleven times."

"You've done thirteen counts eleven times. You've done fourteen counts
none times."

She went back to the mark.

---

They did it behind the house on the flat bit where the barrow lived,
because the yard at the front was where the mule was and the mule had
opinions. It was cold and the ground had gone hard and there was a
week's worth of washing on the line, and every so often the wind moved
one of her mother's sheets and Uzuki lost the count, and it was
absolutely no good saying so.

She had a bokken. He had a bokken. There was a real one in the house,
above the door in the back room, and it had come from her grandfather
and it was not for her.

"One."

She went.

Counts one to seven were easy and had been easy for a year: they were
mostly footwork and one turn of the hips, and the sword did nothing you
could not do with a stick. The whole third form was, her father said,
about the eighth, and everything before it was the eighth getting ready
to happen.

"Seven."

She set the back foot.

"Eight."

And the thing that was supposed to happen was that you put the chakra
into the back foot and the ground gave it to you and you were across —
and what actually happened was that she put the chakra into the back
foot and the ground gave it to her and she was across and about a hand's
width too far, so that the fourteenth count arrived with her weight on
the wrong side of her own knee and the wood nowhere near where a
person's collar would have been.

"Ah," said her father.

"I know."

"You went hard."

"I *know*."

"You go hard every time and every time you tell me you know, and then
you go hard." He put the point of his bokken on the ground and leaned on
it, which was a thing she had noticed him doing more of lately. "It's
not a jump. You keep making it a jump."

"It looks like a jump."

"It looks like a jump when *you* do it."

---

He came over and moved her back foot with his own foot, two inches, and
put a hand flat between her shoulders.

"You've got about a thimble of it," he said. "You've had a thimble of it
since you were seven and you'll have a cup of it by the time you're
twenty if you don't do anything stupid, and the whole art is spending a
thimble like it's a thimble. You're spending it like a bucket and then
being surprised when there's a mess."

"How do I spend a thimble?"

"You put it in *late*," he said. "Not at the set. At the going. You're
paying before you've decided."

She thought about that.

That was the thing about her father: about one time in five he said
something that went into her head and stayed there, and the other four
times he said something like *don't go hard*, and there was no way
whatsoever of telling in advance which sort of afternoon it was going to
be.

"Again," she said.

"Now she wants to."

---

She did the eighth on its own for a while, without the rest of it, off
the mark, back foot, across, back to the mark.

*Late. Not at the set. At the going.*

Twelfth time. Thirteenth.

"Where does it come from?"

"What?"

"The forms. Where'd they come from?"

Her father was quiet for about a step and a half.

"My father," he said.

"And where'd he get them?"

"Ah," said her father.

She kept going, back foot, across, back to the mark, because you could
get more out of him when he had something else to look at.

"He never said. He taught me nine of them and he numbered them and the
numbers go up to fourteen." He said it perfectly evenly. "There's no
four, no six, no ten, no eleven, no thirteen. I asked him twice. First
time he said he'd forgotten them, which was a lie, because you don't
forget four and six and keep five. Second time he looked at the door for
a bit and said something about how it wasn't a good time for it."

"He did that to me too," said Uzuki. "The door thing."

"Did he."

"About my hair."

"Mm," said her father.

Fourteenth. Fifteenth.

"Do you know what happened to them? The other five?"

"No."

"Do you think about it?"

"I think," her father said, "that I've got nine, and that nine is more
than most people in this village have got, and that a man who spends his
life being cross about the five is a man who never gets very good at the
nine. Back foot. You're creeping."

She went back to the mark.

---

She got it on the eighteenth.

She knew before she landed. There was a moment somewhere between the
back foot and the ground where the payment happened later than she had
ever made it happen, so late it felt like it was not going to happen at
all, and then it did, and she was across and her weight was inside her
own knee and the fourteenth count came out of her arms without her
having to send it anywhere.

The wood stopped exactly at her father's collar.

Neither of them said anything for a second.

"There," he said.

"That's it? That's what it's meant to feel like?"

"That's what it's meant to feel like."

"That's *nothing*," said Uzuki, outraged. "That's less than the other
way. That's — I did *less* — "

"Yes."

"— than every single one of the other seventeen — "

"Yes."

" — well why didn't you *say* — "

"I said it four hundred times," said her father, "and you were spending
a bucket, and now you're not, and that's the entire lesson and it took
you a year, which is fast." He put a hand on her head, the wrong way, so
the hair went up. "Well done."

Then he sat down on the barrow.

---

He did it the way you sit down when you have decided to, and he arranged
his bokken across his knees, and he said something about the light going
and how she'd get another six repetitions before it did.

She did four of them.

Then she stopped and looked at him, and he was still sitting on the
barrow, and he had not got up.

He was not coughing. That was the part she thought about later. She had
learned to hear the coughing from three rooms away and she had a whole
set of feelings ready for it, and he was not doing it — he was sitting
on the barrow with his hands on the wood and his breath going in and out
in a considered sort of way, like a man carrying something up a hill in
stages.

"Da?"

"Go on. Two more."

"You said six."

"Then do six," he said, "and stop talking, you're wasting the light."

She did six.

He did not get up for any of them.

---

When it got too dark she came over and he held out the bokken and she
took it, and she stood there for a second with one in each hand.

"Take them in," he said.

So she took them in.

She put them in the rack by the back door, his on the top peg and hers
underneath, which was where they went, and which he had put them in
every single evening she could remember, and she stood in the doorway
afterwards for a moment looking at the two of them up there in the dark
of the passage.

Then she went and got the lamp, because he was still out on the barrow,
and it was cold, and somebody was going to have to see him back in.
