# Chapter 14, Scene 7 — One Out

*Act 2, year 1, month 2, the fourteenth to the eighteenth. The Kanaya
road, west. Nozaki Uzuki, age eleven. POV: Uzuki. ~2,150 words.*

---

She told him the number on the sixteenth and she never worked out why.

She went over it more than once, at eleven and at fifteen and once, badly,
at nineteen, and she never got a reason that held. It was not a decision.
It was the fifth evening of the way back, and the fire was going, and the
mule was about ten feet off doing the thing it did at night which was
nothing at all, and she had been quiet for about an hour, and then she
said it.

"There's one thousand four hundred and seventy-five days until I'm
fifteen."

---

He looked up.

"How did you get it?"

---

That is the whole of it and it does not look like anything, and she was
not able to explain to anybody at any point afterwards why it was the
thing it was.

She had told two people a number in her life. She had told her father, at
seven, about the days until a thing, and he had said *fancy*, and had
meant it kindly, and had gone back to what he was doing. And she had told
her mother, at nine, about the water and the trips, and her mother had
said she was a clever girl and had put a hand on her head.

Nobody had ever asked her how she got it.

---

So she told him.

It took about eleven minutes and she did it in order, oldest first,
because that is how she did everything, and he did not interrupt once.

*The twenty-sixth of the third month, which is the day I walked north out
of Nakadō with a cart and a hundred thousand ryō and four days in front of
me, and that is day nought and I set it then and I have never moved it.*

*Sixteen hundred and twenty on the twenty-second of the ninth, and I know
that one absolutely because I wrote it under a bridge at Ōgi in charcoal
on sacking and I can see the sacking.*

*Then nine days in the tenth month when I was not counting, and I did not
know I had stopped, and on the eighth I did it three ways and got three
answers within four of each other and took the lowest one and marked it as
a number I had made and not counted.*

*Sixteen hundred and three.*

*Then every day since, and the charcoal ran out on the ninth of the
twelfth and it has been in my head ever since, and it is one thousand four
hundred and seventy-five.*

---

He got the book out.

She stopped talking and watched him do it and did not know what was
happening, and what was happening was that he had turned to a blank page
at the back and was ruling it in columns.

"Say the dates again."

She said them again.

He wrote them down. He wrote every single one of them down, in the small
exact hand, in a column, with the numbers beside them, and then he did the
gaps, and then he did the gaps again in a different order, and it took him
about six minutes and he did not say anything for any of it.

She sat on the other side of the fire and watched a boy check her
arithmetic.

*He is doing it properly.*

*He is not doing it to be kind and he is not doing it to see whether I can
count. He has got a number and he is finding out whether it is right,
because that is what he does with a number, and it did not occur to him
that it might be a different sort of thing.*

*And I do not think anybody has ever done this.*

---

He put the pencil down.

"You're one out."

"What?"

"You're one under. It's fourteen seventy-six."

She said, "Where?"

And he turned the book round so she could see it, which he had never done,
and put his finger on the eighth of the tenth month.

"There. You took the low one. You had three answers within four and you
took the lowest and you marked it, and you were right to mark it and the
low one was one under. It should have been sixteen hundred and four."

He took the book back.

"And you've carried it since. Everything after that is correct. The
arithmetic's clean the whole way — a hundred and thirty-one days of it in
your head with no paper, and it's clean. It's just anchored one low."

---

Then he shut the book and put it inside his coat and went and put a stick
on the fire.

That was all of it.

He did not say *well done* and he did not say *that's impressive for
somebody who cannot write it down* and he did not look at her for longer
than he had to, and by the time she had worked out what to do with her
face he was on the other side of the fire with his back half turned doing
the thing he did with the throwables.

She sat there for a while.

*He checked it.*

*He got the book out and ruled a page and did the gaps twice and told me
where the fault was and told me the rest was clean.*

*And then he put a stick on the fire.*

She put her hands round her knees.

*I do not know what to do with this at all.*

---

She did not correct the number.

She thought about it that night and she thought about it on the seventeenth
and she was still thinking about it on the eighteenth, and she kept the
one that was one out, and she was never able to say why to anybody
including herself.

She got as close as she ever got at about the twentieth.

*Because he gave it to me.*

*No. That is not it and it is soft and I am not having it.*

She went round again.

*Because if I change it, then the number is his number. And it has been my
number since the twenty-sixth of the third month and it is the only thing
I have had the whole way through, and it went under a bridge and into a
cellar and it is the only one of my four columns that is left.*

*And it is wrong by one. And I know exactly where the one is and I know
exactly why it is there, which is that I lost nine days in Ōgi being
hungry and made a number up and was honest about making it up.*

*So the one is nine days in Ōgi.*

*And I am not taking that out.*

She never told him that either.

---

The other thing happened on the seventeenth, in the afternoon, and it was
her fault and she started it out of nothing.

"If you could have anything to eat," she said, "what would you have?"

He said, "What?"

"Anything. Anything at all, it doesn't have to be sensible, and it's in
front of you now."

He walked about forty yards.

"I don't know."

"You've got to have something."

"I haven't got anything."

---

She let it go and he did not.

She found that out about a mile and a half later, when he said, out of
nowhere, in the middle of neither of them saying anything:

"There's a thing you do with barley."

She waited.

"You do it in the pan you've done fish in and you don't wash the pan
first. And there's a — you put a green thing in it, and I don't know what
it is, and it goes almost to nothing and you can taste it and you can't
see it."

He walked for a bit.

"It takes about an hour and it's not worth an hour."

She said, "It sounds worth an hour."

"It's barley," he said, and then, after about eight paces: "It was."

---

That was the whole of it and he did not say one more word about it, and he
did not say whose pan it was, and he did not have to, and she knew inside
about two seconds and did not say anything for the rest of the afternoon
except about the road.

*He worked at that for a mile and a half.*

*I asked him a question with no answer in it and he took it away and did
it properly for a mile and a half and came back with a thing about a pan.*

*And it was not a thing he had ready. It was not a thing he had ever said.
I have got no way of proving that and I would put four hundred days on
it.*

She walked behind him and to the left and looked at the back of his head.

*So he does do it. He does it late and he does it sideways and he does it
about barley.*

---

She nearly told him about the hair on the eighteenth.

She had it as far as the front of her mouth twice, on a bank, with the hat
in her hand because it was hot for the second month and she had taken it
off for the first time in fourteen days.

She had the whole thing lined up — the bowl every two months for six
years, the door shut, the afternoon light, her father not saying, the rain
on the twenty-fifth of the tenth month, a man in a hall saying *for the
blood* and a number going into a book, and a measurement somebody had made
with his own hand at arm's length four inches from her head.

*He would check it.*

*That is what he would do. He would ask me how I got it and he would find
the fault and he would tell me exactly where it was and then he would put
a stick on the fire.*

*And there is no fault in it. It is not arithmetic. It is a thing on my
head that is worth money to people I have never met, and there is nothing
to check, and he would sit there with it and have nowhere to put it.*

She put the hat back on.

*And then he would do something about it.*

*And I have not worked out yet what he does about things, and until I have
I am not giving him one he cannot fix.*

---

They did nineteen miles on the eighteenth, which was the most they had
done in a day, and the mule was fine and she was fine and he said so, in
the ledger, that night, in four words.

She read it over his arm because she read everything over his arm now.

> *18/2. 19 mi. All three sound.*

She sat back on her heels and looked at the fire and had a thing arrive
that she did not go looking for.

*All three.*

*He has counted the mule.*

She did not say anything about that either.
