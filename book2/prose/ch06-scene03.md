# Chapter 6, Scene 3 — Eleven

*Act 1. A room, and then another room. A boy, age twelve. POV: the boy.
~1,620 words.*

---

The door opened four times, and it had never opened four times.

Water and bowl. Bucket. Bowl going back. That was three and that was
every day and it had been three every day since before the count broke.

The fourth time there was no bowl and no bucket and there were two of
them, and one of them had a cloth.

---

*Washed.*

*Second time.*

*The last time was after the man with the book.*

He stood and let them do it and did not help and did not get in the way,
because he had worked out a long time ago that helping made it take
longer, since they had to stop and see what he was doing.

The left one was the same left one and he was still worse.

*So washing comes before.*

*Last time washing came after. Warm food, more in the bowl, blanket,
washed. This time it is only washing and it is on its own.*

*So it is before.*

He did not know what it was before. He put it down as a thing that
existed and had no other side to it yet, which was most of what he had.

They took the cloth away. They did not go out.

---

They took him through the door.

He had not been through the door since a number he had stopped counting,
and the passage was longer than it was in his head, and it went up, and
he had not known it went up.

*Nine. Twenty-two. It turns. Thirty-one, thirty-two —*

He lost it at about forty because there was light at the end of it, real
light, coming down and not along, and his eyes hurt, and one of them put
a hand between his shoulder blades and moved him on.

*Start again. One.*

He got another nineteen and then there was a door and it was open.

---

There was a chair and a table and a lamp on the table, which was silly,
because there was already light.

The room was above the ground, and he did not look at the window,
because he had decided between the door and the chair that he was not
going to look at the window, and he did not go back over why.

They put him in front of the table. They did not put him in the chair.
Then they went and stood against the wall behind him and did not say
anything at all for the whole of it.

---

The man came in and he did not bring the smell of the passage in with
him.

That was the first thing and it was the only thing for a while. Everyone
who came in brought it — the wet in the stone and the bucket and the
cold — because they had come along it, and it came with them, and it went
out when they went out.

He came in out of the same passage and he did not have it on him.

*He is quiet on the floor.*

His shoes did not do anything. The keepers' feet did a thing on stone and
this man's did nothing at all, and he crossed the whole room and put a
book on the table and it did not make a sound either.

He sat down in the chair.

"Good morning," he said.

---

The boy did not say anything, because nothing had been asked.

The man waited. Then he said, "That was a greeting and not a question.
You are quite right."

He opened the book.

---

*One.*

"How old are you?"

"I don't know."

*He wrote that.*

*Two.*

"Do you know what month it is?"

"No."

*He wrote that as well.*

*Three.*

"Can you put one out for me?"

He put one out.

It was the right forearm and it was the place where the wood went and he
put it out to the length the wood went to, because that was the length,
because that was what it was for.

The man did not get up and did not come round the table and did not put
anything against it.

He looked at it for a long time.

"Ah," he said, and wrote.

---

*Four.*

"Does it grow, or does it come out of you already made?"

The boy did not understand the question and said so, which was a thing he
had not done in two years, and nothing happened to him.

The man rephrased it, twice, and neither time did he sound as though
rephrasing it was a cost.

"Is it there before I ask for it," he said, "or do you make it when I
ask?"

"I make it."

*He wrote that.*

*Five.*

"Every time?"

"Yes."

*Six.*

"Can you make it longer than that?"

"I don't know. Nobody has wanted it longer than that."

*He wrote both.*

---

*Seven.*

"Can you put one out somewhere else?"

The boy stood with his arm out and the bone out of it and did not answer.

*Somewhere else.*

*Nobody has ever asked for somewhere else. It is the arm. It is that
place on the arm because that is the place the wood goes.*

*I do not know if I may.*

He had never asked anybody anything. There was nothing to ask about,
because everything came in through the door and there was no version of
it where he had a say in what came in.

He heard himself say, "May I?"

And the man said, "Yes, of course."

---

He put one out of the back of his left hand.

It came up between the two middle knuckles and it hurt more than the arm
did, and it was smaller, and it was not straight, and he looked at it and
had no idea at all whether it was right.

Nobody said anything. The keepers did not say anything.

*That is the first thing I have ever done in this building that was mine.*

*They asked and I picked where.*

He did not have anywhere to put that, so he stood with his hand out and
waited.

"Ah," said the man. And wrote, for longer than the other times.

---

*Eight.*

"Does that hurt?"

Nobody had ever asked him anything about himself.

They had asked him to stand up and to hold still and to put his arm out
and to be quiet, and those were not questions even when they had the
shape of one. This had the shape of one and was one.

"Yes," he said.

*Nine.*

"Which one hurts more?"

"The hand."

"By a lot?"

"Yes."

He wrote all of it.

---

*Ten.*

"How long does it take to come back?"

The boy stood.

*Come back.*

*From where.*

*Nothing has ever gone.*

"I don't know," he said.

The man looked up from the book.

It was the only time he did that and it was not unkind and it was not
anything the boy could read at all, and it lasted about as long as it
takes to put a bowl down.

"Nobody has taken one," said the man.

"No."

"Ah," he said. And did not write.

---

*Eleven.*

"Would you break that one off for me?"

*Would you.*

He had never been asked to do anything. He had been told, and the telling
was fine, the telling was the shape of the whole world. *Would you* was
not a shape at all. It had a gap in it where he was supposed to go.

He put his right hand over the back of his left and he broke it off.

---

It was worse than anything that had ever happened to him.

It was worse than the wood and worse than the wall and worse than the
long time when the count broke, and it went up his arm and stopped his
breath and put something behind his eyes, and he stood absolutely still
and did not make any noise at all.

*Do not make a noise.*

*A noise makes the visit longer.*

He held it out on his flat palm with the blood coming up in the two
knuckles behind it, and it was about an inch and a half long and it was
whitish and it was his.

The man leaned forward and looked at it on the boy's hand and did not
take it and did not touch him.

He watched the boy's face for a while instead.

Then he wrote.

---

*And then one more, after he had closed the book.*

"Who taught you?"

"Nobody."

He had already closed the book. He opened it again and wrote that one
very briefly, and closed it, and stood up.

"Thank you," he said.

---

They took him back down the passage and it went down and it was shorter
and he did not count it.

The bolt went over.

He sat on the mat with his left hand held in his right and the two
knuckles going and he had the whole of it laid out and he went through it
in order, twice.

*Eleven. He wrote after nine.*

*He was in the room for about four hundred.*

*He said good morning and he said thank you and he said of course.*

He stopped on that one and could not get past it, because there was
nowhere to put it and no other one like it to put it with.

*That is the first time.*

*I have not got a number for that, because it is one.*

He put his hand round his hand.

*It went well.*

*I answered all of them and I did the thing that was asked, and the hand
one was mine.*

*They are going to use me.*
