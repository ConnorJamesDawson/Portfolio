# Chapter 4, Scene 7 — The Thing He Told Her

*Act 1, month 4, the third, evening. Kanmuri lane, Kayano. Nozaki Uzuki,
age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,450 words.*

---

"You've got about a fifth of it," said Anzai.

They were in the front room and it was properly dark outside and Chiyo
was putting things in a cloth at the table with the sort of energy that
meant nobody was going to be allowed to refuse them.

"A fifth."

"A fifth, and I'm being generous because you're going to walk out of here
and I'd rather you walked out of here with a number than a feeling." He
was sitting down for the first time since two o'clock and he looked, if
anything, worse. "You can find it. You can hold it. You can put a small
one out and stop, about one time in three. You cannot do ninety and you
will not be able to do ninety for about six months."

"How do I get to ninety?"

"By doing eighty-nine," said Anzai. "There isn't a way round it and
anybody who offers you one is selling something. Every day. On yourself,
on your mother, on a dog if there's a dog. Ninety a day and you'll have it
by the autumn.

"And write down what you do," he said. "Not the feelings. The number, and
where your hand was, and what it was like after. Because in four months
you'll be certain you've got worse, and you won't have, and the only
thing on this earth that'll get you past that is a piece of paper with
last month on it."

Uzuki got her father's stub of pencil out and wrote *90* on the back of
the mill's chit, under *water queue, 6, ask 3.*

---

"Now sit down," said Anzai, "because I'm going to tell you the last thing
and it's the one that matters, and I want you looking at me while I do
it."

She sat down.

"You're going to go home," he said, "and you're going to put your hands
on your father, and it is not going to be enough."

---

The room was very quiet.

"I know," said Uzuki. "You said. Tazu said. The nights and the edge and
not the thing itself, I know — "

"You do not know," said Anzai, "and I'm not going to argue with you about
whether you know, because you can't know it yet and I could not have
either at your age, and I'm going to say it anyway so that it's *said*,
because that's all I can do about it.

"Here's what's going to happen.

"You're going to be good at it. You're better at it now than you have any
business being after three and a half hours and you're going to be
properly good by the winter. And your father is going to have some
nights that he would not have had, and that is real, and it is worth what
you paid for it and more.

"And he is going to get worse anyway. And there is going to come a
night — and it will be one specific night, and you'll know it while it's
happening — when you put your hands on him and do everything right, every
single thing, ninety of them, exactly the way you've been taught, and it
will make no difference at all.

"And you will decide," said Anzai, "that it made no difference because
you did it wrong."

---

Uzuki sat in the chair in the front room and did not say anything.

"You'll go back over it," he said. "That's what you'll do, because you're
that sort. You'll go over the whole night and you'll find one — one go
out of ninety where you weren't sure, or one where you pushed, or the bit
where you were tired and your hand slipped an inch — and you will build
the whole of it onto that.

"And you'll be wrong. It won't be that. It will be that what's in your
father cannot be reached by what's in your hands, and it never could
be, and it could not have been reached by mine either, and it could not
have been reached if you'd walked here a year ago."

He leaned forward.

"I want that last part in you now, before it happens. A year ago. It
would not have worked a year ago either."

"You don't know that. You've never seen him."

"No," said Anzai. "I have never seen him and I do not know what he has
got and I am not going to give it a name from a child's description,
because that is how men in my trade tell themselves comfortable stories.

"So take it as this and not as more than this: *the thing you are
frightened of is that you were too late. And I have watched a great many
people be too late, and about one in nine of them actually were.*"

---

She got up and she was shaking and it was not the arms.

"That's not — you can't just — "

"I can't," Anzai agreed. "That's exactly right. I can't. I've told you a
true thing you're not able to use, and you'll carry it about for years
being no help to you at all, and then one day you'll be about twenty-six
and it'll come back and it'll do something."

He got up too, with an effort.

"That's the whole of what an old man can do for you," he said. "I can't
stop it. I can only put it in your hands early so that when you go
looking for something to hold, there's something in there that isn't a
lie."

---

Chiyo tied the cloth and gave it to her at the door and would not be
thanked.

"Bread, the rest of that rice, and an egg, and there's a thing in the
bottom for your mother that you're not to open." She stood back. "Four
days?"

"Three. I can do it in three."

"Do it in four."

"Three."

"God help everyone who ever tries to tell you anything," said Chiyo, and
went in.

---

Anzai came as far as the doorway.

He stood at the top of the step with one hand on the frame and looked up
the lane, where somebody was going along the top with a lamp.

"Anzai-sensei."

"Mm."

"Your step's nine inches," said Uzuki. "It's in shadow from about noon
and the lane slopes and there's nothing on the front of it. Thirty-one
people have gone over it since Tuesday and four went right down and one
of them was an old man with a basket."

Anzai looked down at it.

"Thirty-one."

"Since Tuesday. I counted. If somebody put a bit of white paint along
the nose of it, or a rail on that side, it'd — "

"My father put that step in," said Anzai.

"I know. A man told me. He said three people have offered you a rail."

"Four."

"Then — "

"My father put that step in," said Anzai, "in about the second year of
his practice, when he could not afford to have it done properly, and it
is nine inches because he got the levels wrong and he was too proud to
take it up again, and he was a difficult man and I did not like him very
much and he has been dead nineteen years."

He put his hand flat on the door frame.

"And I go down it about eleven times a day," he said, "and every single
time, for nineteen years, my foot has gone down nine inches and expected
five, and I have thought about him. Every time. Eleven times a day.

"And if I put a rail on it I'll stop."

Uzuki stood in the lane and looked up at him.

"That's the worst reason I've ever heard," she said.

"It is," said Anzai. "Mind the step."

---

She went up Kanmuri lane at about half past seven with the cart and the
cloth and everything else, and at the top she turned round once.

The green door that had been blue was shut and there was a light behind
the front window, and the step was in the dark, being nine inches.

*Ninety a day.*

*Write down the number and where the hand was.*

*And one night it won't be enough and it won't be because I did it
wrong.*

She got that far and stopped, because she was ten and it was the third of
the month and her father was four days away and alive.

*I'll do it in three,* she thought, and went out of Kayano on the north
road in the dark, walking, because sleeping was a thing she was going to
do on the way.
