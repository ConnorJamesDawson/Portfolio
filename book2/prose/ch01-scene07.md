# Chapter 1, Scene 7 — Short

*Act 1, month 2. Nakadō, the kitchen and the Ōgi road. Nozaki Uzuki,
age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,510 words.*

---

Nobody said her father had stopped working.

What happened was that the slate stopped filling up.

The slate hung by the front door and it had the week on it in her
mother's writing — days down the left, jobs against them, and a line
through each one when it was done. In a good week the slate ran out of
room and things got written up the side. In an ordinary week there were
nine or ten.

In the second week of the second month there were four.

Uzuki stood in front of it one morning with a piece of bread in her hand
and looked at that for a while.

*Ōgi Tuesday. Mill. The letter for the crossroads man. Fusano Friday.*

Fusano Friday had a line through it already and it was Wednesday.

---

She asked her mother about it, which was a mistake, because her mother
was at the table with the box out.

The box came out on the last day of the month. That was the rule of the
box: her father tipped it out and her mother counted it and there was a
conversation about the roof, and then the box went back on the shelf.
It was not the last day of the month. It was the eleventh.

"Mm," said her mother, when she asked, and did not look up.

"There's only four."

"There's four this week."

"There were eleven the week before Ōgi."

"There were." Her mother moved two coins from one side of the table to
the other and wrote something. "Eat that or put it down, you're getting
crumbs in it."

Uzuki ate it.

She stood there and ate it and watched her mother's hand, and her
mother's hand went: a pile, and a number written; another pile, and the
number crossed out and a new number written; and then a long pause with
the pen sitting in the air, and then a line drawn under everything and a
number at the bottom.

Uzuki could read the numbers. She could not read the *sum*. She could
see that things had been moved from one side to another and that the
answer had been arrived at twice, and she did not know which side was
the good side, and there was no way to ask that did not sound like
asking whether they had any money.

"Who's doing the Ōgi round now?"

"Nobody's doing the Ōgi round now, it's Wednesday."

"Who's doing the *parcels*," said Uzuki. "The mill's had somebody twice
this week, I saw the barrow."

Her mother put the pen down.

"Iwasa's boy is doing some of them," she said.

"Da does the mill."

"Da does the mill when Da does the mill."

"He can do the mill."

"Uzuki."

"He can walk to the *mill*, it's — "

"Uzuki," her mother said, "nobody's taken it off him. Nobody's said a
word to him or to me. They just stopped putting them in his hand, and
that's how it's done, and you'll find that out about people sooner or
later so you may as well find it out now."

She picked the pen back up.

"Go and see if he wants anything."

---

He was in the back room in the chair with his feet up on the stool and a
blanket he had not asked for over his knees, and he was mending a strap.

That was the other thing about the second month: he had started mending
things. Everything in the house that had a fault in it was getting the
fault taken out of it, one item a day, in order, and Uzuki had thought
at first that this was excellent and had brought him a basket lid.

"Anything you want?"

"Tea in a bit. Not yet."

"There's four things on the slate."

"There's four things on the slate," her father agreed, and turned the
strap over.

"Iwasa's boy is doing the mill."

"Iwasa's boy is fourteen and has a barrow and no sense, and he'll do the
mill until he drops a sack of it in the ford, and then they'll come
back." He bit the end of the thread. "Don't go and say anything to
Iwasa's boy."

"I wasn't going to say anything to Iwasa's boy."

"Uzuki."

"I *wasn't*."

---

She went to Ōgi on the Thursday.

Nobody sent her. That was the whole of it, and she was clear about it
afterwards and clear about it at the time: nobody sent her. She got up
before either of them, and she took the little cart, and she took the
two parcels that were sitting by the door for the mill because they were
sitting by the door for the mill, and she went.

It was eleven miles and she did it in a bit under three hours with the
cart, which she was pleased about, and she was not going to be able to
tell anybody she was pleased about it.

The mill took the parcels. The man at the mill said, "Where's your da,"
and she said he was busy, and the man said, "Aye," in a way she did not
like the shape of, and gave her a chit.

Then she did the thing that she thought at the time was clever.

There were three more things in Ōgi that her father usually collected
when he was there — a return parcel from the cooper, a bag from the
carter's yard, and the money from the man at the crossroads for the
letter, which was two hundred and forty and had been owed since the
month before.

She did all three.

The cooper's return went on the cart. The bag went on the cart. And she
walked out to the crossroads and knocked on the door and said she had
come for the two hundred and forty for the letter.

The man was perfectly nice about it.

He said, ah, well now, and that he did not have it on him today, and
that he would settle up with her father next time and there was no sense
her carrying money on that road at her age anyway. And then he said,
since she'd come all that way, would she take a hundred on account and
tell her da the rest was safe as houses.

And Uzuki, who was ten, and who had come eleven miles with a cart and
wanted very badly to arrive home with something in her hand, said thank
you and took the hundred.

---

She got back at four with the cart full and a chit and a hundred in a
twist of cloth, and she was so ready to be told off for going that she
had the whole of her side of it prepared on the last mile.

Her mother came out and looked at the cart.

"You did the cooper."

"And the carter's yard. And the mill's given a chit for the two big
ones, he said settle at month end."

"You went to the crossroads."

"He gave me a hundred on account," said Uzuki. "He says the rest is safe
as houses and he'll settle with Da next time."

Her mother stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

Uzuki held the twist of cloth out.

Her mother took it, and unwound it, and counted it, which took no time
at all because it was a hundred, and then she wound it back up.

She did not say anything.

She did not say *he owes two hundred and forty and he has owed it since
the eighth of last month and your father has been to that door four
times*, and she did not say *a man who will not pay a grown carrier will
always pay a child something, because something buys him another two
months*, and she did not say *you have just sold two hundred and forty
for a hundred and there is no getting it back now, because it is settled
in his head and it will never be unsettled*.

She said: "You must be starving."

"I could eat a horse."

"There's no horse," said her mother. "There's the end of yesterday's and
you'll have to make do."

---

Uzuki ate the end of yesterday's, standing up, at the counter, still in
her coat, and told them both about the road and the ford being down and
how Iwasa's boy had gone the long way round with the barrow and she had
passed him twice.

Her father laughed at the right places.

At some point her mother put the twist of cloth on the shelf above the
stove, at the back, behind the tin, where things went that were not
going to be talked about.

Uzuki saw her do it.

She thought — and she was pleased with herself for thinking it, and she
turned it over for a while that evening and could not find the hole in
it — that her mother had put it up high because a hundred was worth
keeping safe.
