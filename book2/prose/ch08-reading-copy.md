# Chapter 8 — The Right Answer

*Book two. Act one, month 8, the 2nd – the 29th.
Eleven scenes, 18,480 words.*




*Act 1, month 8, the second. A hay barn above Ōtoma. Tadayoshi Ruri,
age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,830 words.*

---

The line came on the bottom of a docket and it was not a job.

> *2nd of the 8th. Ōtoma. The barn on the hill, any time after dark.*
> *No parcel.*

*No parcel.*

He looked at that for a long time on the road, because in eight weeks
there had been four sheets and every single one of them had been a place,
a time, a name, a thing to say, and a parcel, and this one had the first
three and the shape where the fourth ought to be.

*So it is not a job and he has told me that in two words rather than
leave me working it out for three days.*

*Which is the third time he has done that, and I have started to notice
that he does it, and I have not once seen him do it.*

---

The barn was half full and the doors were open at both ends and Kajiya was
sitting on the good end of a bale about a third of the way in, where he
could see both doorways and was not framed in either.

He looked exactly the same as he had in a yard at Sakabe on the first of
the sixth month, which was two months, and which was somehow the thing
Tadayoshi found most disquieting about him.

*He has not got worse and he has not got better and he has not got any
older.*

*And he is tired in the way that a thing is tired when it has been tired
for four years and has stopped going up and down.*

"Sit down."

He sat down.

"Four jobs," said Kajiya. "Fukae. Hanase. Then the two in the seventh
month, the one out to Nunobiki and the one back."

"Yes."

"You opened none of them."

"No."

"How near did you come?"

*He has asked me a question with a right answer in it and the right
answer is not none.*

"I learned the knot on the first one," said Tadayoshi. "On the milestone
outside Sakabe, on the second. It took about four minutes. I could have
put it back and nobody would have known."

Kajiya nodded once and did not say anything, and Tadayoshi understood
about a year later that not saying anything at that particular moment
had been a decision.

---

"Right," said Kajiya. "Then here's what that was."

*What what was.*

"You've been on trial since the first of the sixth month. That's what
carrying is. It isn't a job, it's an exam, and everybody starts on it and
most of them are still on it a year later and don't know."

Tadayoshi sat on a bale in a barn and had the entire preceding two months
turn over underneath him.

*I have not been working.*

*I have been being watched work.*

"You thought it was a job," said Kajiya.

"Yes."

"Good. That's the point of it."

---

"What was the exam?"

"Whether you'd do a dull thing right four times when nobody was
watching."

"Somebody was watching."

"Somebody was checking," said Kajiya. "That's not the same and don't
confuse them, it'll get you killed. Nobody stood behind you on a road for
nine days. Somebody at the far end had a look at a knot."

*And a knot you have looked at is a knot you can see has been looked
at.*

*So the check was not whether the parcel was intact. It was whether the
cord had been through my hands.*

*And it had. I learned it on a milestone.*

He said so.

"I know," said Kajiya. "He said it was the cleanest one he'd had off
anybody and that you'd plainly had a go at it and hadn't finished. He
was pleased with you. So was I."

---

"And there's the other half," said Kajiya, "and I'm going to tell you
because you'll work it out in about a year and it'll sit worse then."

He shifted on the bale.

"One of the four had nothing in it."

*What?*

"Rags. About the weight of a book. Tied properly, waxed, sealed like the
rest, and a man at the other end took it off you and said thank you and
put it under a bench and it's still there."

Tadayoshi sat very still.

"Which one?"

"Hanase."

---

*Hanase.*

*Four days east from Fukae. Then eleven minutes in a yard with a man with
a bad chest. Then the bed I would not take, twice, in front of him, so
that he was offended and will remember my face for a year.*

*Then a sixteenth for a floor. Then a man asking a keeper for ages at two
in the morning. Then a window and seven feet and eleven miles.*

*Then a coat on a nail.*

He did the sum. He could not have stopped himself doing the sum.

*Nine days of walking, a sixteenth, a coat, and the whole of the second
line of a book I have started, and it was rags.*

He waited for it to be a bad feeling.

It did not come.

---

*Because that is not what it was.*

He sat on the bale and worked it through properly, the way he worked
everything through, and it came out in about eleven seconds and it came
out clean.

*The parcel was not the product.*

*I was not carrying a thing from a man to a man. Somebody wanted to know
what a boy of ten does over four days when there is nobody behind him,
and the only way to find that out is to send him four days with something,
and the something has to be real enough that he does not know it is not.*

*So the four days were the product. The four days are what got made.*

**Nothing I did was wasted. It was all of it the point.**

*And the coat went on the same road either way, because the man at two in
the morning was not there about a parcel.*

He looked up.

"That's all right," he said.

Kajiya looked at him for slightly longer than the question needed.

"Mm," he said.

---

"Right. Now the reason, and you'll not like the reason, and I'm telling
you it first because I'd rather you had it before you had anything
nice."

*He does this.*

*He did it in the yard as well. He says the worst thing at the front. He
has done it twice now and both times it was the thing that made me
believe the rest of it.*

"Go on."

"Every team they've got looking at you," said Kajiya, "is a team that
isn't looking at somebody else."

He let that sit.

"That's the whole of it. That's the entire reason and there isn't another
one underneath. You put four of theirs in a dry bed and there was a man
in a lodging at Hanase eight weeks later asking a keeper how old
everybody in the house was, and I've got that date on a sheet, and eight
weeks is fast. Eight weeks is very fast.

"So you're going to be hunted whether we do anything about it or not.
And that means the only question anybody round this table has got is
whether the thing they're hunting is any good at it."

---

"An untrained one lasts about eight months," said Kajiya. "Then he's dead
in a ditch and we've got nothing and they've got their attention back and
it comes down on the next lot. A trained one goes on drawing for years."

*Years.*

"So we're going to spend some money on you," said Kajiya. "And I want you
to be clear about what that is, because in about four years somebody will
tell you it was because we thought you were special, and they'll believe
it, and it will be a lie. It's because you're a thing they can't stop
looking at, and every year you go on being that is a year of quiet for
about eleven hundred people you will never meet."

He got up off the bale.

"That's what you're for," he said. "Do you want it?"

---

Tadayoshi sat in the barn.

He was aware, at the time, of the shape of what he was supposed to feel,
because he had read enough and been in enough kitchens to know how people
were meant to take a thing like that. He waited for it. He gave it a fair
chance.

It did not arrive, and what arrived instead was so much better that he
did not examine it for about two years.

*He has costed me.*

*He has taken a boy of ten and put him against eleven hundred people and
a number of years and worked out what the trade is, and he has done the
whole sum out loud in front of me, including the part where I am a thing
being spent, and he has not put one single word on it to make it go down
easier.*

*It is correct. I have checked it and it is correct.*

*And nobody has ever done that before.*

He went back through it, because he did not believe things until he had
been round them twice, and it held. Every term of it held.

**Nobody has ever told me what I am for.**

*My mother gave me instructions and never once gave me a reason. Meno
told me what a thing was. Aoyagi told me where to stand. My father told
me what he thought about hiding and did not tell me what to do about it.*

*And a man in a barn has just told me, in about ninety words, exactly
what I am worth and exactly what for and exactly what it costs, and he
did it without being asked, and he did not make it kind.*

*That is the first honest thing anybody has said to me about myself since
the fifth of the fourth month.*

He stood up.

"Yes," he said.

---

"Right," said Kajiya. He was already moving. "Then the carrying stops."

"Stops?"

"You'll not carry another parcel for me as long as you live. There's a
man at Ōtoma bottom end, name of Hōki. You'll be with him from the ninth.
Do what he says, which will mostly be walking about looking at ground,
and don't correct him in front of anybody."

He got to the barn door and stopped in it, which was a thing Tadayoshi had
already noticed he did not do.

"And boy."

"Yes."

"The four days weren't wasted," said Kajiya. "But you worked that out on
the bale, and you worked it out in about ten seconds, and you were pleased
with yourself for it."

He went out.

Tadayoshi stood in a hay barn above Ōtoma on the second of the eighth
month with an empty docket in his hand and no idea at all what had just
been said to him.

*Act 1, month 8, the eighth. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki.
~1,800 words.*

---

There were four marks in it by the eighth.

She got the bag out in the afternoon, when there was nobody in the house
and nobody due, and sat on the floor of her own room with her back
against the wall and the door shut and laid it out flat on her knees.

*Twentieth of the seventh.*

*Twenty-ninth.*

*Third of the eighth.*

*Sixth.*

She looked at those for a while.

*Right. Do it properly. Don't sit here being frightened at a piece of
flour bag. It's four numbers. You can do something with four numbers.*

---

She started with the thing she thought she was starting with, which was
*why*, and she did it the way her father had done the mill chits: one
question at a time, all the way through, and write down the answer even
when the answer is no.

*Is it a day of the week.*

The twentieth was a Tuesday, because it was a Kuriya day and Kuriya days
are Tuesday and Friday, and she had been up there in the afternoon and
come back with her hands not right.

The twenty-ninth was a Friday. So that was two for two and for about four
seconds she had it.

Then the third was not either. And nor was the sixth.

*No.*

*Two out of four is not a thing. Two out of four is what you get if you
put four marks anywhere in a week.*

*Is it drink.*

She went and got the box off the shelf, which she was allowed to do now
and had been allowed to do since about the fifth of the seventh month, and
went down the out column for both months.

There was drink in it. There was drink in it in the way there is drink in
any house — a little, at month end, and once for the wedding — and there
was none of it anywhere near the third and none anywhere near the
twentieth.

*No.*

*Is it money.*

She went down the out column properly for that one, both months, and
looked at every single line, and then she looked at the in column, and
then she looked at market days, because she knew Ōgi went on the fourth
and the eighteenth and Iwasa's man took the cart both times.

The sixth was two days after the fourth.

*Is that anything?*

*It might be anything. Two days after a market is when you know what you
got.*

She wrote *market?* on the back of the bag with a stroke under it,
because it was not an answer and she was not going to write it in a
column as if it were.

*Is it Mother.*

She made herself do that one and she made herself do the whole of it,
because leaving it out because she did not like it was exactly the thing
her father would have taken the chit off her for.

*What did she do on the twentieth.*

*Barley. She did the barley and it turned out well and she said so
twice.*

*The twenty-ninth?*

*I don't know.*

*The third?*

*I don't know.*

*The sixth she was at the water in the morning with Ine and she came back
in a good mood.*

She sat with that.

*I don't know. That's the answer to two of the four and it's the honest
answer and I'm putting it down.*

*And the two I do know are both her being all right.*

---

*Is it me.*

That one took her longer and it was the one she had been walking round
for about a week without admitting she was walking round it.

*Was I in the house. Yes, all four.*

*Was I up. No. Bed by about nine, all four, except the third where I was
up till ten doing the thing to the basket.*

*Had I said anything to either of them that day.*

She went through it and it was nothing. A bucket. A gate. Something about
the mule. She had said perhaps forty words in the house on the sixth and
she could account for most of them.

*No.*

*It isn't me.*

She was surprised by how much of a relief that was, and then immediately
ashamed of the relief, and put both of those down and moved on.

---

By the time she had finished she had eleven questions on the back of the
flour bag and eleven answers and nine of them were *no* and two of them
were *?*.

And she sat on the floor of her own room with her back against the brick
and understood what she had actually found, and it was not a rule.

*There isn't one in this house.*

*I have gone through everything that happens inside these four rooms.
What she does, what she says, what I do, what's in the box, what's on the
shelf, what day it is. Nine no's.*

*And the two that aren't no are both outside.*

*Market. And the thing that broke on the shed on the second of the
eighth, which is the day before the third.*

*And that's the shape of it. That's what all four of them have got in
common if they've got anything: something happened somewhere else.*

She put her head back on the brick.

*So it isn't her.*

*She isn't doing it. She isn't causing it and she can't stop causing it
and there is nothing she could be doing differently, because the thing
that decides it is a price at Ōgi or a hinge on a shed or the weather in
somebody else's field.*

*Which is —*

She got it, and it went through her properly, and she sat on the floor
with the bag on her knees.

**The thing that decides it isn't in the house, so nobody in the house
can do anything about it.**

*I've been looking for a rule so that I could manage it. That's what I
was doing. I sat down to find out what sets it off so that I could stop
it happening.*

*And there isn't one.*

*And a rule would have been better. A rule would have been the best thing
that could have happened. If it was drink I could water it. If it was
money I could earn. If it was Mother I could tell her.*

*Four marks and no rule is a thing you can only wait for.*

---

She very nearly stopped there.

She had the bag on her knees and her back on the wall and she had been at
it for about two hours and she had got an answer, and it was a bad answer,
and there is a point at which a person puts a thing away.

She did not put it away, because she was her father's daughter and
because there was one more sum on that bag she had not done, and she knew
she had not done it, and she was not going to be a person who leaves a
sum.

*The gaps.*

*Twentieth to the twenty-ninth is nine.*

*Twenty-ninth to the third is five.*

*Third to the sixth is three.*

She wrote them down.

*Nine. Five. Three.*

---

She looked at those for a long time.

*That's not nothing.*

*That is very much not nothing.*

She did it again on the back of the bag, in a column, and it came out the
same, because three numbers do not change when you write them twice.

*Nine, five, three.*

*It's going down.*

*Each one's less than the one before and it's less by about the same, and
if it goes on the way it's going —*

She stopped, and made herself do it, and did it in her head first and then
on the bag because she did not trust her head with it.

*Nine, five, three. Next one's two. Then it's one or it's two again.*

*And then it's every day.*

*And nine and five and three is nineteen days and I have gone from
nineteen days to about ten and it's the eighth of the eighth month.*

---

She sat on the floor of her own room and was more frightened than she had
been at any point since the twentieth of the seventh month, and the thing
that frightened her was not a sound through a wall.

It was three numbers she had written herself on the back of a flour bag.

*Check it.*

*Don't sit here going hot. Check it. Are the dates right.*

They were right. She had written all four of them on the night, in the
dark, with the door shut, and she had never once been wrong about a date
in her life.

*Are the gaps right.*

She counted them on her fingers, twice, both directions.

*Nine, five, three.*

*Right.*

*So it's right, and it's getting closer, and I can put a number on how
much closer it's getting, and I have got no idea at all why.*

---

She sat there until the light started going and then she did the last
thing, which was the useful one, and which took about four seconds and
changed the next two months of her life.

*Everything on this bag is about me.*

She looked at the four columns.

*The ninety is me. The money is me. The days till I'm fifteen is me.*

*And the fourth one is her.*

*There is not one single thing on this bag about him.*

*Not what he does. Not where he goes. Not what kind of a day he's had.
Not one mark, in a month and a half, about the person the whole of it is
actually about.*

*Because I decided on the sixteenth of the seventh month that he wasn't a
thing I was going to write down.*

She folded the bag.

*Right.*

*Then I start.*

---

She did not put it in a column, because a column has a heading and a
heading is a thing somebody could read.

She started keeping it in her head instead, the way she kept the other
one, the one that had been running since she was seven and had never been
written down anywhere: what he did, and when, and how it went, and what
came before it.

She started that evening, at the table, with the barley.

*Came in about six. Went out again to the shed and came back. Sat down.
Said something about Iwasa's gate. Ate all of it.*

*Nothing.*

*Fine. Then that's a nothing day and a nothing day goes in as well,
because a column with only bad days in it isn't a column, it's a
grievance.*

She put the bag behind the brick and went and helped with the water.

*Nine, five, three.*

*Two.*

*Act 1. A room. A boy, age twelve. POV: the boy. ~1,290 words.*

---

He knew the passage by gaps.

His door went, and then there was a gap, and then another door went, and
the length of the gap was which door it was. He had never decided to learn
that and could not have said when he had; it had been true for as long as
the counting had been true.

*Mine.*

*Six, and then that one.*

*Then a long one, longer than I can hold, and then two together that are
almost the same and I have never been able to separate them.*

*And there was a nine, and the nine stopped.*

The nine had stopped a long time ago. He had counted the days since and
then stopped counting them, and the number was gone now and he did not
try to get it back.

The six had never stopped. The six had been there every day of the whole
of it.

---

He was thirty-eight days into it.

He did not have a name for what he was thirty-eight days into. He had the
shape of it, and he had got the shape by having been through the other
one, and the two of them sat in his head side by side like two rows of the
same length.

*The first time it went: the man with the wood. Then nine days and the
food came warm. Then more in the bowl. Then the blanket, folded. Then
washed, by two who were not the keeper.*

*The second time it went: the room with the lamp. Then ten days and
nothing. Then the blanket, taken, folded, going out. Then the food cold
on the eleventh. Then less in it.*

*And then thirty-eight days of nothing at all.*

He had worked out early on that the second row did not have an end on it
yet.

He had not worked out what went on the end.

---

The inspection at the six-door happened on a day he did not have a number
for, because he did not number days, but he had it against his own bowls
and that was enough.

He knew what it was before it got there.

*More feet.*

That was the whole of it. That was the only thing an inspection ever was
from inside a room with a bolt on it: the passage carried two people every
day and on that day it carried four, and four people walk differently from
two — not louder, *rougher*, because two people walk in step after a while
and four never do.

*Four. And one of them stops.*

*Somebody stands in the passage while the others go in.*

Then the six-door, and the bolt, and then a long time.

*Long.*

He counted it, because there was nothing else to count. He got to about
four hundred and lost it and started again and got another hundred and
some, and then the bolt went, and the four went back up the passage, and
his own door did not open at all that day until the bowl.

---

Then it started.

He did not see any of it. He never saw one single thing that happened at
the six-door in two years. What he had was the count and the count was
enough.

*The bowl's gone in twice today. Not three.*

He noticed it on the second day of twice and he did not want to have
noticed it.

*Twice.*

*And the bucket didn't come out.*

He sat with his back on the wall and put his hand round his left hand,
which was a thing he had started doing in the sixth month and had not
stopped.

*Cold. It'll be cold now.*

He did not know that. He could not know it; there was a wall and a
passage and a door between him and any bowl but his own. **He knew it the
way you know it is raining.**

*And then less in it.*

*And then — *

He stopped there, because he did not have the end of the row, and putting
a thing on the end of a row when you have not got it is how you get a
number wrong.

---

It took eleven days from the twice.

On the fourth day of it the passage carried two people again and one of
them stopped at the six-door and did not go in, and stood there for a
while, and went on.

On the seventh the bowl at the six-door went in once.

On the ninth and the tenth he could not tell, because the keeper was quick
that week and the gaps went short and he lost two of them.

On the eleventh, the six-door did not open.

---

He waited.

That was the whole of the next part and there is nothing else to say about
it. He sat on a mat with his back on the wall and he waited, and the
passage went up and down with the bowls and the bucket, and every single
time a door went he counted the gap after it, and it was never six.

*One.*

*Two.*

*Four.*

He got to nine and stopped counting them, the same as he had with the
other one, and this time he knew exactly why he was stopping and did it
on purpose.

*Because a number you keep is a number that is going to get big.*

---

Then he did the thing he had been not-doing since the second day of twice,
and he did it in the dark, on the mat, with his hand round his hand.

*Put them next to each other.*

*His went: the four feet. Then a long time in the room. Then eleven days.
Then the door.*

*Mine went: the room with the lamp. Then ten days and nothing. Then the
blanket. Then cold. Then less.*

*Then thirty-eight.*

*And thirty-eight is more than eleven.*

He sat with that.

*So it isn't the same.*

*Or it is the same and it is slower, because I am not the same as him,
because they got something out of me and I do not know whether they got
anything out of him.*

*Or it is the same and it is slower and slower is the whole of what I
have got.*

He put his forehead on his knees.

**He had a shape for what was going to happen to him and he had built it
out of a door he had never seen, four along a passage, from the gaps
between other people's bowls.**

*And I knew.*

*I knew on the second day of twice. I knew before it happened and I got
it right and there was nothing at all I could do with it.*

---

He never found out who it was.

He did not know whether it had been a boy or a girl. He did not know
whether they were older than him or younger, or what they could do, or
whether they had had a wall to mark and had stopped marking it, or whether
they had ever heard *his* door and counted the gap after it and made a
number out of him the way he had made a number out of them.

He had one thing about that person and he had it for the rest of his life
and it was this:

*The gap was six.*

He said it once, out loud, in the dark, which was the first time he had
said anything out loud in a room on his own in two years, and it did not
sound like anything, and he did not do it again.

Then he lay down and counted the bowl in the morning.

*One hundred and eighty-three.*

*Act 1, month 8, the ninth to the twentieth. Ōtoma. Tadayoshi Ruri, age
ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,870 words.*

---

Hōki was about fifty and had a bad knee he did not favour and hands that
had been broken at some point and set well, and he was the first person
Tadayoshi had ever met whom he could not get a read on in under a minute.

*He is not kind and he is not unkind. That is not two things I have
weighed against each other. It is that neither of them is present.*

*And he has looked at me for about eleven seconds in total across two
days and he has never once looked at my hands, and everybody looks at my
hands.*

"Right," said Hōki, on the ninth. "Walk to the end of that lane and come
back."

Tadayoshi walked to the end of the lane and came back.

"What did you leave?"

---

He had nine.

He gave them in order, because that was how he gave things: a footprint at
the gate where the mud was standing; the gate itself, moved and not put
back; a dog he had made bark, which was not a mark but was a record;
the four seconds he had spent looking at the shut window on the left,
which anybody watching would have seen him spend; where he had crossed,
and that he had crossed at the narrow bit, which is what a person who does
not want to be in the open does; his own tracks doubled, out and back, on
the same side; the wet on the wall where he had put a hand on it; the
time, because it was eleven in the morning and there is no reason for a
boy to be in a lane at eleven in the morning; and the fact that he had
come back at all, which is a thing almost nobody does.

Hōki listened to all nine without moving.

"Three more," he said.

"Which three?"

"That's the lesson," said Hōki, and went indoors.

---

That was how the whole of it went and it took Tadayoshi about four days to
stop hating it.

**Hōki asked questions and did not answer them.**

He did not explain anything. He did not correct anything. He would say a
thing like *what did you leave* or *where would you have gone* or *why is
that man not looking at you*, and then he would wait, and then, whatever
you said, he would say *and* or *three more* or nothing at all, and go
and do something else.

*He is doing it on purpose and he is doing it because it works on me.*

*I cannot leave a question standing. I have never once been able to. He
found that out on the ninth in about a minute and he has been using it
ever since and he has not made one single remark about it.*

*It is the most efficient thing anybody has ever done to me.*

He got the three, on the fourth day, in the middle of eating.

*The lane is a dead end and I did not know it was a dead end when I went
into it, which means I went into a lane without knowing what was at the
end, and anybody watching knows I did not know.*

*I looked at the shut window on the left and I did not look at the shut
window on the right, which tells them which side I think a thing is on.*

*And I came back at the same speed I went, which is the speed of a boy
with nowhere to be, and I had just done a thing and been pleased with it,
and a boy who has just done a thing walks differently, and I made myself

*Act 1, month 8, the twenty-sixth, night. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten.
POV: Uzuki. ~1,710 words.*

---

There were four knives in that kitchen and she had known where all four of
them were since the third of the seventh month, because knowing where
things are in a house is a thing she had always done and had never once
asked herself about.

*The big one, on the board, out all the time.*

*The bread one, in the drawer, out every day.*

*The little one with the split handle that does everything, in the jar by
the window, and it is in that jar about nine times a day.*

*And the pig one.*

The pig one was in the back of the drawer under a cloth and it was about
seven inches and it was the only thing in that kitchen that anybody looked
after. It got oiled. It had a piece of leather over the point.

*It comes out for the pig, and the pig is the eleventh month, and it comes
out about a week before because he does it up in the shed and he does the
big one and that one at the same time on the block.*

*So it's the eleventh month.*

---

She stood in the kitchen in the dark with her hand on the drawer and did
the arithmetic before she opened it, because she was not going to do a
thing like that without doing the arithmetic.

*If I take the big one it's gone by tomorrow morning and there's a
conversation about it at breakfast.*

*If I take the bread one it's gone by tomorrow morning.*

*If I take the one out of the jar it's gone in about four hours, because
somebody wants that one nine times a day, and there is a version where I
put it back every morning and get it out every night, and I would forget,
and the morning I forgot would be the morning it mattered.*

*The pig one is the eleventh month.*

*So it's ten weeks.*

She stood with her hand on the drawer.

*And I have just given myself ten weeks.*

*I did not sit down and decide that. I worked out which knife would not be
missed and the answer to that question is a date, and now I have got a
date, and it is the eleventh month, and I did not choose it, a drawer chose
it.*

She thought about the flour bag behind the brick with 1,643 on it.

*Two numbers.*

*One thousand six hundred and forty-three, and about seventy.*

*And they are not about the same thing and they do not fit inside each
other and I have got both of them.*

She opened the drawer and took the pig knife and shut the drawer and put
the cloth back the way it had been, which took her about eleven seconds
and which she did with two hands and no noise at all.

---

She sat on the floor of her own room with it across her knees.

It was heavier than she had expected. That was the first thing and it was
the only thing about it that surprised her: she had picked it up perhaps
four times in her life, to move it, and she had never held it with the
intention of it being hers.

She took the leather off the point and put it back on.

*Right.*

*Say what it's for.*

---

*In case he —*

She got that far and stopped, and waited, and there was nothing behind the
word.

*Try again.*

*If he ever —*

*If he ever what.*

She sat with it on her knees in the dark for a long time.

*I don't know.*

*I do not know what this is for. I have taken a knife out of a kitchen and
I cannot finish the sentence, and I have tried twice, and both times it
goes as far as him and then it stops.*

*Because I don't know which one of us it's for.*

She looked at that for about a second and a half and then put it down
without examining it, the way she had put down the thing in the yard at
the wedding, and she was aware of doing it, and she did it anyway.

*And a thing you have got and cannot say what for is exactly the sort of
thing Dad would have taken off me.*

*He'd have had it out of my hand in about four seconds and he'd not have
been cross, he'd have said: what's it for. And I'd have said I don't know.
And he'd have said: then you haven't got it, you're just carrying it.*

She put the leather back on and held it in both hands.

*I'm carrying it.*

*All right. I'm carrying it.*

---

And then she found the thing she had actually come out to the kitchen
for, and it was not a knife at all, and it took the whole of the day to
arrive.

*It's a place to put it.*

*I couldn't get nine seconds into a column. There isn't a heading it goes
under and there is no list any more and it has been sitting in me all day
like something I swallowed.*

*And now it's in a knife.*

She sat on the floor with it.

*That's what it is. That's what it's for and I can say that one. It's for
the nine seconds. It is the nine seconds, made into a thing, so that it is
not in me any more.*

*And I can look at it.*

*Every night, if I want. I can put my hand under the mat and it will be
there and it will be exactly as true tomorrow as it was this morning, and
nobody can say I made it up and nobody can say it was a wall and a bucket,
because there is a knife under my bed and I put it there.*

**It has to go somewhere I can look at it.**

She put it under the mat, at the top, where her hand went when she lay on
her right side, which is how she slept.

Then she lay down on it and it was uncomfortable and she left it.

---

She got up again at some point and did the last thing, because it was the
twenty-sixth and she had done it every night since the second of the
seventh month and there was no reason on earth not to.

She knelt at the corner and put both palms flat on the brick.

*Little. Stop. Wait.*

*One.*

Nothing happened.

---

She waited, because you do wait. Getting hold of it is a push and it takes
a moment and some nights it takes three or four, and there were nights in
the fifth month when it had taken her nine, and she knew all of that and
was not going to be silly about it.

She held still with her hands on the brick and waited.

*Come on.*

*Find it. Hold it. Don't spend it.*

*Find it.*

There was nothing to find.

It was not that it was faint and it was not that it was slow. There was
nothing there at all, in the place where the thing was, the way a step
that is not there is not a shallow step.

*That's not — *

She took her hands off and put them back on.

*Try it properly. Both flat. Warm them first.*

She warmed them and put them back on the brick and held them there for a
long time, longer than she had ever held for a first one, and nothing came
up under them at all.

---

She sat back on her heels.

She was not frightened, which she noticed at the time and could not
account for. She had thought, in some part of her, that if this ever went
she would be frightened, and she was not; she was something else, and the
nearest she could get to it that night was *tired*, and it was not that
either.

*It's not gone.*

*It can't be gone. It was there on the eighteenth and it was there on the
twenty-third and I did a good one at Kuriya's this afternoon and he got up
in one and it was better than Tuesday.*

*So it's there. It was there four hours ago.*

She looked at the wall.

*It's there when there's somebody in front of me.*

She stayed on her heels for a bit.

*Right.*

*Then it's the wall.*

*The wall's never been the point. Anzai never gave me a wall. He gave me a
man's leg and a cup of water and I made the wall up myself because there
was nobody in the house to do it on and I had to do it on something.*

*So it's the wall, and I'll do it tomorrow, and if it's the wall tomorrow
as well then it's the wall.*

She counted the forty seconds anyway, out of habit, kneeling on the floor
with her hands in her lap.

There was nothing to check.

---

She got the flour bag out and put it on her knees and looked at the first
column.

She had written a number in it every night for a hundred and fifteen
nights. Twenty, twenty-two, nineteen, twenty-four, twenty-four, twenty-six,
and on and on down the bag, and *31 good* about a third of the way, and
twenty-nine on the night of the twentieth of the seventh month, and
twenty-eight, and twenty-nine again.

She sat with the pen over it.

She did not decide anything. She was not aware of making a decision and
she would have said, if anybody had asked her, that she was going to do it
in the morning.

She put the pen down and folded the bag and put it behind the brick and
put the brick back.

Then she lay down on her right side with her hand where it went, and there
was a knife under the mat, and she could feel the shape of it through the
matting, and it was uncomfortable.

She reached out with the other hand and put it flat on the wall.

It was cold, and it was brick, and there was nothing behind it.
