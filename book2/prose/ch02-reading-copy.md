# Chapter 2 — One Declared

*Book two. Act one, month 3. Nine scenes, 15,230 words.*



*Act 1, month 3. The road from Shioiri to Ōura. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten.
POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,790 words.*

---

They went up on the Tuesday with the mule and the tin and a bag of
Meno's flat cakes, which nobody wanted and everybody ate, and they were
past the head of the inlet before the light was properly up.

Tadayoshi had eleven of them in the tin.

Nineteen, if you counted the ones from the first four days, but he did
not count those, because the first four days had been him learning where
his own hand was and the work showed it. The eleven were the ones laid
after the fourth day, in the new frame, with the tapered fingers and the
dished palm, and he could pick every one of them out of four dozen by
eye at arm's length.

They had grain in them.

That was the thing he was carrying up the road like a stone in his boot.
They had grain in them because he had put grain in them, the way he
always did, laying the second course badly on purpose — and Akagane's
frame had spent nine weeks getting rid of exactly that, and the two
things had met in his hands and gone to war for four days, and the
eleven in the tin were what he had ended up with.

*They are not what he thinks they are.*

*He is going to ask me what the grain did. And what the grain did is that
I made it do the same thing it has always done, because I have a hand
that will not stop.*

---

"You're doing the face," said Iwao.

"I do not have a face."

"You've done it since the ferry stones. It's a small one and it goes on
for about a mile at a time." His brother walked backwards for a bit in
front of him, which he could do for a surprisingly long way. "Is it the
tin?"

"No."

"It's the tin."

"It is the road," said Tadayoshi. "I dislike the road."

"You dislike the road," Iwao agreed, and turned round, and let it go,
which was one of about four things about him that Tadayoshi had never
been able to account for. He always let it go. He never let anything go
because he had lost interest — he let it go the way you put a thing down
on a shelf you can reach.

---

Their father walked at the mule's head and said very little for the
first two hours, which was normal. Tadaharu on the road was a different
man from Tadaharu at the table: quieter, and he looked at things — at
fences, at what was in the fields, at how full the carts coming the other
way were. He had told Tadayoshi once that the road was the only place he
did any of his actual work, and that everything at the counter was just
the paying.

At about the ninth mile Iwao dropped back level with him and said,
"Da, I want to talk to you about the form."

*Here,* thought Tadayoshi.

He had been waiting for it since the ferry stones.

---

The form was a nomination.

Iwao was in his last year, and in his last year the Academy at Kiri put
about six names forward for a thing called early selection, which meant
you were seen by somebody from the corps at the winter trial instead of
waiting for the general graduation in the spring. Aoyagi did not teach
that year and did not have an opinion he was willing to give a
ten-year-old. What Tadayoshi knew was that Iwao had been in the top two
of that room since he was eleven, and that the top two did not always go
forward, and that the difference was a piece of paper with a parent's
name on it.

"No," said their father.

"You haven't heard it."

"I've heard it in my sleep. I heard it at breakfast on the ninth and I
heard it through a wall on the eleventh and I've heard it every day since
in one form or another, and the answer's your mother."

"The answer's my mother," Iwao agreed.

"So you know."

"I know."

And then he did not say anything for about a quarter of a mile, and
Tadayoshi, walking behind them with the bag of cakes, thought: *he has
just done something.*

---

*Record it.*

*He was told no. He agreed with the reason. Then he stopped talking, and
he stopped talking for four hundred yards, and he did it without going
sulky — he made a remark about the cart we passed and he laughed at Da's
answer and he was entirely pleasant, and the whole time he was not saying
the thing.*

*And now Da is the one carrying it.*

He watched the back of his father's head.

*He is going to bring it up himself.*

Four hundred yards later, his father said, "It's not that I think you'd
be bad at it."

"I know that."

"It's — " Tadaharu made a shape with one hand. "There's a difference
between a family that's got a boy at the Academy, which is nothing,
everybody's got a boy at the Academy, and a family that's got a *name*
at the winter trial. That's a list. You go on a list and then somebody
looks at where you're from."

"Yes," said Iwao.

"You agree with me."

"I agree with you completely. That's the whole point of it."

---

It took him two hours.

Tadayoshi walked eight feet behind and watched it happen and could not
afterwards have said how, and he tried — he tried the entire second half
of the day, and then again on the flat by the river, and then again in
bed at the inn.

Because he could do this. That was the thing. He could take a thing apart
and find the piece doing no work; he had done it to a five-seal sequence
in front of twenty-five people; he had done it to four hundred years of
copper. Watch a thing whole, then watch it again, then find the part you
can remove without the thing stopping.

He watched his brother for two hours and there was nothing to remove.

There was no sequence. That was what he kept arriving at and could not
make useful. Iwao did not have four moves he used in order. He said a
thing, and their father said a thing, and then Iwao said a *different*
thing — not the next one, a different one, built out of what their father
had just said — and it went on like that, thirty or forty exchanges, and
at no point did any of it look like a person trying to get something.

At one point Iwao argued the other side. He made their father's own case
better than their father had made it, and made it *warmly*, and their
father said, "Yes, exactly," and looked pleased, and Tadayoshi watched
his brother hand a man a stick and get handed it back.

At one point he made him laugh about Meno.

At one point — and this was the one Tadayoshi could not stop turning over
— he said, "You're not wrong about the list, though," and said nothing
after it, and their father filled the silence with the thing that beat
his own argument.

*He does not push. He arranges it so that the other person walks.*

*He has been doing that to me my entire life.*

---

They stopped for the flat cakes at the crossroads shrine and their father
signed it there on the step, with the pencil off the tin, on his knee.

"Your mother's going to skin me."

"I'll tell her."

"You will not tell her, you'll *manage* her, and I'll be standing behind
you nodding like an idiot, and we'll both know exactly what's going on
and she'll know as well." He held the paper out at arm's length and
looked at his own name on it with an expression Tadayoshi could not read
at all. "There. Winter trial."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. You didn't ask me for anything, that's the trick of
you." He folded it and gave it over. "You never do."

Iwao put it inside his shirt.

Then he sat down on the shrine step next to Tadayoshi and took a flat
cake off him without asking, and said, "Right, small. What's in the tin
that you're not saying?"

---

*Do not.*

"Eleven of mine are laid in the new frame."

"Akagane's thing."

"Akagane's thing." He watched a beetle on the step. "He says the grain
will change, and that Kanaya will notice about the third dozen and put
the grade up without saying why."

"And will it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

*Because I put the grain in by hand, and a frame cannot take out what a
hand puts in, and I have spent four days making sure of it.*

"Because it is a fine adjustment," said Tadayoshi, "and there are other
variables."

Iwao ate his flat cake.

"You know what I like about you," he said, "is you're a terrible liar
and you do it anyway. Most people who can't lie stop trying." He got up
and dusted the back of his trousers. "It's very brave. It's like watching
a man with no legs enter a race."

"I did not lie."

"No," his brother agreed. "You didn't, did you." And he went and took the
mule's head off their father, cheerfully, so the old man could walk with
his hands free for the last stretch.

---

They came over the top above Ōura at about four with the light going long
behind them.

It was the biggest place Tadayoshi had ever been, which he was aware said
more about him than about Ōura. A river, and a bridge with a customs post
nobody manned, and two hundred and some roofs going down to the water in
no order whatsoever, and smoke, and a road through the middle with
things on both sides of it.

He stopped for a second at the top, the way he had stopped at the top
every time since he was six.

*Six terraces at home. Eight people.*

*I can see about forty houses from here without moving my head, and I
know what is in one of them, and it is a counter with a man behind it who
will make a face at my brother's father over a bead.*

"Come on, small," said Iwao, from ten yards down. "You can count them
tomorrow."

Tadayoshi came on.

*I have already counted them,* he thought, and did not say, because there
was nothing in it for anybody. *There are forty-one.*

*Act 1, month 3. Nakadō, Tazu's shed. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki.
~1,620 words.*

---

"I've got four things," said Uzuki.

Tazu did not look up. He was doing something to a tray of small dry
somethings with the back of a spoon, and the shed smelled the way it
always smelled, which was of about nine plants at once and one of them
gone off.

"Four."

"Four things I want to ask you and they're all about the same thing and
if you tell me to go home I'll come back tomorrow."

"You'll come back tomorrow anyway," said Tazu. "Shut the door, you're
letting the wet in."

She shut the door.

---

She had spent eleven days getting the four things into an order.

That was the part nobody would have believed if she had told them, and
she had not told them, because there was nobody in that house she could
have said it to without it turning into a conversation about not getting
her hopes up. Eleven days. She had had them in a different order four
times. She had had six things at one point and then worked out that two
of them were the same thing wearing a hat.

"One," said Uzuki. "When you said a medic could teach it. Did you mean
that, or was it a thing to say on the way out of a house."

Tazu put the spoon down.

He looked at her for a moment with an expression she had not seen on him
before — not surprise exactly; more the look of a man who has been handed
a tool he did not know was in the room.

"That's a very rude question."

"Yes."

"It's also a good one." He wiped his hands on the front of himself.
"Sit on the box. Not that box, the other one, that one's got something
in it."

She sat on the other box.

---

"I meant it," said Tazu. "And it was a thing to say on the way out of a
house. Both. That's how most true things get said, in my experience —
sideways, at the door, when the paying part's over."

"So it can be taught."

"The palm work can be taught. Some of it. To some people."

"Which people?"

"People with control." He sat down on the stool. "Now that's the bit
everybody gets wrong and I'm going to be careful about it, because I've
watched a lot of people be got wrong about it. It isn't strength, it
isn't a bloodline, and it isn't being clever. It's whether you can put a
small amount of something exactly where you meant to and stop when you
said you'd stop."

Uzuki thought about the eighth count of the third form.

She thought about *you're paying before you've decided*, and about a
year of going hard, and about the eighteenth try, which had felt like
nothing at all.

"Say the second thing," said Tazu.

---

"Two. How long."

"An afternoon for the bones of it. A year for it to be worth anything.
Ten to be a medic, and you won't be a medic, so put that one down."

"I'm not trying to be a medic."

"No," said Tazu. "You're not, are you."

"Three." She had her hands flat on her knees. "What would I be able to
do. After the afternoon. Exactly."

---

He took his time over that one.

He got up and went to the shelf and moved two jars that did not need
moving, and came back, and she let him do it, because she had learned
that from her father: when a person is going to give you the true
version, they usually walk about first.

"You'd be able to take the edge off," said Tazu. "That's the honest
answer and it's smaller than you want. A hand on a chest, and the tight
part goes slack for a bit, and the coughing that's got hold and won't let
go — that stops, sometimes, for an hour or two, because half of that is
the muscle panicking and you can talk muscle out of things.

"You'd be able to do the nights. That's not nothing, girl, don't let your
face do that. The nights are where it gets won and lost. A man who sleeps
four hours is a man with a chance and a man who sleeps none isn't."

"And the thing itself."

"No."

"Not at all?"

"Not at all," said Tazu. "Not in an afternoon and not in ten years and
not with the best of them standing over you. What's in your father is in
the deep part and it has a shape, and shifting a shape is a different
trade from taking the edge off, and I'd be lying to you if I said the
first one was a small version of the second."

Uzuki sat on the box with her hands flat on her knees.

"All right," she said.

"You're not crying about it."

"I did that on the ninth."

---

"Four," she said. "This is the actual one."

"I know. I've been waiting for it."

"Kayano's three days. A medic at Kayano is a person with a house and a
door and other people wanting them." She had gone through this at night
about forty times and it came out of her flat and fast. "Why would one
of them sit down for an afternoon with somebody who can't pay them?"

Tazu laughed.

It was not a kind laugh and it was not an unkind one; it was the noise a
man makes when a child asks the question the adults did not.

"Because it's quicker," he said.

---

"Quicker than what?"

"Than doing it themselves." He leaned forward with his elbows on his
knees. "Listen. A medic worth the name is the busiest person in whatever
town they're standing in. Everybody wants them and there's one of them.
And most of what comes through that door is not interesting — it's
nights, and edges, and old men's chests, and a hand on it for twenty
minutes at a time, three times a week, for years.

"Now. You come to that door and you say *my father's ill, come with me,
it's three days*, and what they hear is: six days of walking, a fee I
won't get, and a case I can't fix. And they say no, and they're right to.

"But you come to that door and you say *teach me the palm for an
afternoon and I'll take it three days away and you'll never see either of
us again* — " He sat back. "That's a good trade. That's a good trade for
*them*. They spend an afternoon and they get a chest off their list
permanently."

"That's — " Uzuki stopped.

"Go on."

"That's not asking for help. That's — "

"That's selling them something," said Tazu. "Yes. Which is why you'll get
it, and why your mother wouldn't, because your mother would go and ask
nicely and take no for an answer, and you're going to walk in there and
tell a busy person how to have a shorter week."

---

She sat on the box for a bit and did not say anything.

"Now say the fifth thing," said Tazu.

"There's only four."

"There's five. There's always five. Go on."

She looked at the floor of the shed.

"Would it have worked," said Uzuki. "If we'd done it last year."

Tazu was quiet.

"That's the one I'm not answering," he said. "Not because I'm being
delicate with you. Because I don't know, and a man who tells a child
*yes* to that question has just handed her something she'll carry
around for forty years, and I've seen it done and I won't."

"But you think — "

"I think you should ask me things I can answer," said Tazu, "and you're
extremely good at it, and that one isn't one."

---

He got up and went round the bench and got something out of the drawer.

It was a stub of pencil and a piece of paper about the size of his palm,
and he squared it up on the bench with two fingers, and wrote something
on it, and looked at it.

Uzuki watched him.

He looked at it for about as long as it had taken him to write it. Then
he folded it once, and once again, and put it in his shirt pocket, and
buttoned the pocket.

"Right," he said. "Go on, out. I've got this to finish before the light
goes."

She got up off the box.

At the door she stopped, with her hand on it.

"Was that a name?"

"Out," said Tazu.

---

She went home the long way, past the ditch and round by the field, which
took nine minutes longer and gave her nine minutes.

*He wrote it and he didn't give it to me.*

*Which means he's got a name. Which means he knows somebody, or knows
somebody who knows.*

*And he isn't going to, because a bit of paper off Tazu makes it a thing
Tazu sent a child to do, and he's got to live here afterwards, and he's
the one they send for.*

She kicked a stone along the path in front of her for a while.

*So I'll go without one.*

*You don't need a name for a door. You need a door.*

She got to the gate and stopped and looked at the house, which had a
light in the back room and no light in the front, which meant her mother
was at the table with the box out, at the eleventh of the month, again.

*Four things,* Uzuki thought.

*And now I know what I'm selling.*

*Act 1, month 3. Kanaya's counter, Ōura. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV:
Tadayoshi. ~1,990 words.*

---

Kanaya's counter was three doors up from the bridge and had a bell on a
strap that hit the door every time it opened, which Tadayoshi had decided
years ago was not for the shop's benefit but for the customer's: it told
you, before you were properly inside, that you had been noticed.

"Right," said their father, on the step. "Faces in."

"I have no face," said Tadayoshi.

"You've got a *terrible* face, you look like a bailiff. Iwao — "

"Da."

"— don't."

"Don't what."

"You know what."

Iwao did about a quarter of it. Just the mouth, and something with the
eyebrows, for less than a second, and Tadayoshi had to look at the
bridge.

Then the bell hit the door and they went in.

---

Kenji had run that counter for eleven years and was, by Tadayoshi's
estimate, about forty-five, and he had one of those faces that has
settled into its most-used expression: a downward, patient, slightly
pained set, the face of a man who has spent his working life being shown
things that are not as good as the person showing them thinks.

He had the tin open before the door had finished shutting.

"Ruri-san."

"Kenji-san."

"Four dozen?"

"Four dozen and three."

"Mm," said Kenji, in a tone that suggested the three were going to be a
problem, and tipped the tin onto the black cloth.

---

The grading went the way it always went for the first nine minutes.

He did them in tens, sliding them across the cloth with the edge of a
bone spatula, and he did not pick any of them up. That was the thing
about Kanaya's counter. Tadayoshi had watched four other buyers in Ōura
in his life, and every one of them had a glass on a chain and used it,
and Kenji had a glass on a chain and did not.

"Two. Two. Two. Low three, that's a nice one, don't get excited. Two.
Two — "

"Kenji-san," said their father, "you've done thirty in four minutes."

"I've done thirty in four minutes because thirty of them are twos." The
spatula went on sliding. "Two. Two. Two."

He got to the end.

"Two," said Kenji, "and a low three on eleven, and I'll give you thirteen
for the lot."

---

"Thirteen," said Tadaharu.

"Thirteen."

"It was fourteen and a half last month for one less."

"It was."

"And the work's not worse."

"The work is exactly the same," said Kenji, "which is my entire point,
and the price is thirteen, and I'll tell you why if you want to stand
here and be told why, and you won't enjoy it."

"Go on."

"Sunaba's not moving." He put the spatula down. "There's a house in
Sunaba sitting on eleven hundred pieces of gemstone-grade because the
southern boats stopped in the ninth month and haven't started, and until
that house shifts what it's got, nobody up the line is buying, and I am
buying off you today entirely out of habit and a certain regard for your
mule." He looked up for the first time. "Thirteen. It's not the drops.
It's not you. It's a boat."

*He is telling the truth,* thought Tadayoshi.

*And he is enjoying it slightly, because he has been carrying that for
about a month and this is the fourth time this week he has got to say it
out loud to somebody who did not know.*

---

Their father put both hands on the counter.

Tadayoshi could see the next ten minutes from where he stood. His father
would argue, and he would argue *well*, because he had walked that road
four hundred times and knew the trade — and it would not matter, because
Kenji was not wrong about the boat, and you cannot argue a man out of a
boat.

They would take thirteen. They would take thirteen and walk home and put
thirteen on the table and Hisui would say the number back at Meno.

"Kenji-san," said Iwao, "can I ask you something that's nothing to do
with this?"

---

Tadayoshi turned his head.

"What," said Kenji.

"How do you do it without the glass?"

There was a pause of about a second.

"What?"

"You've got a glass. It's round your neck. You've had it round your neck
every time we've come in here since I was about seven and I've never once
seen you put it on." Iwao leaned on the counter — not familiarly, on his
forearms, the way you lean when you are settling in to listen. "My da's
been coming here eleven years and I've been coming here five and I've
been trying to work it out since I was nine and I can't, so I'm asking."

Kenji looked at him.

"Because I don't need it," he said.

"Right, but *how*."

"Because I've been doing it for — "

"No, no, everyone says that." Iwao waved it away, cheerfully, the way you
wave away a thing you have heard four hundred people say. "Everyone in
every trade says *because I've been doing it thirty years*, and it's not
an answer, it's a way of not being asked again. My great-aunt says it
about tea. What are you actually *looking* at?"

---

Tadayoshi stood eight feet back with the empty tin in his hands and
watched a man's whole face come off its hinges.

It took about ninety seconds.

"The light off the taper," said Kenji.

"Right — "

"You don't look *into* them, that's the mistake, that's what the glass is
for and the glass is for people who look into them. You look at what
comes *off*. You put them on black and you slide them and you watch the
line go up the side, and a two goes — " and he did something with his
hand, a small horizontal wobble — "and a three goes — " a smoother one —
"and you're not looking at the stone at all, you're looking at what the
stone does to a line."

"That's — " Iwao said, and stopped, and laughed. "That's *cheating*."

"That's forty years."

"That's cheating and it's forty years. Show me."

"Show you."

"Do one. Do one and tell me what it is before you've — "

"I've done four dozen and three," said Kenji, "in eleven minutes, and
you were standing there."

"I wasn't watching."

---

And Kenji-of-the-counter, who had a boat problem and a house in Sunaba sitting
on eleven hundred pieces and thirteen thousand ryō on the cloth in front of
him, picked up his spatula and did four dozen and three again for a
thirteen-year-old.

He did them in fives this time and named them as they went.

"Two. Two. Two. Low three — "

He stopped.

He went back a piece.

He slid it under the window light and moved it about half an inch,
twice, and put his head on one side, and did it again.

Then he picked it up.

---

*He has picked one up,* thought Tadayoshi.

*In five years I have never seen him pick one up.*

He knew which one it was before Kenji turned it. He could have named it
across a room. It was the fourth of the eleven, laid on the sixth day
after the new frame came in, the one where he had got the tiredness in
the taper nearly right and had known, putting it down, that it was the
best bad drop he had ever made.

"Where's this from," said Kenji.

"Same place as the other forty-two," said Tadaharu.

"It's not the same." He had the glass off his neck and on his eye and did
not appear to have noticed doing it. "The grain's — the line goes *up*
it. It doesn't step. Look — " and he held it out at Iwao, who looked at
it with an expression of total incomprehension and enormous interest,
which was, Tadayoshi thought, an extraordinary thing to be able to do
with a face.

"I can't see it."

"Well, no. Where are the rest of them?"

"The rest of what?"

"There'll be more than one." Kenji was already going back down the cloth
with the spatula, fast, and he was doing it with the glass on. "You don't
get one. You get a run. Something's changed at your end — a new
sand, a different — "

"Frames," said Iwao.

Tadayoshi did not move at all.

"My cousin makes the frames," said Iwao. "He's been fiddling with them
all winter. Drives everyone mad."

"Frames," said Kenji, in the voice of a man having a private thought in
public. "Frames. Well. There's eleven of them and they're threes, and two
of these are the top of a three, and — " He straightened up and pulled the
glass off. "Fifteen."

"Fifteen," said Tadaharu.

"Fifteen, and tell your cousin to keep fiddling, and if he does the whole
lot in that frame next month I'll take four dozen at three and not blink
at it." He was already writing. "And don't take that to Marukiya, they'd
have your arm off."

---

Their father put the money in the bag and thanked him and said something
about the boat, and Kenji said something back about the boat, and
Tadayoshi stood eight feet away and watched a man write a number in a
column.

*He has written it down.*

*He has written down: Ruri, four dozen and three, and a figure that is
two and a half more than last month for two more pieces.*

*And it is not true. It is not the frame and it is not a new sand and it
is not Akagane. It is eleven drops that came out of my hands with four
days of my own work in them to make them worse, and the work failed, and
because it failed we are going home with fifteen.*

*I have just been paid for not being good enough at hiding.*

He stood in the shop with the empty tin and could not get the shape of
that to sit down anywhere.

---

Outside, on the bridge, Iwao gave him the tin to carry and stretched.

"Well," he said. "That went nicely."

"How did you do that?"

"Do what?"

"The glass," said Tadayoshi. "You asked him about the glass."

"I've wanted to know about the glass for four years." His brother looked
genuinely puzzled. "Haven't you? He's had it round his neck the whole
time. It's *mad*."

"You asked him about the glass and he put the glass on and found the
eleven and went to fifteen."

"Did he?" said Iwao. "Huh."

And Tadayoshi understood, standing on the bridge at Ōura with an empty
tin in his hands, that his brother was not lying — that there had been no
sequence, and no plan, and nothing to take apart, because there had never
been anything there to take apart in the first place. Iwao had wanted to
know about the glass. That was all of it. That was the whole mechanism.

*He simply likes people.*

*And it works better than anything I have ever built.*

---

Their father went to see a man about a strap, and Iwao took two of the
fifteen and disappeared for eleven minutes and came back with a thing
wrapped in paper.

"What's that?"

---

"Now here's the part I actually want to argue," he said, "and it's not
about the road, so you'll have to let me get to the end of it.

"Everything she said is right and it's all about the going. And I'd take
the other side of every one of them and lose. So I'm not going to."

"Then — "

"I'm going to talk about the coming back."

He put his hand flat on the blanket.

"It's going to get worse and there's going to be a stretch of it. Might
be a long stretch. And in that stretch there is going to be a great deal
of sitting in this room with me at three in the morning, and it is going
to fall on the pair of you, and there is nothing on this earth I can do
about that except be sorry about it, which is worth nothing.

"And I would rather — " and here his voice went, for the first time, and
he took it back — "I would rather she came into that room with a pair of
hands that could do something.

"That's it. That's the whole of my side. Not because it'll fix me. It
won't. Because there is a difference between a child who sat in the dark
for a year being no use, and a child who sat in the dark for a year and
was *doing something*, and that difference doesn't finish when I do. She
carries that one for sixty years."

---

Her mother said, "And if it goes bad while she's gone."

"Then it goes bad while she's gone."

"Kiyoshi — "

"Then it goes bad while she's gone," he said, "and she'll have been doing
something, and that's the best answer I've got and I know exactly how bad
it is."

Uzuki sat on the floor by the end of the bed and looked at the boards.

"There isn't going to be a Kayano," said her mother.

"What?"

"There isn't going to be a Kayano," she said again, and stopped, and
started something else. "It doesn't — the money doesn't — "

"She's not asking for money. That's the whole of her plan, that's the
clever bit, she's not — "

"I know what her plan is." Her mother's voice came out sharp and then
came back down. "I heard her plan. Her plan is better than anything I've
had."

*She's saying it again,* thought Uzuki. *She said that in the kitchen.
She said it's a good plan and then said no in four seconds.*

*And she's just started a sentence about money twice and stopped it
twice.*

---

"Sae," said her father, gently. "What is it."

Her mother sat with her hands in her lap.

"Nothing," she said. "It's nothing. It's the road."

And Uzuki, who was ten, and who had spent eleven days being furious with
the wrong parent, took that entirely at face value.

---

It went on a bit after that but the shape of it was over.

Her mother said seven days and her father said nine and they settled on
nine. Her mother said she would not go past Kayano for anything and
Uzuki said she would not go past Kayano for anything. Her mother said if
the road at the wood was wet she was to wait for a cart and go with it
and Uzuki said yes, and meant it, and was told she did not mean it, and
said yes again.

Her father said, "And you take the mill's two parcels and you get the
sixty up front, and you ask for it up front in front of somebody else so
he can't go back on it."

"That's what I was going to do."

"I know it is," he said. "I'm saying it so your mother can hear that
you'd thought of it."

Her mother made a noise that was not quite anything.

"Nine days," she said. "And if you're eleven I'm coming to get you."

"You wouldn't get four miles," said her father.

"I'd get to Ōgi," said her mother, "and I'd stand in the road at Ōgi and
shout, and it would be extremely undignified and I would do it, so don't
be eleven days."

---

Uzuki went out into the passage afterwards with her ears ringing.

She stood in the dark bit by the rack for a moment, the way she had stood
there twelve days before, and then she reached up and took her bokken off
the bottom peg, because she was going to be on a road for nine days and
there were going to be evenings, and she had a form with fourteen counts
in it and the eighth one was still not reliable.

She stood there with it in her hand.

The rack had two pegs.

Hers was empty now and her father's was not, because his was still up
there where it had been every evening of her life, on the top, put back
by him, and it had not come down since the afternoon on the flat bit by
the barrow when she had got the eighth count on the eighteenth try and he
had sat down and had not got up.

She looked at that for a while in the dark.

Then she took hers upstairs, and did not say anything to anybody about
it, and put it under the bed where she could reach it.

*Act 1, month 3. Shioiri, the kitchen. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV:
Tadayoshi. ~2,090 words.*

---

He got his afternoon on the twenty-third.

Meno was asleep. His father was at Ōura with the mule about a strap that
had been about a strap for three weeks. Hisui had taken Koishi up to the
fourth course to look for a cat that did not exist and was not going to
be back for an hour. Akagane was in the shed and would not come out of
the shed for anything short of the sea coming in.

His mother was at the table with the comb.

She had had it out every day since Iwao gave it to her, and she did not use it
at the table, she *looked* at it — turned it over, put it down, picked it up
an hour later — the way somebody handles a thing they have not yet decided how
to feel about. It was tortoiseshell and it had cost two thousand ryō of the
extra and it was, by a distance, the nicest object in the house.

He put the ledger on the table in front of her.

"Would you read that."

---

She looked at it, and then at him.

"All of it?"

"The last four pages. It is a proposal. It is written as a proposal
because I could not think of another way to make you sit through the
whole of it before you answered."

"Ah," said his mother.

"That is not — "

"No, it's fair." She put the comb down. "Go on then. Sit down, don't
stand over me, you're your great-aunt's boy."

He sat down.

---

She read it.

That was the first thing that went wrong, and he had not seen it coming
at all: she read it *properly*. He had prepared for her to look at a
page and hand it back. He had prepared for *not now*. He had prepared —
he had specifically prepared, at about two in the morning, four days ago
— for her to be angry that it was written down.

She turned the lamp round so it came over her shoulder and she read the
four passes in order, with a finger, and at the sums she stopped and did
them herself in her head, and he watched her do it because you could see
her do it, and at one of them she went back up a line and did it again.

It took her eleven minutes.

At the end she went back and read the narrow column.

Then she put her hand flat on the page, the way Meno did, and left it
there.

"That's good," she said.

"It is not — "

"It's *good*, Tadayoshi. Don't do that thing where you get told a true
thing and argue with it. The sums are right, the Kanaya line is worth
more than you've put on it, and the bit about Akagane and me is — " she
moved her hand and looked at it again. "I didn't know that. About the
frame. About what it's for."

"Nobody knows that. He does not know it, he has half of it, he thinks the
fingers run ahead — "

"They do run ahead."

"They run ahead *because the frame cannot do the job at all*," said
Tadayoshi, "and he has made a better version of a thing that should not
be necessary, and he is going to keep making them for the rest of his
life, and — "

He stopped, because his voice had gone somewhere he had not authorised.

His mother did not say anything about that. She let it go past, which he
was grateful for and did not say so.

"You've ruled a fourth column," she said.

"Yes."

"Narrow."

"That is how it is done."

"That's how *she* does it," said his mother, and something went over her
face and away again. "All right. Do you want the answer, or do you want
me to say I'll think about it?"

"The answer."

"You're sure."

"I did not spend eleven days carrying this about in my shirt to be told
you will think about it."

"No," she said. "No, you didn't."

---

"You're not declaring," said his mother. "You're confessing."

---

The fire went. Somebody's shutter, down on the second course.

"Say that again."

"Your case is: be worth something openly, on a day of our own choosing,
in a way that's boring. That's the whole of it and it's a good case." She
turned the ledger round so it faced him. "And there is no day of our own
choosing. There hasn't been one since you were eight."

"Why."

"Because a child of this house gets presented in the year they turn
eight." She said it perfectly evenly. "Assessed. Written down. Yes or no,
and it goes in the column. Hisui was. Akagane was. Iwao was — you don't
remember, you were three. Koishi goes in two years and Hisui has already
started dreading it and you'll see it on her when it comes.

"You weren't."

Tadayoshi sat with his hands flat on the table.

"So when you talk about declaring," said his mother, "what you are
describing is walking up to a door and saying *there is a producer in my
house and there has been for two years and we did not present him*. And
that is not a declaration, and it will not be boring, and it will not go
on an old list. It's an admission. And the first thing anybody who hears
it will want to know is not what you can do.

"It's what else we haven't said."

---

He looked for the hole in it.

He looked for it the way he had looked for the hole in his own case, and
he found it in about four seconds, and it was not a hole, it was the
place where his case had been standing.

*I built the whole of it on a day of our own choosing.*

*Every line after pass two assumes we get to pick the day.*

*And we do not, and we have not since the eighth month of the year I was
eight, and I did not know that, and I have had eleven days and four
passes and I did not know the thing the entire argument stands on.*

*And she has known it for two years.*

"You knew," he said.

"Yes."

"When you closed your hand over the drop and said *nobody sees this*.
You had already — "

"I'd already not presented you," said his mother. "It was the eighth
month. You'd have gone in the eighth month. I'd had three months of not
doing it before you ever came up those stairs with that thing in your
fist."

---

Tadayoshi got up and stood by the stove because he could not sit down any
more.

"Then it is worse," he said. "Do you understand that it is *worse*? Every
month it is worse. If it was three months in the eighth month it is
twenty-seven months now and it will be thirty-nine next year and there is
no version of this where the number goes down — "

"I know what the number does."

"Then why."

She did not answer.

"Why," said Tadayoshi. "Not the rule. I know the rule, I have had the
rule for two years, I have kept it every working day and I have never once
asked you for anything except this. Why."

---

His mother sat at the table with her hand on the ledger.

"They came for him in the daytime," she said.

"Kongō."

"They came in the daytime. That's the thing nobody says about it. I've
had — " she stopped and started again. "Everybody thinks a thing like that
happens at night. It didn't. It was the middle of the afternoon in the
eighth month and the whole compound was out, all forty of us, on the
paths, at the doors, on the second course and the third course, watching.

"It took about twenty minutes. There was no shouting. He put his tools
down and went and got his coat, and my mother's brother said something to
one of them and got an answer, and then they all went down the hill, and
there was a boat, and by the evening the fire was lit and we ate.

"And every single person on that hill was somebody who could have done
something. Forty of us."

She turned the comb over on the table.

"And I have never in my life been able to work out whether we could have.
Not once. Not in fifty years. I've had it at three in the morning about a
hundred times and it does not come out."

---

He waited.

He waited a long time, longer than he had ever waited with Meno, because
he understood that he was being given something and he did not yet know
how big it was.

"That is not a reason," he said at last.

"No."

"It is a thing that happened."

"Yes."

"You have given me a thing that happened and I asked you for a reason and
you know they are different, and — "

"I know they're different," said his mother.

"Then give me the reason."

"I haven't got one," she said. "I've got that."

---

She said it without any drama in it whatsoever, and that was what took
his legs out.

"I've got a hill with forty people on it and one man walking down it, and
I have had it for fifty years, and every time anybody in this family says
*we should* about anything at all, that is what I have got instead of an
argument. And it isn't good enough. I know it isn't good enough. Your
father's known it isn't good enough for eighteen years and he's too kind
to say it in front of Meno.

"And it is still what I've got, and you're ten, and the answer is no."

---

There was nothing left after that.

He knew there was nothing left because he went looking, properly, the way
he did, and every route he tried came back to a hill in the eighth month
with forty people standing on it.

He picked the ledger up off the table.

"May I ask one more thing."

"Yes."

"When Koishi is presented," said Tadayoshi. "In two years. Will you take
her?"

His mother looked at him for a moment.

"Yes," she said.

"Because if you do not, then Hisui goes on the same list I am on, and
Koishi goes on it, and — "

"Yes," said his mother. "I'll take her. Hisui's taking her. It's already
been said. That was said the week she was born."

"Then you and I are the only two people in this house who are not — "

"That's right," said his mother.

---

He stood there with the ledger.

"Your hands are freezing," she said.

"It is the second month."

"It's the third month, and they've been like that since the tenth, and
you sit out in that shed till the lamp goes because you think nobody
knows." She reached out and took both of them and turned them over,
briskly, without any ceremony at all, the way she would have looked at a
mount — and stopped, at the knuckles, where the cracks were.

"Ah," she said. "Tadayoshi."

"They close in the fourth month."

"They didn't close last year till the fifth."

"They closed in the fourth."

"They did *not*," said his mother, and got up and went to the shelf and
got down the flat tin with the grease in it that had been on that shelf
since before he was born, and came back, and took his right hand, and did
the knuckles one at a time with her thumb, hard, the way you would work
it into a strap.

It hurt. He let it hurt.

Neither of them said anything for about two minutes.

Then she did the left one, and put the lid back on the tin, and put the
tin on the table next to the comb, and said, "Take that out to the shed
and *use* it," and picked her comb back up and turned it over.

He took the tin and the ledger out to the shed.

He did nine drops that evening in the new frame, and every one of them
had grain in it, and the grease made the seals slip for the first four
and after that it did not.

*Act 1, month 3, the twenty-sixth. Nakadō, and the Ōgi road. Nozaki
Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,400 words.*

---

She had the bag packed for two days before she went, and unpacked it
twice, and it came out the same both times.

Bread and the hard cheese, wrapped. The waterskin. Her father's small
knife, which he had put on the table without saying anything about it,
and which she had taken without saying anything about it. The good coat.
Two pairs of the thick socks and no third pair, because a third pair was
weight and she had worked out that a wet pair dries against you at night
if you are sensible about it.

Her bokken, along the bottom of the cart, under the sacking.

And the mill's two parcels for Kayano, and the sixty in the small purse,
counted out in front of the miller's wife on the Thursday, which she had
asked for in exactly the way her father said and got without an argument.

She did not take the comb her mother had put out.

She looked at it for a while and then put it back on the shelf, because
a comb was for somebody who was going somewhere, and she was going to
work.

---

She got up at four.

The house at four was a different house. She had known that in a general
way and had never had a reason to find it out properly: the stairs made
noises in places they did not make noises in the daytime, and the room at
the bottom was full of the smell of a fire that had gone out, and
everything in it was a shape you had to identify.

She had her boots in her hand.

Her mother was sitting at the table in the dark.

---

Neither of them said anything for a moment.

There was no lamp lit. Her mother had her hands round a cup that had
nothing in it and had, by the look of her, been sitting there for some
time.

"Ma."

"Boots on outside," said her mother. "You'll wake him."

"I was going to."

"I know you were."

Uzuki stood in the middle of the room with her boots in her hand.

"I was going to leave a — I did leave a — it's on the — "

"I've read it."

"Oh."

"It's a good one," said her mother. "You spelled Kayano wrong."

"Did I?"

"There's no *u* in it."

"There is when I write it," said Uzuki.

Her mother made a small noise that in the daytime would have been a
laugh.

---

She got up and went to the stove.

Uzuki watched her do it and could not see what she was doing, because the
stove was the darkest corner of the room and her mother's back was in the
way, and there was the sound of the tin being moved and put down again.

Then her mother came back.

She took Uzuki's hand, and turned it over, and put something into the
palm of it and closed the fingers over.

It was a twist of cloth.

---

*It's the hundred.*

She knew it by the shape and the weight before she knew it by anything
else. She had carried it eleven miles in a cart in the second month with
her hand going to her pocket about every quarter of a mile to check it
was there.

*She's had it behind the tin since the Thursday and now she's giving it
to me.*

"Ma — "

"Don't."

"I said I wasn't going to need money, that's the whole — the whole thing
is that I'm not asking anybody for — "

"I know what the whole thing is."

"Then why are you — "

"Because you'll need boots," said her mother.

---

Uzuki stood in the dark with her fingers closed round it.

"I've got boots."

"You've got boots that'll do six days and you're doing nine, and there's
a wood on that road, and if you come out of that wood with a boot gone
you're a child in a town four days from home with no boot." Her mother's
voice was perfectly steady and did not go anywhere near the middle of
anything. "So that's not for the medic and it's not for food and I don't
want to hear about it again. That's for if something happens. And if
nothing happens you bring it back and put it where it was."

"All right."

"Say it back."

"It's for if something happens," said Uzuki, "and I bring it back."

"Good."

---

She did not ask where it had come from.

She thought about it — she thought about it for about a second and a half
at the door with her boots still in her hand — and she did not ask,
because she already knew where it had come from, and because her mother
had not said, and because there was something in the not-saying that she
did not want to open at four in the morning with a cart to get to Ōgi.

*She kept it since the second month.*

*She kept it and she wouldn't spend it on the roof and now she's given it
to me for boots.*

She got that far and stopped, and put the twist of cloth in the inside
pocket and buttoned it, and did not go any further, and it would be a
long time before she did.

---

Her mother did not come out.

She stood in the doorway with her arms folded while Uzuki put her boots
on on the step, and she said, "Nine days," and Uzuki said, "Nine days,"
and that was the whole of the goodbye, because her mother did not do the
other kind and never had and Uzuki would not have known what to do with
one.

At the gate she looked back once.

Her mother had gone in and shut the door.

There was a light on in the back room, which meant somebody had lit the
lamp, which meant her father was awake and had been awake and had let the
pair of them do it without him, because he had known perfectly well that
neither of them could have managed it with him in the room.

Uzuki looked at that for a second.

Then she put her shoulder into the cart handle and went.

---

It was about three quarters of an hour to the fork and it was still dark
when she got there, and she stopped at the milestone the way her father
stopped at the milestone, and did the thing he did, which was to walk
round the cart once and count the load out loud.

"Parcels, two."

"Purse."

"Bag."

"Skin."

"Sacking."

She got to the end and it came out six, and six was right, and she had
had six in her head since Tuesday.

Then she put her hand flat against her coat, over the inside pocket,
which was a thing she had done about four times since the gate without
noticing she was doing it.

*Seven.*

---

She stood at the fork in the dark with her hand on her coat.

The Ōgi road went off to the left, which she had done eleven times and
could have done asleep, and the north road went straight on past the
milestone into a country she had never been in, and there was a smell off
it of wet trees.

*Six things I'm carrying, and one I'm not supposed to need.*

*Four days that way. An afternoon in the middle. Four days back.*

*Nine, and she said if I'm eleven she'll stand in the road at Ōgi and
shout.*

She took her hand off her coat and put it back on the handle.

Somewhere behind her, a long way off, a cart was coming up out of Nakadō
with a lamp on it, and she thought about waiting for it, and worked out
that waiting for it would cost her half an hour and she would only pass
it again before the wood.

So she did not wait for it.

She went on past the milestone in the dark, with the cart making the
noise a cart makes, and she was over the first rise before it got light
and she did not look back from up there, because she had already looked
back once at the gate and you only get the one.
