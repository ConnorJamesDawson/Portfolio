# Chapter 1, Scene 6 — The Book

*Act 1, month 2. Shioiri, Meno's room on the third course. Tadayoshi
Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~2,020 words.*

---

"Thirty-two people," he said.

Meno did not look up. She was doing the thing she did in the afternoons,
which was to take the tea things off the tray one at a time with both
hands and put them back on it one at a time with both hands, and which
had been explained to him once, years ago, as *keeping them going*, and
which he had understood at the time to mean the cups.

"Hisui said thirty-two people were from here," he said. "At the table.
She said *this is where thirty-two people were from*."

"She did."

"Where did they go?"

"Sit down, you're standing over me."

He sat down.

---

Ruri Meno was seventy-one and her hands had stopped about twenty years
ago. It had not been a single event; it had come on the way the tide
comes on, a little further up the beach each year, until one winter she
had put down a mount she was setting and had not, in the end, picked it
up again. Nobody in the compound had ever said the word *stopped* out
loud in her hearing. They said she kept the books now. She did keep the
books.

Her knuckles were the size of the last joint of his thumb.

"Thirty-two people," she said, "is the difference between two numbers,
and Hisui says it at that table the way a woman throws a plate. She
doesn't know who they were. She knows there were forty of us and there
are eight of us and she can subtract."

"How many can you name?"

"All of them."

"Then name them."

"No," said Meno.

---

He waited. He had learned, by about seven, that with Meno the waiting
worked and the asking did not; she was the only adult in the compound
who did not fill a silence, so a silence with her had to be a thing you
were prepared to sit in for a while, and he was.

She put the third cup back on the tray with both hands.

"Get the book," she said.

"Which book?"

"You know which book. You've been circling it since you could walk."

---

It was on the shelf and it was heavy and the boards had come loose from
the block at the top, so you carried it flat on both forearms and not by
the spine. He put it on the low table and she turned it round with the
heel of her hand.

Inside it was ruled.

That was the first thing, and it went into him hard, and he could not
have said in that moment why. Somebody had sat down with a straight edge
and put lines down a page, four columns, the same four columns for a
hundred and something years, and then people had written in them.

*Name. Born. Died. And one more.*

The fourth column was narrow. Narrower than the others. Whoever had
ruled the page had thought it would need less room.

"That's my grandmother's hand as far as there," said Meno, "and my
mother's from the middle of that page, and mine since I was thirty-one."

"Why did you start at thirty-one?"

"Because that's when the woman before me stopped."

She said it without any weight at all and he understood that he had just
been told something and did not know yet what it was, and he put it
away.

---

She read him some of it.

She read it the way you read a rota — no ceremony, one line to a person,
and she went at a pace that was clearly the pace she went at when
nobody was listening.

"Ruri Kagami. Born the year of the high water. Died here. Ruri Suzu,
born the year after, died here. Ruri Tōno, born — that's wrong, that's
been wrong my whole life, somebody put a two where a three goes — died
at Sunaba, brought back. Ruri Mikado, taken."

"Taken."

"Taken." She went on. "Ruri Shiro, taken. Ruri Ise, born, died here. Ruri
Kayo, died here, that's a bad one, don't ask me about that one. Ruri
Manzō — "

She stopped.

She did not stop the way a person stops mid-word. She stopped the way a
person gets to the end of a line and then puts her hand down flat on the
page and leaves it there and carries on with the next one.

"Ruri Take, born the second year of the rebuilding, died here."

"You missed one."

"I did not miss one."

"Ruri Manzō," said Tadayoshi. "You said the name and then you did not
say the last column."

"Read to the end of the page," said Meno, "and stop being clever at me."

---

He looked at the fourth column instead.

*Died here.* That was most of it, and it was written out in full every
time, which struck him as a strange choice for a narrow column.

*Taken.* That was the next commonest. It was a shorter word and it was
written smaller, and in four places somebody had written the year beside
it in a different ink, as though they had found out afterwards.

And in some of them there was a third word.

It was one character. It was the shortest thing in the column and it had
been written by three different hands across the pages and it looked
slightly different every time, and it said **went**.

"What's that one?"

"That's that one."

"It means they left."

"It means they went," said Meno.

"Where?"

"Away, boy. That's what the word does."

"Where away? Ōura? Sunaba? Out of the country? Did they write? Do we
know if — "

"We don't say the names," said Meno.

---

He sat back on his heels.

*Three.*

*My mother: nobody sees this, and no reason, and the reason she gave was
half a name.*

*Meno: we don't say the names, and no reason, and she has just told me
she can name all thirty-two.*

*Hisui gets nine goes at a table and never once gets given a reason
either, and she is twenty-nine.*

*This family does not answer questions. It issues rules and then gets
old.*

He did not say any of that, because he was ten and not stupid, and
because the book was open in front of him and she had not yet taken it
away.

"May I read it?"

"You're reading it."

"May I read it properly. On my own. For a while."

Meno looked at him for what felt like a long time.

"You may read it in this room," she said, "sitting where you are, and
you may not take it out of this room, and when I say give it back you
will give it back, and you will not go up the hill and start knocking on
shutters."

"Agreed."

"*Agreed*," said Meno. "Listen to him."

---

He read it for about forty minutes and he counted the third word.

He did it properly, page by page, back through the hundred and some
years, and he lost the count twice because her hand was still lying flat
across the lower half of one page and he was not going to ask her to
move it, and each time he lost it he went back to the top of the page
and started again.

In the part that mattered — the part that covered the people who had
been alive in Meno's own lifetime, the thirty-two —

*Nineteen.*

He counted it three times. It came out nineteen three times.

*Thirteen died or were taken. Nineteen went.*

*Hisui is not making an argument. She is the last one making it.*

He sat with that and looked at the narrow column that whoever ruled the
page had thought would need less room than the others.

Then Meno said, "Give it back," and he gave it back, and she put her
hand flat on the cover and did not put it on the shelf.

---

"Get the box down while you're up."

The box was black and shallow and lived under the shelf and he had seen
it perhaps five times in his life. It was the work that had never gone
to Ōura: pieces kept back, most of them for no reason he could see, a
few of them because they were so good it would have been an insult to
sell them by weight.

She let him look through it, which she had never done.

There was a mount with three empty settings that somebody had never
filled. There was a comb-piece the length of his hand with grain in it
so fine you had to take it to the window. There was a thing that was
obviously a first attempt at something, with the taper wandering, and he
liked that one best and had no idea why.

And there was one at the bottom that was wrong.

---

It was a plain drop, bigger than the ones they made now, the length of
his forefinger. He picked it up because it was the only one in the box
he had not been able to classify from above.

He ran his thumb up the taper.

*The note is in the wrong place.*

He did it again, slowly, from the eye down to the point and back.

A note lived where the courses ended. That was not an opinion, it was
where it lived; you laid down to the last course and the last course
finished and the thinness sat at the finish, and on a two-course drop it
was at the fat end, always, without exception, in every piece he had
ever handled including four hundred of his own.

This one had it a third of the way up.

Which meant a course had stopped there. Had stopped, in the middle, and
then started again above it, and gone on to the end.

*That is not a thing that happens. That is a hand going wrong halfway
and being picked up again. A bad piece. Somebody old, or somebody cold,
or somebody who was interrupted.*

*It is also — *

He turned it over.

The break was clean. Whoever had stopped, had stopped square, and
started again square, and the piece above the fault was laid as well as
the piece below it.

*A hand that goes wrong does not go wrong tidily.*

He looked at it for a while longer, and he did not have anywhere to put
that, so he put it where he put everything he did not have anywhere for.

"Whose is this?"

"Kongō's," said Meno.

He looked up.

"That is my mother's uncle."

"It is."

"She said his name to me once. Two years ago. She said *because of my
uncle* and she has never said anything else about him and neither has
anybody, and — "

"Put it back," said Meno.

"I want to ask about — "

"Put it *back*, Tadayoshi."

---

He put it back.

She had him set the lid on and slide the box under the shelf with his
foot, because she did not want it lifted twice, and then she asked him
whether he had eaten, in a voice that had gone back to being about tea
things.

He said he had.

He went down two courses to the workshop in the last of the light, and
he did not go and start the nine drops he had left to do that day.

He got a used ledger out of the back of the bench drawer instead — a
thin one, ruled in three columns, that his father had brought home years
ago because Kanaya threw them out when the year turned. He put it on the
bench under the lamp and looked at it for a bit.

Then he took a straight edge and ruled a fourth column down the page,
narrow, on the right, and made it narrower than the other three, because
that was how it was done.

He wrote three things in it that night, and the first one was:

*Nineteen went. Nobody says the names.*
