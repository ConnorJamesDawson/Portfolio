# Chapter 6, Scene 9 — Kui

*Act 1, month 6, the first. The yard behind the bell house, Sakabe.
Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~2,820 words.*

---

He came down to Sakabe on the last day of the fifth month and did not go
and look at the riverbed.

He put the reasons down that night, in order, because he did not trust
himself on it.

*One. The road is watched at both ends and it has been for three weeks
and a boy on it is a boy on it.*

*Two. There is nothing there. I did the ground on the ninth with my own
feet and I have got all of it and going back gets me the same numbers
with a party of Kiri surveyors standing on them.*

*Three. Four days of walking for nothing, at a time when I have got an
appointment on the first.*

He looked at the three of them.

*Those are all true and they are all correct and they are not why.*

Then he wrote the fourth one, which took a while.

*Four. The seventh line is empty and it is honest and I do not want to go
and stand in that bed, because if I stand in that bed I think it will
fill itself in, and I will not be able to tell afterwards whether it
filled in because I saw something or because I was standing in the place
where it happened and could not bear the empty half any longer.*

*I would rather not know than know wrongly.*

*Write that down and then look at it in a year.*

---

The bell house at Sakabe was on the landward side and the yard behind it
had a wall on three sides and a hedge on the fourth, and there was a
handcart in it with no wheel, and the man was sitting on the shaft of the
handcart when Tadayoshi came in over the hedge instead of through the
gate.

He had thought about that for two days and had decided that coming in
over the hedge was worth what it told the man, and he was right about
that, and it was the first professional judgement of his life.

"Hedge," said the man. "Good."

He did not get up.

---

"Right. I said I'd tell you what for, out loud, and then you can say no,
so here it is and it takes about a minute."

He said it in a minute.

There was a war in the Mist and it was four years old and it was not a
war anybody outside the Mist called a war. There were people who had
decided that a village which kills its own children is a village that has
stopped being one, and there were a great many more people who agreed
with them quietly and did nothing, and the ones who had not done nothing
were down to something under eleven hundred and had been eleven hundred
for two years, which sounded stable and was not, because it was eleven
hundred different people every year.

"We're losing," said the man. "I'll say it once so you don't have to work
it out. We've been losing for four years and we'll be losing next year,
and if you want a thing that's going to win you want a different door."

*He said that first.*

*He said the worst thing about it in the first minute and he said it
before he said what he wanted.*

*That is either the most honest thing anybody has done to me since Meno
or it is a very good way of selling something, and I cannot tell which,
and I am going to have to decide anyway.*

---

"Now the part about you, and you'll not like it."

"Go on."

"I'm not here because of what was done to your people."

Tadayoshi stood in the yard and did not move.

"I don't know whose you are and I don't want to and don't tell me. There
are six or seven it could be and I gave up keeping the list about two
years ago." The man leaned forward on the shaft. "And there's two hundred
like you. Two hundred at least, in four years, and I've had a dozen of
them in front of me in yards like this one, and I couldn't use a single
one, because being owed something isn't a skill."

"Then why—"

"Because you put four of theirs into a dry bed and then walked forty-one
miles up a coast without being taken." He said it flatly, like an
inventory. "That's it. That's the whole of the reason. If your family
were all alive and well and you'd done that in a bed I'd be sat on this
shaft saying the same words."

*He is telling me that he does not care.*

*He is telling me, in a yard, to my face, on purpose, that nothing that
happened at Shioiri is of any interest to him at all, and that the only
thing about me that has a use is the worst four minutes of my life.*

*And he is right.*

*And he has told me. He could have not told me. He could have sat there
and said sorry about your family, son, and I would have — *

He stopped that one because he did not like where it went.

*I would have believed it.*

---

"What's the work?"

"Carrying, to start. Messages, money, small freight, six ports and about
nine inland places, and you'll not know what's in any of it and you'll
not ask." The man counted it off. "Four days to a fortnight a run. You
go where you're sent, you don't pick, and you don't take two things at
once."

"And after carrying?"

"After carrying, when somebody's got to sit on a road for three hours and
make sure a thing doesn't come up it." He looked at him. "You know what
that is. I'm not going to be clever about it."

"Yes."

"Say it back."

"Fences," said Tadayoshi. "On a road. Against people."

"That's it."

---

"Ports," he said.

"What about them?"

"You said six ports. Is it always the same six?"

The man did a thing with his face that was not quite a look.

"It's whatever's got freight in it," he said. "This year it's six.
Next year it's four of those six and three others. Why?"

*He has just asked me a question and he asked it like a man asking a
question and not like a man collecting one, and I do not know which it
was, and it does not matter, because the answer is my mother and I am not
going to put my mother in a yard.*

*So say something that is not it.*

He stood there and looked for the sentence, the way he had looked for one
in front of Akagane, and there was not one; there was no true small thing
he could say about ports that was not about her.

So he did not say anything.

He stood in the yard with his mouth shut and let the question lie on the
ground between them, and the seconds went past, and nothing whatever
happened to him.

*Oh.*

*That is available.*

*I have been standing in front of people for five months answering
questions because they were asked, as though a question were a thing you
had to pick up, and it is not, and I did not know that, and I have just
found it out at the age of ten in a yard behind a bell house.*

The man looked at him for about two seconds.

"Right," he said, and did not ask again, and that was the second thing he
gave away for nothing and Tadayoshi did not miss it.

*And that is the nearest I have ever come to it.*

*I did not say a false thing. I have never said a false thing and I do
not think I can. But a man asked me why and walked away with the wrong
answer in his head and I put it there by standing still.*

**Silence is a thing you can do to somebody.**

---

"Pay."

"A quarter a day when you're moving. Nothing when you're not. Keep either
way, both ends, and a floor that isn't a road."

*A quarter.*

*Ide paid me nine a week and a dinner and the coal cupboard, and this man
is offering me a quarter a day and calling it a man's wage, and I cannot
make those two numbers stand next to each other, and I do not know which
of them is the strange one.*

*Because I have never bought anything.*

He stood in the yard and had it whole for the first time, and it was not
a comfortable thing to have.

*I have bought bread nine times. That is the entire list. I do not know
what a coat costs or what a bed costs or what a man gets for a day, and I
have never once in my life had to decide whether a thing was worth what
was being asked for it, because everything I have ever had came out of a
house that made things and did not buy them.*

**I cannot price anything. Not one thing.**

*So ask him. Ask him why it is a great deal, because things that are a
great deal are things somebody has costed, and he has costed it, and he
is the only rule I have got.*

"Why that much?"

"Because it's what a man gets," said the man. "And you do what a man
does. If I paid you what a boy gets you'd work out inside a month that I
was doing it, and then I'd have a boy who does what a man does and knows
I'm short-changing him, and that's a thing I'd have to manage. I'd rather
pay you and not have to manage you."

*That is the reason.*

*That is the whole reason and there is nothing kind in it and he has told
me the ugly half without being asked.*

**A man who tells you why the good thing is good is a man who has not
finished the sentence with a knife in it.**

"All right," said Tadayoshi.

---

"One condition and it's the only one and it isn't negotiable."

"Say it."

"You keep nothing on paper." The man's voice did not change and it did
not need to. "Not a name. Not a place. Not a date. Not a route, not a
sum, not a description of a man's face, not a single word of any of it.
If you have to hold something, hold it in your head, and if you can't
hold it in your head then you can't have it."

"Why."

"Because every single person we've lost in four years was lost off a
piece of paper." He held up a hand and counted on it, without any
particular feeling. "A list in a cellar. A tally in a lining. A letter
somebody kept because it was fond. A ledger in a house that got turned
over for something else entirely. Four years. Not one of them off a
mouth. Not one."

---

Tadayoshi stood in the yard behind the bell house at Sakabe with the
ledger flat against his chest inside his shirt, where it had been for
fifty-six days, and heard a professional arrive independently at the
exact finding he had made in a harbour office on the twenty-sixth of the
fourth month with a day book open in front of him.

*That is the same finding.*

*He got it off four years of losing people and I got it off giving Ide my
own name in one second because I cannot lie, and it is the same finding,
and there is nobody else in the world who has said a true thing to me
since the fifth of the fourth month.*

*He is real.*

*That is how I know he is real. Not the riverbed and not the sitting
down. It is that he has got my finding.*

"All right," he said.

And he knew, while he was saying it, in about a second and a half, that
he was not going to do it.

---

He worked out afterwards — much later, years later — that he had known
before the man finished the sentence, and that everything after that was
building the reason.

The reason was this: *the book is not a record of postings. The book is
about her. Those are two different things and I can keep one and not the
other.*

He looked at that for about as long as it takes to breathe out.

*That is not true.*

*If a man takes that book off me on a road, he does not stop at the part
that is about her. He gets forty-four entries and he gets a hand he can
match and he gets every place I have stood since the fifth of the fourth
month, and the fact that I was writing about my mother does not make it
less of a list.*

*I know that.*

*I know it completely.*

He said nothing.

*I am going to do it anyway.*

*I have got one thing that goes all the way back and it is nine lines
about a riverbed and it is forty-four numbered pages and it is the only
object in the world that says any of it happened, and if I stop writing
it, then in a year there is no difference between me and somebody who
never had a family at all.*

**He did not, that night or ever, write down that he had decided to go on
writing things down.**

---

"Last thing," said the man. "What do you do?"

"What?"

"For the sheet. What do you do."

Tadayoshi told him.

He told him about the four seals and about a second and a quarter. He
told him about courses and what a note was and why two courses cost more
than twice one, and about grit, and about the difference between grit and
a floor, and about how far the under-hand had to be off the material and
what happened if it was wrong by an inch. He told him about the tin and
what was in it and how long a third of a tin lasted at eleven feet. He
told him about tapered points, tips up, a hand's width apart, and how
long a stand of them held a walking man, and how long it held a running
one, which was nothing at all.

It took about four minutes and he did not know how to make it shorter,
and the man let him get to the end of it without moving.

Then he took out a sheet and a stub of pencil and wrote one word on the
top of it.

*Kui.*

**The stake.**

---

*He has written the fences.*

*I told him about fences for four minutes and I told him nothing about
the other thing at all, and he did not ask, and I did not decide not to
say it. My mouth did that.*

*I have not lied. I answered the question that was asked and every word
of it was true.*

*And there is a man in this yard who has now got a word for me, and it is
made out of six minutes in a channel where I did not hurt anybody, and it
is not made out of the ninety yards.*

*I did not choose it.*

*And I am not going to correct it.*

He stood in the yard and felt the size of that, and put it down, and
found that it went in the book quite easily and did not fight him at all,
which was the part that stayed with him.

---

The man gave him the sheet.

It had four lines on it and a place and a date eleven days off, and it
was a job, and Tadayoshi read it twice and then looked at it a
third time for a reason he could not immediately name.

*That is not his hand.*

The word on the top — *Kui* — was pencil, and it was the man's, and it
was a bad hand, fast and flat and made by somebody who wrote a great deal
and did not care.

The four lines under it were ink. They were ruled off at the left. The
character for *the twelfth* had a foot on it that came back under itself,
which is a thing about nine people in a hundred do, and the whole of it
sloped about a degree and a half to the right and did not vary anywhere.

*Somebody made that. Somebody sat down and made that.*

"Whose is this?"

The man was already getting up off the shaft.

"That's the ticket's," he said.
