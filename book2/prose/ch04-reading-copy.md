# Chapter 4 — Eleven Days

*Book two. Act one, month 3 (30th) – month 4 (7th).
Ten scenes, 16,880 words.*



*Act 1, month 3, the thirtieth — day five. Kanmuri lane, Kayano. Nozaki
Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,690 words.*

---

By the second day she had a routine, which she was faintly ashamed of.

Water at the pump at the top of the lane, first thing, before the queue.
The privy behind the brewery yard, which nobody minded because nobody
looked. The cart tipped on its side across the doorway at night and
right side up against the wall in the daytime, so that she was a girl
with a cart and not a girl sleeping in a door.

And the step.

---

The step down into Anzai's doorway was about nine inches, which is four
inches more than a step wants to be, and it was in shadow from about
noon, and Kanmuri lane sloped, so that people came down it with their
weight already forward.

Twenty-two people went over it in two days.

She counted, because there was nothing else to count. Nine of them
recovered. Eleven put a hand on the wall. Two went right down, and one of
those was an old man with a basket, and she got to him before he was
properly down and got a hand under his elbow and he called her a good
girl and gave her nothing, which she had not wanted, and then went off up
the lane telling somebody about the step.

After that she started saying it.

"Mind the step."

She said it to everybody who came down the lane, whether they were coming
to that door or not, in the flat carrying voice she used at the mill,
because it turned out you could not say *mind the step* apologetically —
it came out as a question and then they went over it anyway.

"Mind the step."

"Ta, love."

"Mind the step."

"What ste — oh."

"Mind the step."

By the middle of the second day about half the lane knew she was there.

---

She got fed on the second afternoon and had not asked to be.

A woman two doors up came out with a bowl and put it on the wall beside
her, and said, "You've saved my mother's hip twice," and went in again
without waiting to be thanked, which Uzuki understood perfectly and was
grateful for, because she had got herself into a state about the
thanking.

It was barley and it had been hot at some point.

She ate all of it and put the bowl on the woman's step, washed, and went
back to the doorway.

*Day five. Two days at this door.*

*Bread gone yesterday morning. One bowl of barley. There is water.*

*And a hundred in my coat.*

She had thought about the hundred a great deal. She thought about it
about eleven times a day, and every time it came out the same, which was
that a person who spends the boot money on barley is a person who will
have no boots and no barley, and that if she ate her way through it in
Kayano she would have walked four days to be a beggar in a lane.

So the hundred stayed in the pocket.

*If nothing happens you bring it back and put it where it was.*

She was going to bring it back.

---

On the third day she got into an argument about the step with a man who
maintained that if you knew about it, it was not a hazard.

He was about fifty and he came down the lane twice, and the second time
she said, "Mind the step," and he said, "I know about the step, girl,
I've known about that step since before you were born."

"That's why you didn't go over it."

"I didn't go over it because I'm not a fool."

"You didn't go over it because you knew about it," said Uzuki. "That's
not the same. The ones who go over it aren't fools, they're just new."

The man stopped.

"How many?"

"Thirty-one. Since Tuesday."

"Thirty-one people have gone over Anzai's step since Tuesday."

"Thirty-one have gone over or caught themselves. Four went right down."
She had the numbers because she had nothing but the numbers. "It's a
nine-inch step in a shadow on a slope and it's got nothing on it. If
somebody put a bit of white paint on the front of it, or a rail, or
anything, it'd be — "

"Anzai won't have a rail," said the man. "There's been three of us
offered."

"Why not?"

"Because his father put that step in," said the man, "and that's the
whole of it, and that's all you'll get out of him about it," and he went
off down the lane, and about four steps later said, without turning
round, "Thirty-one, though. God."

---

The girl came on the evening of the third day.

Uzuki heard her before she saw her, because she came down Kanmuri lane at
a pace and she was carrying two things and swearing about one of them,
and Uzuki got up off the ground and said, "Mind the step," and the girl
said, "I *live* here," and went over the step.

She did not go down. She got a knee and a hand on the doorway and swore
properly this time and got up.

"Are you all right?"

"I'm — " The girl straightened up. She was about sixteen and she had a
face that had been awake too long and a smear of something down the front
of her. "Yes. Thank you. Who are you?"

"Nozaki Uzuki."

"Right," said the girl. "That's not — I meant why are you in my door."

---

Her name was Chiyo and she did for Anzai, which appeared to mean
everything: the house, the door, the messages, the fetching, and, for the
last six days, four trips up to the tannery with things in a bag.

She had been in that house since she was twelve.

"He's not here," she said.

"I know. The tannery. Since Tuesday."

"Since Tuesday." Chiyo put the two things down on the step, which was the
first time Uzuki had seen anybody in that lane treat the step as a
surface rather than a trap. "What d'you want him for?"

"I want him to teach me the palm work."

Chiyo laughed.

It was not a cruel laugh. It was the laugh of somebody who has had six
days of something and has been handed one more thing.

"Right," she said. "You and about four hundred people. Look — "

"I'm not asking him to come anywhere. I'm not asking him to see anybody.
I want an afternoon and then I'll go, and I'm four days from home and
I've been in this door since Saturday and I'll be in it Wednesday and
Thursday and every day after that until he comes down this lane, so if
you're going to tell me to go away you'd better mean it, because it isn't
going to work."

Chiyo looked at her for a long moment.

---

"Since Saturday," she said.

"Since Saturday."

"You've been in that door four nights."

"Three. Saturday I got in late and slept in it, and then Sunday, and
Monday, and tonight'll be four."

"Where've you been eating?"

"A woman two doors up gave me barley on Sunday."

"Sunday," said Chiyo.

"There's water at the pump."

Chiyo said something under her breath that Uzuki did not catch, and then
picked up one of the two things she had put down, which turned out to be
a covered basket, and took the cloth off it and looked in it, and put the
cloth back.

"That's his," she said. "That's for when he comes back, because he'll
come back and he'll not have eaten since Thursday and he won't go to bed
and he won't let anybody near him for about two hours."

"All right."

"And there's enough in it for two," said Chiyo, "because I always do
enough for two, because his sister comes on Fridays."

She stood there with the basket.

"It's Tuesday," said Uzuki.

"It's Tuesday," Chiyo agreed, and put the basket down on the step again,
and took out a wrapped thing about the size of her hand, and held it out.

"Don't be in the door when he comes," she said. "Be *near* the door.
There's a difference and you'll know it when you see him."

---

She went in and the door shut and Uzuki sat down on the ground with the
wrapped thing in both hands and did not open it for about a minute.

It was rice with something through it.

She ate half of it and wrapped the other half back up and put it in the
bag, because she did not know when Wednesday was going to produce
anything, and then she sat in the doorway with her back against the wood
and her arms round her knees.

*Four things she said and three of them were about him.*

*He hasn't eaten since Thursday. He won't go to bed. He won't let
anybody near him for two hours.*

She turned that over.

*That's not a man who's going to sit down and teach anybody anything.*

*That's a man coming off six days of something, and I'm going to be
standing in his doorway with a speech.*

She thought about the speech.

She had had it for eleven days and it had got better every day: three
days out, an afternoon, three days back, no fee, a chest off his list
permanently. She could say it in the dark. She had said it in the dark,
twice, on the road, to a wall at Sanzu.

*Be near the door. Not in it.*

She got up and moved the cart, and moved herself, and sat down again
against the wall of the house next along, about four feet to the left of
the doorway, which was a worse place to sleep in every respect.

Then she put her hand flat on the inside pocket, and the hundred was
there, and she went to sleep with her chin on her knees in the fourth
night of the doorway that she was no longer in.

*Act 1, month 4, the fifth, afternoon. Shioiri. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten.
POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,460 words.*

---

He worked out at about two in the afternoon that he was going to have to
eat something, because his hands had started doing a thing.

It was not shaking. He had been shaking on and off for two days and had
stopped noticing it. This was different: he had gone to put the slate
down on the flags after the fire and had missed the flag, not by much, by
about an inch, and had stood there for a moment looking at his own hand
with the professional interest of somebody whose entire trade was where a
hand was.

*Two days.*

*One cake on the morning of the third before school, and the water off the
runnel.*

*And an inch.*

He went down to the low house on the second course and opened the door he
had shut at three in the morning.

---

The crock had five flat cakes in it with the stone on top.

He ate one standing in the kitchen, badly, and had to stop halfway
through and put his hand on the table, and then finished it, and then
made himself wait about ten minutes before he had a second one, because
he had a general idea from somewhere that this was the correct approach
and no evidence for it whatsoever.

Then he wrapped the other three in the cloth off the board and put them
in his shirt.

*Three days if I am careful. Four if I am unpleasant about it.*

He filled the waterskin at the butt.

---

Then he stood in the middle of his own kitchen and worked out that he was
leaving.

It arrived in the flattest possible way and there was nothing in it he
could argue with, and he stood and let it go through in order.

*There is no food on this hill after the three in my shirt. Meno's crock
is the crock. Everything else is on a shelf in a house I have already
been through.*

*The nearest food is Ōura and Ōura is a day and a half.*

*There is no water problem. There is a fuel problem in eleven days and a
water problem in never.*

*And the fifth course is a house with a bed in it and no reason for me to
be in it.*

*I am not staying because I live here. I am staying because I do not know
where else there is.*

*That is not a plan. That is a place where I have stopped.*

He looked round the room.

*So: go to Ōura, and sell the tin, and then have some money, and then be
a person with money and no plan instead of a person with no money and no
plan. That is a smaller problem and it is the only one I can move.*

---

He went up to the workshop on the third to get the tin.

The tin was where it had been for two days: on the bench, open, with
eleven drops in it and about a dozen and a half of the rest of the
month's work, and one of his mother's on top because he had put it there
on the Tuesday morning before school and had not been back.

He put the lid on it.

Then he stood in front of the bench for a while, because of the frame.

---

It was where he had put it down on the night of the twenty-third, dished
side up, with the tapered fingers and the fillet at each root and the
rolled edge at the wrist.

*Take it.*

*It is nine weeks of a winter and it is the best thing anybody in this
family has made in my lifetime and I said that out loud to the man who
made it and it was true.*

He picked it up.

It was lighter than the old one. It sat further forward. The rolled edge
came under the web of his thumb exactly where he had been rubbing since
he was seven without knowing he was rubbing.

*And it is a Ruri frame that nobody else on the coast has got.*

He stood with it in both hands.

*Think about it properly.*

*I am going to walk into Ōura tomorrow with a tin of crystal and sell it,
which is a thing a boy from a crystal family does, and everybody within
four hundred yards of that counter has known my father for eleven years.
That is already a risk and I am taking it because I have to eat.*

*A frame is not a risk I have to take.*

*Anybody who has ever bought Ruri work knows what a Ruri frame looks
like. This one does not look like a Ruri frame. This one looks like a
Ruri frame that somebody has spent nine weeks improving, which is a thing
exactly one man on this coast could have done, and he is on a boat, and
the improvement is going to be the first thing anybody notices about the
work that comes out of it for the next thirty years.*

*If it turns up in a pawnshop in Ōura it is not a frame. It is a
signature.*

He put it down on the bench.

Then he opened the drawer at the back and took the old one out from under
the rag.

---

It was two hundred years old and beaten thin by somebody dead and it did
nothing at all.

The palm was flat and the fingers were the same thickness all the way out
and it had a spot on the heel where four generations of hands had worn
the copper down to about half, and there were about nine hundred of them
on that coast, and it was the most anonymous object in the building.

He put it inside his coat.

*It is worse and I am taking it because it is worse.*

*Enter that.*

He did not enter it. He had the ledger inside his shirt and he did not
get it out, and he was aware of not getting it out, and he did not
examine why.

---

He put Akagane's frame in the drawer at the back, under the rag, where
the old one had been.

He did it carefully — dished side down, so nothing could sit in the dish;
and he put the rag over it properly and not in a heap, and he shut the
drawer, and then he opened it again and looked at it, and shut it.

*Somebody will come here eventually. A house with a trade in it does not
stay shut for ever. Somebody will buy the hill or be given it or break
into it, and they will go through this bench, and they will find it.*

*And they will not know what it is. They will think it is a frame.*

*That is the whole of what happens to nine weeks.*

He went out and shut the workshop door.

---

He left at about four, up the shoulder path, with the tin under his arm
and the old frame inside his coat and three flat cakes and a waterskin
and a ledger and a corner of burnt paper and a drop with a fault in it.

And the grease tin.

He had gone back for that at the last minute, from the shed, where she
had told him to take it and use it, and it was flat and it was tin and it
weighed almost nothing and there was no argument in favour of it at all.

He put it in his coat pocket and did not cost it.

---

At the top of the shoulder he stopped, because he had stopped there every
day of his life going anywhere, and because if he did not stop there
deliberately this time he was going to find out later that he had walked
past it.

Six courses. Thirty-one roofs. Thirty of them shut and one of them open
at the top, third from the end, because he had gone in there this morning
and not shut it after him.

He looked at that for a while, and then went back — four hundred yards
up and along, which cost him twenty-five minutes and the last of the
light — and shut Take's door.

Then he came back to the shoulder and went north-east, on the top road,
towards Ōura.

*It is a day and a half.*

*I have three cakes and a tin.*

*And after Ōura I do not know.*

He walked until he could not see the path and then a bit further, and
slept under a wall about six miles on, in his coat, with the tin between
his knees.

*Act 1, month 4, the first — day six. The tannery below Kayano. Nozaki
Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,680 words.*

---

She went on the Wednesday and she went to look and not to ask, and she
made herself say that out loud at the top of the lane before she set off,
which was a thing she had started doing on the road and had not yet
stopped.

"Look. Not ask."

A woman going past with a yoke said, "What?"

"Nothing."

---

The tannery was twenty minutes downstream and you could smell it from
about half of that, and when she got there she understood why nobody had
been willing to say what a tannery was.

It was a long low place with a yard, and the yard had pits in it, and one
of the pits at the far end had gone in.

You could see where. There was a raw quarter-circle of ground with a
board fence lying across it at an angle and about four feet of one wall
of the pit standing up out of a slump of clay and stones, and the whole
of the far end of the yard had been trodden into porridge by six days of
people going back and forth.

There were about twenty men standing around not doing anything, which she
worked out after a while was because there was nothing to do and had not
been for two days, and they came anyway.

---

She did not go in.

She sat on the wall on the road side, about forty yards up, where a bank
of nettles came down to the fence and there was a gap in the boards, and
she stayed there for six hours.

She learned nine things and only one of them was about her.

---

*One. They got four out on the first day and two of those were walking.*

She got that off two men at the fence who were saying it to a third who
had come from somewhere else. Everybody at that yard was saying the same
eleven facts to whoever came, over and over, in the same order, and by
about noon Uzuki had them all.

*Two. They got two more out on the second day and one of those died at
the fence and the other one is in the long shed.*

*Three. There are three in the long shed. One is the one from the second
day. One is a boy of about fifteen whose legs were under it. One is the
foreman and nobody says anything about the foreman except that he is in
the long shed.*

*Four. There is nobody left under it. They stopped digging on the fourth
day and there was an argument about stopping and the argument is still
going on at that fence.*

*Five. Anzai has been here since Tuesday and has been out of the long
shed to sleep twice.*

---

She saw him at about eleven.

He came out into the yard and stood in it for about two minutes with his
hands hanging and his head back looking at the sky, and somebody brought
him something in a cup and he drank about half of it and gave it back and
went in again.

He was not what she had built.

She had built, over eleven days on a road, a man of about fifty with the
sort of authority you can see from across a street. What came out of the
long shed was smaller than that and older than that and moved like
somebody carrying a full bucket in each hand, and the front of him was
filthy, and she watched him stand in a stinking yard with his eyes shut
and she thought, with a lurch:

*He's not going to be able to.*

---

*Six.*

The long shed had a window at the end and the window had no glass in it,
because it was a shed, and the bank of nettles came up to within about
eight feet of it.

She was in the nettles for two hours and she paid for it for three days.

But she could see in.

---

*Seven. It is not what I thought it was.*

She had had a picture and the picture had been in her head since Tazu's
shed and she had never once examined it: a hand on a chest, and a light,
and the person breathing better.

What it was, was a man sitting on a stool beside a board bed for
twenty-five minutes with both hands flat on a boy's thigh and his head
down and his eyes shut, not moving at all — and the boy on the bed
crying, the whole time, the entire twenty-five minutes, in the flat
exhausted way of somebody who has been crying since Tuesday.

Then Anzai took his hands off and sat back and put his palms over his own
eyes.

And the boy stopped crying.

He stopped about forty seconds after the hands came off, and he said
something, and Anzai said something back that was three or four words,
and then the boy went to sleep, or stopped being awake, which from eight
feet away in a nettle bank is the same thing.

*Eight. It took twenty-five minutes and it worked and it worked
afterwards and not during.*

*And it is going to come back.*

---

She knew that because she watched it come back.

She stayed in the nettles. She stayed there because her legs had stopped
being a consideration at about the ninety-minute mark, and because Anzai
had gone from the boy to the man in the middle bed and had done the same
thing for about a quarter of an hour, and then to the third one, and then
had gone out and been in the yard with his eyes shut and come back —

— and at about half past three he sat down on the stool beside the boy
again and put both hands flat on the same place.

*Four and a half hours.*

*He did twenty-five minutes and it bought four and a half hours and now
he is doing it again.*

She sat in the nettles and did the arithmetic that she was going to carry
for the rest of her life.

---

*Nine.*

*Three men in that shed.*

*Say twenty minutes each and say four times a day, which is what I have
watched, and that is four hours of him, every day, and he has been doing
it since Tuesday and it is Wednesday week.*

*And in between he sleeps twice.*

*So the whole of that man, awake, is about — call it fourteen hours a
day, because I have watched him do fourteen — and four of them are in
that shed and the rest of it is everybody else in Kayano, who are still
having babies and falling off things and going yellow, and Chiyo said
four hundred people want him.*

*An afternoon is four hours.*

She put her chin on her arms in the nettles.

*I have walked four days to ask a man for four hours.*

*And I have been telling myself for eleven days that it costs him
nothing, because it isn't money, because he doesn't have to come
anywhere, because he can teach me while he's sitting down.*

*And what four hours of him actually is, is that boy in there not getting
his night.*

---

She got out of the nettles at about six and walked back up the road with
her legs on fire and did not notice for the first mile.

*It isn't a good trade for him.*

*Tazu was wrong. Tazu wasn't lying, Tazu meant it — but Tazu was talking
about a medic in a town with an old man's chest on his list, and my
father isn't on anybody's list, he's four days away in another country,
and I am not taking a thing off Anzai's week.*

*I'm asking him to give me four hours of himself out of a shed with three
men in it.*

*That's not a trade. That's begging with a diagram on it.*

She stopped in the road.

*And I'm going to do it anyway.*

---

She spent the Thursday in the lane rewriting it.

That was the whole of the Thursday. She sat with her back to the wall
four feet to the left of the doorway and said *mind the step* about
eleven times and took it apart and put it back together, and by the
evening she had thrown out most of the thing she had carried for eleven
days.

Out: *it's a good trade for you.*

Out: *you get a chest off your list permanently.*

Out: *three days there and three days back and an afternoon in the
middle*, which she had liked very much and which she now understood was
a sentence about her own walking and not about anything he cared about.

Out: the whole of Tazu's argument, which she had been so pleased with,
and which she had rehearsed to a wall at Sanzu, and which had been built
for somebody else's situation and did not survive four hours in a nettle
bank.

What was left was about nine words and she did not like it at all,
because it was true and it had no case in it and it did not give him
anything.

*I have nothing to give him.*

*I'm going to stand in front of a man who hasn't slept since Tuesday and
tell him I've got nothing, and ask anyway.*

She sat against the wall in the dark with her arms round her knees.

*Ma would say that's begging.*

*Da would say — *

She stopped there and did not finish it, because she found she did not
know what her father would say, and she had never in her life not known
that, and she was too tired to go looking.

Somewhere at the bottom of the lane a door went.

She got her head up off her knees.

Then it went quiet again, and it was not him, and it was not going to be
him until the Friday.

*Act 1, month 4, the seventh. Kanaya's counter, Ōura. Tadayoshi Ruri,
age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,760 words.*

---

He went in at about ten in the morning and the bell hit the door.

There were two people at the counter and he stood behind them with the
tin under his arm and his hood up and looked at the floor, and it took
eleven minutes, and in the eleven minutes he heard Kenji say *good
morning* twice and *that's a nice one* once, and there was nothing at all
in any of it.

Then the door went and it was the two of them.

He put the tin on the counter and took his hood down.

---

Kenji did not say anything.

That was the first thing and it went into Tadayoshi like cold water,
because Kenji said something to everybody — Kenji had a greeting for a
man buying a lace and a greeting for a woman looking at a tray she was
not going to buy from — and what he did when a Ruri came into his shop
was *Ruri-san*, always, eleven years of it.

He looked at the tin. Then at the boy. Then, for about a second and a
half, at the door.

"Shut that," he said.

"The — "

"The door. Shoot the bolt. Top one, it's stiff."

Tadayoshi went and shot the bolt.

---

*He has heard.*

*Obviously he has heard, it is four days, and a household under order
stops sending work, and a shop that buys from a household under order is
a shop that has to write something on a return.*

*So he knows there is no more Ruri work coming to this counter and he
knows why, in whatever version of why they gave him.*

*And he has just shut his own shop in the middle of a Friday morning.*

He came back to the counter.

Kenji had the lid off the tin and had tipped it out on the black cloth
and was going through it with the spatula, fast, in fives, without saying
the grades out loud.

"How much do you want for it," said Kenji.

"I do not know what it is worth."

"Yes you do."

"There are thirty-one pieces and eleven of them are in the — " Tadayoshi
stopped. "Eleven of them are the ones you graded up last month."

"Twelve."

"Eleven."

"I've got twelve here," said Kenji, and put two aside with the spatula
and did not look up. "You've done another one since and you didn't know
it. There's your eleven and there's this."

---

He did the whole thirty-one in about four minutes.

Then he stood back from the cloth with the spatula in his hand and said,
"Fourteen."

"That is too much."

"It is not too much."

"Four dozen and three was fifteen. This is thirty-one and eleven of them
are threes and the rest are twos and a low three at best, and that is
about nine and a half, ten if the boat has started moving."

"The boat has not started moving."

"Then it is nine and a half."

"It's fourteen," said Kenji, "and I'll tell you once that I'm not going
to discuss it, and you can take it or you can put the lid on and go and
try Marukiya, and Marukiya will give you six and then somebody will be
round at Marukiya's asking who sold them, which they will answer, because
they are not fools either."

Tadayoshi stood at the counter.

*He is buying the tin at half again what it is worth and he is doing it
fast and he has bolted his own door.*

*That is not kindness. A kind man asks whether I have eaten.*

*That is a man buying an object off his premises.*

"All right," he said.

---

Kenji counted it out.

He did it the way he did everything at that counter, which was quickly
and in stacks and without any ceremony at all, and he pushed it across
and Tadayoshi put it away in four places on his person because he had
worked out on the road that four places is better than one.

Then Kenji swept the thirty-one into the tin and put the lid on and put
the tin under the counter and not on the shelf.

He put both hands on the counter and looked at them.

"They came here yesterday," he said.

---

Tadayoshi stood absolutely still.

"Two of them," said Kenji. "About four in the afternoon. In here. Bolted
it themselves, which I've never had done to me in my own shop in twenty
years."

*Do not say anything.*

"They asked me — " He stopped and started again, in a different place.

Her mother stood on the other side of the table.

"Yes," she said.

"That's why you said no. In the kitchen. That's why it was four seconds."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you *tell* me?"

Her mother put both hands flat on the table beside the twist of cloth.

"Because you'd have carried it," she said, "and it wouldn't have got him
to Kayano either."

---

She went out into the passage at some point after that.

She did not decide to. She was in the kitchen and then she was in the
dark bit by the back door with her hand on the wall, and the two women
were talking very quietly behind her in the way people do when they have
been given something to do at last.

The rack was where the rack was.

The top peg had her father's bokken on it, where he had put it every
evening of her life, and where it had been since a Sunday in the second
month when he had handed it to her and told her to take them in and had
sat down on the barrow and had not got up.

The bottom peg was empty.

She had her own in her left hand. She had carried it eleven days and two
hundred and some miles and had done the third form eleven times in a road
with three men in a wood and had not once got the eighth count clean, and
it had been under the sacking in the cart since Sanzu.

She put it back on the bottom peg.

Then she stood in the passage in the dark and looked at the two of them
up there, one above the other, the way they had been on every single
evening of her life.

*There.*

*That's the pair of them back.*

And she stood with her hand still up on the peg and found that she could
not think of one single person in that house who was ever going to take
either of them down again.
