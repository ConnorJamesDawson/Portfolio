# Chapter 9, Scene 6 — Four Seconds

*Act 1, month 9, the seventeenth, night. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten.
POV: Uzuki. ~2,140 words.*

---

It had been a Thursday and she had gone up to Kuriya's and done twenty-five
minutes and it had been a good one but not as good as the fourteenth, and
she had come back down and there had been the barley and somebody had said
something about the gate.

She wrote *17th* on the back of the bag with nothing after it and put it
behind the brick and went to bed at about half nine.

---

It was the light that woke her.

Not a sound. She went over it about four thousand times afterwards and she
was always sure of that: there was no sound first. She came up out of sleep
because the inside of her eyelids went from black to orange, which is a
thing that happens when somebody opens a door between you and a lamp.

She was on her right side. She always slept on her right side.

Her hand was where it went.

---

The door was open and there was a shape in it.

The kitchen was behind him and the kitchen was lit, because the lamp was
on the table, and the lamp had a shade of sorts on the range side, so the
light came flat across the room from the yard end and it lay along the
floor and up the wall by her feet.

And because he was standing in the doorway and not in the middle of it —
because he had come in and turned, and had his hand still on the frame —
there was about eighteen inches of the kitchen she could see past him.

She could see the corner of the table.

She could see the lamp.

And she could see her mother sitting at it.

---

Her mother was sitting on the near side of the table with her hands in
her lap and she was facing the door.

Not sideways. Not turning. **Facing it.**

She was dressed. She had her outdoor shawl on the back of the chair, not
on her, but on the back of the chair, which meant she had brought it out
with her.

She was not doing anything with her hands.

*She's up.*

*She's dressed and she's up and it's the middle of the night and she's
sitting at the table with the lamp lit and her chair turned round.*

*She didn't come out because she heard something.*

*You don't turn a chair round for a noise.*

---

Her mother was looking at her.

Uzuki was on a mat on the floor of her own room with her head about a foot
off the ground and her eyes open and there was a man in the doorway, and
past him, at a table, eleven feet away, in the light, her mother was
looking straight at her.

*One.*

She did not decide to count. It went the way it had gone since she was
seven and it did not ask her.

*Two.*

*She can see me. There is no version of this where she cannot see me. I am
lying on the floor in the light off that lamp and my eyes are open and she
is looking at my face.*

*Three.*

*Get up.*

*Get up. Get up. You are eleven feet away and there is a table between you
and me and it is not even a big table.*

*Four.*

And she understood, on four, with her hand under the mat and her eyes on
her mother's face, that she was counting, and what she was counting, and
that whatever number this stopped at was a number she was going to have
for the whole of the rest of her life.

Her mother did not move.

---

She never got past four.

Not because it stopped at four. It did not stop at four; it went on for
however long it went on for, and she never knew and never tried to work it
out and refused, absolutely and for thirty years, to make an estimate,
because a made number is not a counted one.

She got to four and then the counting stopped, and something else started,
and it was not thinking.

---

He came into the room.

He said something. She never knew what it was. It was two or three words
and it was not loud and it was in the voice — the low level one, the one
that had come through a brick wall on the twentieth of the seventh month
and through a shut door on the sixth of the ninth — and hearing it in the
same room as her was the only part of the whole night that she would call
being frightened.

She was on her right side with her hand under the mat and she did not move
it.

He got down.

---

And then it was over.

That was how she had it, that night and afterwards: not a sequence, not a
thing with parts. There was a moment when he was coming down and there was
a moment when it was finished, and the middle of it was about a second and
she could not put anything into it at all.

She had the leather. She was holding the leather sheath in her left hand
and she did not remember taking it off, and she had it in her hand for the
next four hours and did not put it down.

She had the knife in her right hand and it had gone up and across, once,
and it had come from under her, from the mat, from the place she had been
lying on for ten weeks, and it had gone up into the side of him at about
the height his face was at.

That was all. It was one movement and it was up and across and it was the
only thing she did.

---

He was on the floor.

He went sideways off her and back into the corner between the mat and the
wall and he got a hand up and the hand did not do anything useful, and he
made a noise.

She heard the noise and she did not put a name to it and she did not
recognise it as information, and it was information, and it was the most
important thing in that room, and she did not have it for two years.

**It was the noise a man makes who is going to go on making noises.**

She sat up.

---

She sat up with her back to the wall and her knees under her and a knife
in one hand and a piece of leather in the other and looked at what she had
done, because she had to look, because she was going to have to know.

She did not look for long. There was not that much to see and it was very
bad.

*The face.*

*It's the whole of that side of the face.*

*And the eye.*

She looked at the eye for about as long as it takes to be certain.

*That's gone. That is gone, that is not damaged, that is gone.*

Then she stopped looking.

---

*Right.*

*Now what.*

She sat on the mat with her back against the brick and had nothing at all.

*There isn't a now what.*

*I have not got one. I have not got one single thing after this. I have
had ten weeks and I have thought about this every night for ten weeks and
I have never once, not one time, got past the part where it happens.*

*Because I could not say what it was for.*

*And a thing you cannot say what it is for is a thing you cannot make a
plan after, because the plan is the bit that comes after the what-for, and
I never had the what-for, so there is nothing here.*

She sat there.

*He's not getting up.*

*Look at him. He is not getting up. He is not going to do anything to
anybody tonight and I could put the knife down and walk out of that door
and nothing would happen to me.*

*So it's over.*

*Why am I not —*

---

She looked at the doorway.

The door was still open. The lamp was still on the table. The light still
came flat across the room from the yard end and lay along the floor and up
the wall by her feet.

Her mother was sitting at the table.

---

She had not moved.

She had not moved at any point. Not when the door went; not in the four
seconds; not when he came into the room; not when he said the two or three
words; not in the second in the middle; not when he went into the corner
and made the noise; and not in the however-long-it-was that Uzuki had spent
sitting up against the brick with a knife in her hand looking at a face.

She was sitting on the near side of the table with her hands in her lap
and her shawl on the back of the chair and she was facing the door.

And she was looking at her daughter.

---

That was when Uzuki got up.

---

She did not think anything. She wanted, for years afterwards, to be able
to say that she had thought something — that she had had a finding, or a
sentence, or even a word — and she had not.

What she had was her body doing the thing it had been rehearsed to do on
the second day in that house, in the afternoon, in the sun, when she had
knelt in the corner eleven times with a brick in her hand and told herself
that everybody should know where their things are.

*Down on one knee at the corner.*

*Two fingers.*

*Brick out.*

*Bag.*

She had it in her hand and she was standing at the window before any part
of her had said anything at all, and the last thing she saw in that room —
turning, with the bag in her fist and the knife still in the other hand
and the leather sheath somehow still gripped in her fingers underneath it —
was her mother, eleven feet away, at a table, in a lit kitchen, with her
hands in her lap.

Looking at her.

Not standing up.

---

She was out of the window in four.

The catch was stiff. It had been stiff since the second of the seventh
month and she had known it was stiff and had counted it at four and had
told herself she was being sensible, and it was stiff that night as well,
and it took four, and she was seven from the mat.

She went over the sill and dropped about five feet onto the packed dirt at
the end of the yard and went down onto one knee and got up.

She did not run at first.

She stood in the dark at the end of her own yard for about three seconds
with a bag and a knife and a piece of leather and no shoes on, and she
turned round once, and the window she had come out of was a black square
in a brick wall, and the kitchen window four feet to the left of it was lit
and had nobody in front of it.

The shed door had its top half open the way it had had its top half open
every morning since the fourteenth of the fifth month, and the mule's head
was over it, because he had heard something.

She looked at him for about a second.

Then she went out of the gate and up the lane and past the shrine and did
not go on the road.

---

She was about two hundred yards up before her feet started, because the
lane is stone at the top end and she had come out with nothing on them,
and she stopped and stood on one foot and then the other and understood
what she had done.

*I've come out with no shoes.*

She stood in the lane above Nakadō in the dark and did the arithmetic and
it took about four seconds and it was the first thing she had thought since
the light woke her.

*They're by the door. They're by the kitchen door, on the inside, where
they've been every night since the second of the seventh month, and the
kitchen door is past him and past her.*

*So they're gone.*

*So it's no shoes.*

She stood there.

*Right.*

*Then it's no shoes.*

And she went on up the lane in the dark, on stone, on the seventeenth
night of the ninth month of the year she was ten.
