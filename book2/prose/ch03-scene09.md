# Chapter 3, Scene 9 — What Does Not Burn

*Act 1, month 4, the fifth, morning. Shioiri, the yard on the third
course. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,880 words.*

---

He found it in the light, on the flags outside the workshop, and he had
walked past it twice in the dark.

It was about four feet across and it had been made in the middle of the
yard where the flags are and not against anything, which was the correct
way to do it and which told him, before he was near enough to see what
was in it, that somebody had made a fire on purpose and had not wanted it
to spread.

The rain had been on it for a day and a half.

It had gone to a grey-black slurry in the low places between the flags
and a crust everywhere else, and there was a rim of ash washed out to the
edges of it in a ring, and the middle was still black and still deep.

He stood at the edge with his hands at his sides.

*They did not take the papers.*

---

He knelt down.

He did it properly, which took him about an hour and a half, and he did
it with a flat piece of slate off the shed roof because putting his hands
in it turned out to be a thing he could not do for more than about four
minutes at a time, and because his hands were not going to be any use to
him afterwards if he did.

He went from the outside in.

*Boards.*

There were two, at the edge, half burnt through and lying apart, and they
had been the front and back of something and they were the right size,
and he sat back on his heels with one of them across his knees and turned
it over twice.

*Ten and a quarter by seven and a half.*

*You carry it flat on both forearms because the boards have come away
from the block at the top, and she never had it mended and it has been
like that since before I was born.*

He put it down beside him on the flags.

Then he put it in his lap again, and then he put it down again, and then
he got on with it.

---

There was almost nothing else.

Paper does not survive a fire that somebody has built properly, and
whoever had built this one had built it properly: it had been made up
with something under it and it had been given air and it had burned down
to a depth, and there were no half-pages and no rolled-up centres and no
lucky lumps.

He got three corners.

Two of them had nothing on them at all. The third had a rule on it — a
straight edge, run in ink, top to bottom, about a quarter of an inch in
from the fold — and about a third of the next rule over, and between the
two of them a piece of a character that could have been the top of a
year or the top of about eleven other things.

That was the whole of what was left of a hundred and some years.

He looked at it for a while with it lying on the flat of his palm, and
then a spot of rain came onto it and took the corner of the ink out
sideways, and he closed his hand and opened it and there was less.

He put it inside his shirt anyway.

---

The crystal was all there.

That was the thing.

He got it out of the black in the middle of the fire, piece by piece,
with the slate and then with his fingers, and it came out of the ash the
way stones come out of a stream — dull with it, and then wiped, and then
exactly what it had been.

*The comb-piece.* Grain in it so fine you had to take it to the window,
the length of his hand, made by somebody about four generations back
whose name he had read three weeks ago and could not now recover, because
the page it was written on was under his knees.

*The mount with three empty settings.* Silver gone black and the settings
untouched.

*The first attempt with the taper wandering* — the one he had liked best
and had never been able to say why.

*Eleven more.*

He laid them out on a flag in a row as he got them, in the order they
came out, because that was a thing you could do.

Fourteen pieces of the work of a family that had made crystal at that
inlet for four hundred years, sitting in a line on a wet flagstone in the
morning, none of them damaged, all of them worth money.

And nothing else.

---

*They burned the register and left the box.*

*The box was under the shelf and the register was on it. They took both
out and put both on this fire, and the box burned, and the pieces went
through the bottom of it into the flags.*

*And they did not come back and pick them out.*

*Which means the pieces were never the point. They were in the box and
the box was in the room with the papers and the papers were the point,
and everything that happened to Meno's fourteen articles happened to them
because they were near something else.*

He sat back on his heels in the wet.

*They have burned the only thing on this hill that could not be replaced
and left everything that could be bought.*

*And it was not malice. Nobody stood here being cruel about it. Somebody
had an instruction that said what to do with a household's papers and
they did it, correctly, in the middle of a yard, away from the buildings,
and then they went down to the boat.*

He looked at the fourteen pieces in a row.

*A hundred and some years of names, and nineteen of them went, and she
would not say one of them, and now nobody can.*

---

The last one was at the bottom, under where the box had been.

He nearly did not get it, because by then he had stopped believing there
was anything else and had gone over the middle a second time out of a
sort of stubbornness, and it came up on the edge of the slate with the
ash off it in one wipe.

It was a plain drop, bigger than the ones they made now, the length of
his forefinger.

---

He sat down on the flags with it.

*I know what this is.*

He ran his thumb up the taper without deciding to, from the eye down to
the point and back, twice, and it was exactly where it had been in a
black shallow box under a shelf on the twenty-third of the second month
with an old woman watching him.

*The note is a third of the way up.*

*It is a third of the way up, which is not where a note lives, which
means a course stopped in the middle and started again above it, which
does not happen.*

*And the break is clean. The part above the fault is laid as well as the
part below it.*

*A hand that goes wrong does not go wrong tidily.*

He turned it over in his fingers.

*"Whose is this?"*

*"Kongō's."*

*"That is my mother's uncle."*

*"It is."*

*"Put it back, Tadayoshi."*

---

He looked at the fourteen in the row for a long time.

Then he picked up the comb-piece and put it back down.

*I could take four of them. I could take four of them in my shirt and
walk into any shop in Ōura and be a boy selling a dead woman's jewellery,
and I would get about a third of what they are worth, and I would eat for
two months.*

*And I know exactly what they are. Every single one of them. I could tell
you the courses on the comb-piece and I could tell you why the first
attempt wanders and I could tell you what the mount was for.*

*They are answers. All fourteen of them are answers to questions I have
already asked.*

He put the drop with the fault in it into his left hand and closed it.

*This one is not.*

*This one is the only object I have ever held that I cannot account for,
and the two people who could have told me are a woman who would not and a
man on the other end of fifty years, and it went in a box under a shelf
and it has come out of a fire and it is not damaged and it is still
wrong.*

He held it for a while.

*And I do not know why that is the one.*

*Enter that as a finding, and enter it honestly: I am about to do a thing
I cannot cost, for a reason I cannot write down, and I have gone through
it four times and the reason is not there.*

*It is simply the one I am taking.*

---

He put the thirteen back.

He did it properly: into the ash where the box had been, all of them,
pushed down under with the slate, and then a good six inches of the wet
black over the top, and then he broke up the crust round the edges and
raked it in until the middle of the fire was a fire again and not a place
where somebody had been sorting things.

Anybody who came back and looked at that yard would see a burn a day and
a half old that the rain had got at.

It took him twenty minutes.

*Twenty,* he thought, when he was standing up and wiping the slate on the
grass, and then did not think about it any more.

---

He put the drop inside his shirt with the ledger and the corner of paper,
and buttoned it, and put his hand flat over the outside of his coat to
check, which he was going to do about four hundred times in the following
year without once noticing he was doing it.

Then he went up the hill.

It was about eleven in the morning and it had stopped raining and the sun
was doing the thing it does off wet slate, and he came up the courses past
the shut doors — third, fourth, fifth, all of them shut, thirty-one of
them, because he had gone round in the dark at three in the morning and
done it.

At the top of the sixth he stopped in front of Take's house.

He had shut that one too. He had come back at about half three and shut
it behind him and then gone to sleep on the bed frame, and this morning
he had come out and pulled it to out of habit, the way you pull a door to
behind you when you are the last one out of a house.

He stood in front of it with his hand out.

Then he opened it, and went in, and did not shut it after him, because
there was nobody left on the hill to shut it against.
