# Chapter 6 — Worth Keeping

*Book two. Act one, month 5 (12th) – month 6 (27th).
Ten scenes, 19,750 words.*



*Act 1, month 5, the twelfth. The market at Nezu. Tadayoshi Ruri, age
ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~2,030 words.*

---

He came into Nezu on the twelfth, from the landward side, with a bundle
of cut rushes on his shoulder that he had spent forty minutes cutting
and had no use for at all.

The rushes were the whole of the plan. A boy walking into a town with
nothing in his hands is a boy walking into a town. A boy walking into a
town with an armful of anything is a boy who has been sent for it, and
nobody looks at him twice, and if anybody does look, the thing they
remember afterwards is the rushes.

*Which is the trouble with it. They remember the rushes and they
remember that a boy had them, and if somebody comes along in a week and
asks whether a boy came in off the marsh road, the rushes are the thing
that makes them say yes.*

*So they go in the first ditch inside the town and I do not carry them
again.*

They went in the first ditch inside the town.

---

Nezu sat on both sides of a river that came out into a bay through a
gravel bar, and the bar was the whole reason there was a town there at
all: it broke the sea, and it made a flat brown road of water half a mile
long behind it where anything could lie up, and the state had put a stone
quay on the north side of that road about sixty years ago and the town
had grown backwards away from it ever since.

He counted forty-one boats and stopped counting, because forty-one was
nothing and because he had learned in Sunaba that counting boats is a
thing you do when you have not yet worked out what to count.

The market was on the landward side, on the flat where the road came
down, and it went on Tuesdays and Fridays and it was Friday.

He went into it because it was the loudest place in the town and because
a boy standing still in a market is a boy waiting for his mother.

---

He heard it the first time inside four minutes.

He was standing at the end of a plank table with rope on it, a foot back
from a woman who was buying rope she plainly did not need at a price she
plainly thought was wrong, and the man behind the table said, to nobody,
in the middle of an argument about rope:

"—and they're saying five."

"Five," said the woman.

"Five of theirs. In a dry bed the far side of Sakabe. Four days back
now."

"That's not five, that's the whole of a squad."

"That's what they're saying."

Tadayoshi stood and looked at the rope.

*Four.*

*It was four.*

He was very careful, at that point, with his hands and his face, because
he had discovered in the first week on the road that he could not always
tell what his face was doing, and he had made a rule about it, and the
rule was that when a thing arrived he did nothing at all for a count of
three and then did the ordinary thing.

He did nothing for a count of three.

Then he moved down the table and looked at a coil of tarred line as
though he had been sent for tarred line, and he listened.

---

By eleven he had heard it four more times and he had it in five versions
and he had them separate in his head the way he would have had them
separate on a page.

*One. Five of theirs, dry bed the far side of Sakabe, four days.*

*Two. Six. Same bed. Six is the same as five with one more.*

*Three. Five, and it was Kiri did it themselves, because they were
carrying something and it was worth more than they were. The man who
said that said it the way you say a thing you have said before and got a
laugh for.*

*Four. Bandits off the Nezu road, which is not a thing that exists and
everybody standing there knew it, and the woman who said it said it
because she wanted the other one to stop talking about the state.*

*Five.*

The fifth one was said by a carter with a bad shoulder, sitting on his
own tailboard eating, to a man who had asked him what the road was like.

"Road's fine. Road's not fine at Sakabe, mind, they've got it shut
either end while they take it apart."

"Take what apart?"

"The ground," said the carter. "There's a bit of it stood up like a
hedge."

Tadayoshi went on looking at the tarred line.

"And there's one of 'em ninety yards up the middle of the road."

---

*Stop.*

*Stop. Do not move. Count three.*

He counted three, and put the tarred line down, and walked out of the
market at the ordinary speed of a boy who has been sent for tarred line
and has been told the price and is going back to say what it was.

He got fifty yards up the lane behind the market and put his back against
a wall with a water butt at the end of it and stood there.

---

*Nobody got away from that bed.*

*I know that. I know it in a way I would rather not know things. There
were four and I counted four and I walked round all four of them and I
put my hand on three of them and I did the distances with my own feet.*

*So nobody told him.*

*Somebody has been down there.*

He worked it in order, because working it in order was the only thing he
had, and because the alternative was standing against a wall with his
heart going.

*A boat or a runner took it up the coast. Four days is right for that;
the eighth to the twelfth is four days and the coast road is walkable in
three if you are grown and not being careful.*

*Then a party went down. They have it shut either end, so it is a party
with the standing to shut a road, which means it is not the town and it
is not a family — it is the state or it is somebody the state has sent.*

*And they are TAKING IT APART. Which is the word he used and he used it
about the ground.*

*They are not burying them. They are measuring them.*

He put his hand flat on the outside of his coat, on the left, where the
grease tin was, and did not notice that he did it.

*A bit of it stood up like a hedge.*

*The first four fences. I put them in going in and I never took them
down and they are still standing four days later and they are the
straightest thing in that bed.*

*Eleven feet. Tips up. A hand's width apart.*

*Somebody is standing in front of those with a rule.*

---

*And ninety yards.*

*He said ninety yards. He did not say a number I would have to check. He
said the number.*

*I did that distance with my own feet on the ninth of the fifth month and
it took me two hundred and forty-one paces and I have it written down,
and four days later a carter with a bad shoulder is saying it to a man
about a road, in a market, forty miles away, for nothing.*

**A fight nobody survives still makes a report. It just takes four days
and comes out through a market.**

He put that down, in the place where he put things, and he was aware
while he was putting it down that he was pleased with the shape of it,
and that being pleased with the shape of it was the sort of thing that
was going to be a problem later.

---

There was one more thing and he did not want it and he had it anyway.

*They are saying a man.*

Not one of the five versions had a boy in it. Two of them had a man in
them explicitly — *a big fellow*, one of them, and *a Kiri man gone
off*, another — and the other three had nobody in them at all, which is
its own kind of a man, because when people do not say who did a thing
they are not thinking of a child.

*That is the best thing that could have happened to me and I cannot
manage to be glad of it.*

He stood with the water butt at the end of the lane and made himself go
at it properly, because he did not trust anything he felt about that
riverbed and he had decided on the ninth that he was going to trust the
arithmetic instead.

*It is protective. A description that is wrong by twenty years and four
stone is a description that walks past me in a market, and it is going
to go on walking past me for as long as it lasts, and it is going to
last exactly as long as it takes somebody with a rule to finish
measuring that ground.*

*So it is worth something and the something has a date on it.*

*And.*

He did not want the *and*.

*And the reason I cannot be glad of it is that there is no version of
this where I get to say what happened.*

*There are four of them and I am the only person alive who was there,
and it is going into a book somewhere this week as five, or six, and as
a man, and I cannot go up to any one of these people and say the number.*

*Miyo went ninety yards and there is going to be a line about that in a
briefing, and there is not going to be a line anywhere that says she
called it and put her hand up first, and that she said WAIT, and that
the reason she is ninety yards up a road is that she came through eleven
feet of standing points to get to a boy with a hole in his foot.*

*Nobody is ever going to write that down.*

*I am the whole of the record and I do not get to be it.*

---

He stood there rather longer than he had meant to.

Then he went back down the lane and bought bread, because it was three
days since he had bought anything and because a boy who does not buy
food in a market is a boy who has money he is saving or no money at all,
and both of those are questions.

The bread was better than Sunaba's. He noticed that and then he was
angry about noticing it, in a low uncomplicated way that he did not
examine.

*Right.*

*The road at Sakabe is shut either end and I am north of it and going
north, so that is not a thing I have to solve today.*

*What I have to solve today is the third of the fifth month, in this
town, on that quay.*

*One boat. No master, no cargo, no destination, and no dues.*

*And I have learned exactly one thing since Sunaba and I am going to use
it before I use anything else.*

**Find out what the book was for.**

He ate the bread walking, along the top of the town, where he could see
the whole flat brown road of water behind the bar and the stone quay on
the north side of it with four boats on it and a crane.

*The dues book is for dues. So the boat is not in it.*

*The harbour office is for the coast, and the coast goes to Kiri, and I
am not going into another one of those as long as I live.*

He got to the end of the top road and stood where the ground fell away
and looked at the quay for about six minutes.

*So what else does a boat need.*

*Act 1, month 5, the fourteenth. The yard at Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age
ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,720 words.*

---

Her father had called the mule Genzō, after the man he bought it off, so
that he would remember.

That was the whole joke and he had told it about nine hundred times, to
everybody, including to people who had heard it, including to Genzō the
man, once, at the shrine, at a funeral, which her mother had brought up
twice a year ever since.

Uzuki stood in the yard on the fourteenth with her hand through the
halter and thought about that and did not find it funny and did not find
it anything else either.

*It's just a thing that's in my head. It'll be in my head after.*

---

Shimoda came at about nine and he did not come straight to the point,
which she had expected, and he did not go all round it either, which she
had not.

He said good morning to her mother and he said it to her, separately,
looking at her, which almost nobody did. Then he stood and looked at the
mule for a while with his hands behind him.

"How old's he now?"

"Fourteen," said her mother.

"Fourteen." He went to the head and put his thumb in and looked at the
teeth, and Uzuki held the halter and the mule let him, because the mule
let anybody do anything. "Fourteen. Right."

He went down the near side and picked up each foot in turn and looked at
it and put it down. He did the off side. He put his hand flat on the
back and pressed and watched the head. He stood away and had the mule
walked six paces and back, and Uzuki walked him, and Shimoda watched the
feet and not the head.

Then he stood in front of her mother and told her what he thought, all of
it, in order, out loud, in the yard, with the mule standing there.

"He's sound. He's not lame and he's not going to go lame this year on
those feet, they're good feet, your husband did them. He's fourteen,
which is not old for a mule but it's not young, and I'd get five year out
of him and the last two of those he'd be a pack animal and not a shaft
animal. He'll not back. That's not a fault in him, it's that nobody ever
made him, and I'm not going to make him at fourteen. He's quiet with a
child on his head, which is worth something to me because I've got two.
He eats a lot. That's the shoulder — big through there, he was bred for
more than he's ever done."

He stopped.

"Six," he said.

*Six.*

Her mother said nothing for a second and then said, "Kiyoshi paid eleven
for him."

"Kiyoshi paid eleven for him nine years ago and he was five," said
Shimoda. "And Kiyoshi paid over the odds, and I'd say so if he was
standing here, and he'd have laughed and said he knew."

Her mother looked at the ground.

"He would," she said.

"Six," said Shimoda. "I'll not go seven and I'll not come down to five if
you say five, because it's six. You can put him in front of Ōgi market
and you might get seven and you'd pay a man to walk him and you'd stand
about all day and you'd not be sure of it."

*He isn't cheating us.*

She turned that over and over while they finished it, and it kept coming
out the same way, and she hated it more each time it came out.

*He isn't. He's told her the bad and the good in the same voice and he
put the child bit in himself and that's the bit that put it up not down,
and nobody made him say it.*

*This is what a mule is worth. That's all this is.*

---

He counted it out on the top of the wall. Six flat coins in a row, and
her mother picked them up one at a time and did not put them in her
apron until she had all six in her hand.

*Six.*

*The rent's three a month.*

*Two months.*

*My father worked that animal for nine years and it's two months.*

She stood with the halter and did the other sum on top of it without
meaning to, because it did itself.

*And the hundred with the line under it is thirty-three months.*

---

"Can I ask you something," she said.

Both of them looked at her. Her mother's face did a thing.

"Go on," said Shimoda.

"What's a house like ours worth. To rent."

There was a pause of about the length it takes to decide not to look at
somebody's mother.

"Three," said Shimoda.

"Is that right or is that what we pay."

"It's both," he said. "Kuriya's not skinning you. He's on the low side
for the shed, if anything, because the shed's his and it's falling in and
he knows it. If you went to Ine's brother for the one by the mill you'd
pay three and a half and you'd be nearer the road."

*So that door's shut as well.*

She had been holding that one for about nine days. It had been quite a
good door. It had had a whole shape to it — that Kuriya was charging them
too much because her father was dead, and that if she found that out and
said it to him in front of somebody he would have to stop.

*And it isn't true. He's charging us what it costs.*

*You can't shame somebody out of the going rate.*

---

"One more."

"Uzuki," said her mother.

"It's all right," said Shimoda.

"What do you pay somebody to do stones."

He put his hands behind him again.

"Depends who."

"Me."

Her mother said her name again, differently, and Uzuki did not turn round,
because she had got this far and she was not going to have got this far
for nothing.

Shimoda looked at her for a bit.

*He's deciding whether to lie to me.*

*Go on then. Do it and I'll know.*

"A man does a day for a quarter and his dinner," he said. "A boy your
size does a day for a fortieth and no dinner, and if he's good and he
comes back and I know his face, a thirtieth. That's not me. That's
everybody from here to Iwasa's and it doesn't move."

"A fortieth."

"A fortieth."

"Why."

"Because there's eleven of you," said Shimoda, "and one field."

---

She stood in the yard with the halter in her hand after he had finished
with her mother and the mule was still standing there because nobody had
taken him yet.

*A fortieth.*

*Forty days to make one.*

*The hole's three and a half a month.*

*A hundred and forty days.*

*In thirty.*

She did that one twice because she could not believe it had come out that
badly the first time, and it came out the same, and she stood there with
her ears going hot.

*Right.*

*So it's not that. It was never going to be that. Fine.*

*It's still something. A fortieth is a fortieth and forty of them is one
and one is one, and I'd rather have one than nothing, and anybody who
says different has never not had one.*

*So I'll do the days. I'll do every day there is. And it won't be enough
and I'll do them anyway because the alternative is sitting in the house
being ten.*

---

"He's not going to like the shed," she said.

"He'll like the shed," said Shimoda. "It's brick and it's got a door on
it."

"He gets his head over ours and looks in the kitchen."

"Does he."

"Every morning. He does it at about six and if nobody comes he does it
again at about seven."

Shimoda took the halter off her, gently, the way you take a thing off
somebody who is not going to give it to you.

"I'll leave the top of the door open," he said.

"He won't know where to look."

"He'll work it out."

"He's not clever."

"He doesn't have to be clever," said Shimoda. "He has to be a mule in a
yard, and there'll be a yard, and there'll be a wall over the top of the
door, and in about four days he'll be putting his head over it at six in
the morning at somebody else's kitchen and being disappointed, and in
about a fortnight he'll have stopped, and that's not a sad thing, that's
just what a fortnight is."


*Don't.*

*Don't be like this. It's much easier if you're not like this.*

"Thank you," she said.

---

He took him out of the gate and down the lane and the mule went the way
he went everywhere, at his own speed, with his ears doing whatever they
did, and he did not look back because mules do not, and Uzuki did not
go to the gate.

She stood in the middle of the yard.

*It's bigger.*

*That's what it is. The yard's bigger. That's the whole of what's
happened. Some money's come in and an animal's gone out and the yard's
bigger and in two months the money will be gone and the yard will still
be bigger.*

Her mother had gone in. Her mother had gone in while the mule was still
in the lane, and had not said anything, and Uzuki knew exactly why and
was not going to go in after her about it.

She got the flour bag out from under the stone by the step.

It had the ninety on it in one column — twenty, twenty-two, nineteen,
twenty-four, twenty-four, twenty-six, and on and on, and *31 good* at
the bottom of it — and it had nothing else on it at all.

She turned it over and started a second column on the back, and put
nothing in it yet, because there was nothing in it yet.

Then she put the date at the top of it, the way her father had put the
date on the top of everything, including things that did not need one,
including the inside of the shed door where he had written the day he
hung it.

*Fourteenth.*

*Right.*

*Act 1. A room, and then another room. A boy, age twelve. POV: the boy.
~1,620 words.*

The door opened four times, and it had never opened four times.

Water and bowl. Bucket. Bowl going back. That was three and that was
every day and it had been three every day since before the count broke.

The fourth time there was no bowl and no bucket and there were two of
them, and one of them had a cloth.

---

*Washed.*

*Second time.*

*The last time was after the man with the book.*

He stood and let them do it and did not help and did not get in the way,
because he had worked out a long time ago that helping made it take
longer, since they had to stop and see what he was doing.

The left one was the same left one and he was still worse.

*So washing comes before.*

*Last time washing came after. Warm food, more in the bowl, blanket,
washed. This time it is only washing and it is on its own.*

*So it is before.*

He did not know what it was before. He put it down as a thing that
existed and had no other side to it yet, which was most of what he had.

They took the cloth away. They did not go out.

---

They took him through the door.

He had not been through the door since a number he had stopped counting,
and the passage was longer than it was in his head, and it went up, and
he had not known it went up.

*Nine. Twenty-two. It turns. Thirty-one, thirty-two —*

He lost it at about forty because there was light at the end of it, real
light, coming down and not along, and his eyes hurt, and one of them put
a hand between his shoulder blades and moved him on.

*Start again. One.*

He got another nineteen and then there was a door and it was open.

---

There was a chair and a table and a lamp on the table, which was silly,
because there was already light.

The room was above the ground, and he did not look at the window,
because he had decided between the door and the chair that he was not
going to look at the window, and he did not go back over why.

They put him in front of the table. They did not put him in the chair.
Then they went and stood against the wall behind him and did not say
anything at all for the whole of it.

---

The man came in and he did not bring the smell of the passage in with
him.

That was the first thing and it was the only thing for a while. Everyone
who came in brought it — the wet in the stone and the bucket and the
cold — because they had come along it, and it came with them, and it went
out when they went out.

He came in out of the same passage and he did not have it on him.

*He is quiet on the floor.*

His shoes did not do anything. The keepers' feet did a thing on stone and
this man's did nothing at all, and he crossed the whole room and put a
book on the table and it did not make a sound either.

He sat down in the chair.

"Good morning," he said.

---

The boy did not say anything, because nothing had been asked.

The man waited. Then he said, "That was a greeting and not a question.
You are quite right."

He opened the book.

"How old are you?"

"I don't know."

*He wrote that.*

*Two.*

"Do you know what month it is?"

"No."

*He wrote that as well.*

*Three.*

"Can you put one out for me?"

He put one out.

It was the right forearm and it was the place where the wood went and he

The man did not get up and did not come round the table and did not put

He looked at it for a long time.

"Ah," he said, and wrote.

*Four.*

"Does it grow, or does it come out of you already made?"

The boy did not understand the question and said so, which was a thing he
had not done in two years, and nothing happened to him.

The man rephrased it, twice, and neither time did he sound as though
rephrasing it was a cost.

"Is it there before I ask for it," he said, "or do you make it when I
ask?"

"I make it."

*He wrote that.*

*Five.*

"Every time?"

"Yes."

*Six.*

"Can you make it longer than that?"

"I don't know. Nobody has wanted it longer than that."

*He wrote both.*

---

*Seven.*

"Can you put one out somewhere else?"

The boy stood with his arm out and the bone out of it and did not answer.

*Somewhere else.*

*Nobody has ever asked for somewhere else. It is the arm. It is that
place on the arm because that is the place the wood goes.*

*I do not know if I may.*

He had never asked anybody anything. There was nothing to ask about,

it where he had a say in what came in.

He heard himself say, "May I?"

And the man said, "Yes, of course."

---

He put one out of the back of his left hand.

It came up between the two middle knuckles and it hurt more than the arm
did, and it was smaller, and it was not straight, and he looked at it and
had no idea at all whether it was right.

Nobody said anything. The keepers did not say anything.

*That is the first thing I have ever done in this building that was mine.*

*They asked and I picked where.*

He did not have anywhere to put that, so he stood with his hand out and
waited.

"Ah," said the man. And wrote, for longer than the other times.

---

*Eight.*

"Does that hurt?"

Nobody had ever asked him anything about himself.

They had asked him to stand up and to hold still and to put his arm out
and to be quiet, and those were not questions even when they had the
shape of one. This had the shape of one and was one.

"Yes," he said.

*Nine.*

"Which one hurts more?"

"The hand."

"By a lot?"

"Yes."

He wrote all of it.

---

*Ten.*

"How long does it take to come back?"

The boy stood.

*Come back.*

*From where.*

*Nothing has ever gone.*

"I don't know," he said.

The man looked up from the book.

It was the only time he did that and it was not unkind and it was not
anything the boy could read at all, and it lasted about as long as it
takes to put a bowl down.

"Nobody has taken one," said the man.

"No."

"Ah," he said. And did not write.

---

"Would you break that one off for me?"

*Would you.*

not a shape at all. It had a gap in it where he was supposed to go.

He put his right hand over the back of his left and he broke it off.

---

It was worse than anything that had ever happened to him.

It was worse than the wood and worse than the wall and worse than the
long time when the count broke, and it went up his arm and stopped his
breath and put something behind his eyes, and he stood absolutely still
and did not make any noise at all.

*Do not make a noise.*

*A noise makes the visit longer.*

He held it out on his flat palm with the blood coming up in the two

whitish and it was his.

The man leaned forward and looked at it on the boy's hand and did not
take it and did not touch him.

He watched the boy's face for a while instead.

Then he wrote.

---

*And then one more, after he had closed the book.*

"Who taught you?"

"Nobody."

He had already closed the book. He opened it again and wrote that one
very briefly, and closed it, and stood up.

"Thank you," he said.

---

They took him back down the passage and it went down and it was shorter
and he did not count it.

The bolt went over.

He sat on the mat with his left hand held in his right and the two
knuckles going and he had the whole of it laid out and he went through it
in order, twice.

*Eleven. He wrote after nine.*

*He was in the room for about four hundred.*

*He said good morning and he said thank you and he said of course.*

He stopped on that one and could not get past it, because there was
nowhere to put it and no other one like it to put it with.

*That is the first time.*

*I have not got a number for that, because it is one.*

He put his hand round his hand.

*It went well.*

*I answered all of them and I did the thing that was asked, and the hand
one was mine.*

*They are going to use me.*

*Act 1, month 5, the sixteenth. The north quay, Nezu. Tadayoshi Ruri,

It took him three days to work out where to stand, which was two days
longer than it should have taken and he knew it while it was happening.

He spent the thirteenth on the top road doing the quay with his eyes and
getting nothing, because a quay from four hundred yards up a hill is a
picture and pictures do not have books in them. He spent the fourteenth
going along the front pretending to be somebody's errand and got as far
as knowing where the crane was and which of the four sheds was locked.

*Which is the same mistake. It is the boat again. I am looking for the
boat and the boat is not there and has not been there since the third of
last month, and I am going to go on looking for it because it is the
thing I want.*

*Stop looking for the thing you want.*

*Six sail it.*

*I want to know who the other two were.*

fifty-six days, and heard a professional arrive independently at the
exact finding he had made in a harbour office on the twenty-sixth of the
fourth month with a day book open in front of him.

*That is the same finding.*

*He got it off four years of losing people and I got it off giving Ide my
own name in one second because I cannot lie, and it is the same finding,
and there is nobody else in the world who has said a true thing to me
since the fifth of the fourth month.*

*He is real.*

*That is how I know he is real. Not the riverbed and not the sitting
down. It is that he has got my finding.*

"All right," he said.

And he knew, while he was saying it, in about a second and a half, that
he was not going to do it.

---

He worked out afterwards — much later, years later — that he had known
before the man finished the sentence, and that everything after that was
building the reason.

The reason was this: *the book is not a record of postings. The book is
about her. Those are two different things and I can keep one and not the
other.*

He looked at that for about as long as it takes to breathe out.

*That is not true.*

*If a man takes that book off me on a road, he does not stop at the part
that is about her. He gets forty-four entries and he gets a hand he can
match and he gets every place I have stood since the fifth of the fourth
month, and the fact that I was writing about my mother does not make it
less of a list.*

*I know that.*

*I know it completely.*

He said nothing.

*I am going to do it anyway.*

*I have got one thing that goes all the way back and it is nine lines
about a riverbed and it is forty-four numbered pages and it is the only
object in the world that says any of it happened, and if I stop writing
it, then in a year there is no difference between me and somebody who
never had a family at all.*

**He did not, that night or ever, write down that he had decided to go on
writing things down.**

---

"Last thing," said the man. "What do you do?"

"What?"

"For the sheet. What do you do."

Tadayoshi told him.

He told him about the four seals and about a second and a quarter. He
told him about courses and what a note was and why two courses cost more
than twice one, and about grit, and about the difference between grit and
a floor, and about how far the under-hand had to be off the material and
what happened if it was wrong by an inch. He told him about the tin and
what was in it and how long a third of a tin lasted at eleven feet. He
told him about tapered points, tips up, a hand's width apart, and how
long a stand of them held a walking man, and how long it held a running
one, which was nothing at all.

It took about four minutes and he did not know how to make it shorter,
and the man let him get to the end of it without moving.

Then he took out a sheet and a stub of pencil and wrote one word on the
top of it.

*Kui.*

**The stake.**

---

*He has written the fences.*

*I told him about fences for four minutes and I told him nothing about
the other thing at all, and he did not ask, and I did not decide not to
say it. My mouth did that.*

*I have not lied. I answered the question that was asked and every word
of it was true.*

*And there is a man in this yard who has now got a word for me, and it is
made out of six minutes in a channel where I did not hurt anybody, and it
is not made out of the ninety yards.*

*I did not choose it.*

*And I am not going to correct it.*

He stood in the yard and felt the size of that, and put it down, and
found that it went in the book quite easily and did not fight him at all,
which was the part that stayed with him.

---

The man gave him the sheet.

It had four lines on it and a place and a date eleven days off, and it
was a job, and Tadayoshi read it twice and then looked at it a
third time for a reason he could not immediately name.

*That is not his hand.*

The word on the top — *Kui* — was pencil, and it was the man's, and it
was a bad hand, fast and flat and made by somebody who wrote a great deal
and did not care.

The four lines under it were ink. They were ruled off at the left. The
character for *the twelfth* had a foot on it that came back under itself,
which is a thing about nine people in a hundred do, and the whole of it
sloped about a degree and a half to the right and did not vary anywhere.

*Somebody made that. Somebody sat down and made that.*

"Whose is this?"

The man was already getting up off the shaft.

"That's the ticket's," he said.

*Act 1, month 6, the twenty-seventh. The kitchen at Nakadō. Nozaki
Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~2,100 words.*

---

The box came out on the twenty-seventh because the twenty-seventh was
when the box came out, and Uzuki sat down on the other side of the table
without being asked to and put her hands flat on it.

Her mother did not tell her to go away.

That was new. It had been new for about three weeks and Uzuki had
noticed the exact evening it started, which was the evening after the
first quarter of a ryō came into the house, and had not said anything
about it, because you do not say anything about a door that has opened.

She read the box upside down, which she was much better at than she had
been in the fourth month.

*In: Iwasa, one and a half. Kuriya — no. Not Kuriya. Just a half, and no
name on it, and it's mine, and she's written it with nothing next to it.*

*And the stones. Nine days at a fortieth. She's put them all together as
one line: 9/40.*

*She's counted them.*

She sat with that for a second because it was, without any question, the
best thing that had happened to her since the fourth month.

*She's counted them. She didn't have to. Nobody would ever have known.*

*Out: rent three. Meal. Oil. Tazu, once, and it's a small one.*

*And H.*

---

"What's H?"

Her mother went on writing for about four seconds.

Then she put the pen down in the tray and did not pick it up again, and
Uzuki understood that she had asked the only question that was actually
in the room and that her mother had been waiting for it since the
fourteenth of the fifth month.

"It isn't a letter," said Sae.

"It's a letter. It's written there."

"It's where I'd put a name," said her mother, "if I'd decided."

*Oh.*

She sat very still.

*Ten weeks.*

*Ten weeks of writing a mark in a book instead of a name, twice a month,
in her own house, where nobody but her ever looks at it.*

*She wasn't hiding it from me. She was hiding it from the page.*

"Have you decided?"

"Yes."

"When."

"About the eleventh."

*The eleventh.*

*Sixteen days ago.*

*And she's gone on writing H for sixteen days.*

"Shimoda."

not known at all.

*The mule.*

*He came for the mule.*

*He came into our yard on the fourteenth and he told us what a mule is
worth, item by item, in front of me, and he was fair about it, and he

that as well, and he'd already given her a hundred.*

*A hundred came in before he ever looked at the mule.*

*He did the mule properly anyway. He didn't have to do the mule properly.
He could have given her eight for it and she'd have taken it, and he did

*And that's worse.*

*I don't know why that's worse but it's worse.*

---

"Ask," said her mother.

"Why."

"Because there isn't anything else." Sae put both hands flat on the
table, the way Uzuki had, which neither of them noticed. "I've had it out
every night for ten weeks. There's the rent and there's what's in and
there's what goes out, and I've done it about two hundred times, and
there is no arrangement of those three that gets us to next spring.
There's no cousin. There's no work in this village for a woman of forty
who's never done anything but this house. And Ōgi's four days and a
kitchen in Ōgi means you go and I don't, or I go and you go into
somebody's house here as a girl, and you know what that is, and I'm not
having it."

"I'm earning."

"You are."

"I've got a half a month off Kuriya and I've got the field in the autumn

She stopped, because she had run out of the parts she had prepared.

*She's not wrong.*

"All right," she said.
