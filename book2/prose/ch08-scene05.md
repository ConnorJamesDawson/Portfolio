# Chapter 8, Scene 5 — Four For Four

*Act 1, month 8, the tenth to the twenty-second. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki,
age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,790 words.*

---

The fifth one was on the tenth.

She lay in the dark afterwards and did the gap before she did anything
else, because doing the gap was the only part of it she had any control
over at all.

*Sixth to the tenth is four.*

*Nine, five, three, four.*

*So it isn't going down in a straight line. It's going down and wobbling,
which is what everything does, and four after three is not it getting
better, it's — *

She stopped and made herself say the honest version.

*It's about a week and a half of my life left before it's every other
day.*

---

She started on the eleventh and the cover took her about an hour to
build.

The sacks were in the shed and there were about forty of them and eleven
were split and the rest were going, and they had been going for two years,
because sack-mending is the job that everybody in a farming house knows
about and nobody does, since it takes four hours and saves about a
sixteenth and there is always something with a shorter answer.

She brought nine of them into the kitchen on the eleventh at about seven
and put them on the floor by the range and got the needle out.

"What's that?"

"Sacks."

Shimoda looked at them.

"Those have wanted doing since I had the shed done."

"I know," said Uzuki. "I'll do a few a night. There's no point doing them
all at once, it's not that kind of job."

*There.*

*That's the whole of it. That's the door and the hinge and the reason, and
it took eleven words.*

He was pleased. That was the part she had not planned for and had to hold
her face about: he was pleased, and he said so, and at some point later
that week he told Ine's brother at the water that the girl had done the
sacks, and Ine's brother said something about how you couldn't get a lad
to do that.

Her mother was pleased too. Her mother said *that's good of you, love,*
and meant it, and Uzuki said nothing back at all, because there was
nothing she could have said that would not have been either a lie or the
truth.

---

Then she sat in that kitchen every night for eleven nights.

Seven till about half ten, and later when she could get away with it. Nine
sacks a night going down to four when her hands got bad, and she did them
properly, because a badly mended sack comes back and a sack that comes
back is a reason to stop her doing them.

And she was *there*. That was the whole of the mechanism and there was
nothing clever in it. She sat on a stool by the range with a needle and
about eight pounds of sacking in her lap where both of them could see
her, and she was awake, and she was ten, and she was looking at her
hands.

The eleventh: nothing.

The twelfth: nothing.

The thirteenth: nothing.

The fourteenth: nothing.

---

She had it on the fifteenth and it took her about four seconds and she
put the needle down.

*Four for four.*

*I'm the reason.*

She sat on the stool in the empty kitchen at eleven at night with a sack
across her knees.

*That's not four for four. That's not even a number yet. Four's nothing.*

*Yes it is. Four's four and it's four in a row and the gaps before it were
nine, five, three and four, and I have just done four nights in a row with
nothing in them.*

*And I did not do anything. I did not say anything and I did not stop
anything and I have not once been in the room when it — I have sat on a
stool with a needle.*

*That's all it takes. That's the whole of what it takes.*

*Somebody being in the room.*

---

She got up and went to her own room and lay down on the mat, and she was
about eight seconds from asleep when it arrived, and she went cold all the
way through.

*If sitting up stops it.*

*Then going to bed doesn't.*

She lay in the dark with her eyes open.

*No. That's not — that's not the same thing. Not doing a thing isn't doing
a thing.*

*Say it properly. Don't be soft with it.*

She said it properly.

*If I am in that kitchen it does not happen, and I know that now, and
tonight I found it out.*

*So every night from tonight, when I get up off this mat and go and sit on
that stool, that's me stopping it.*

*And every night I don't —*

She got up.

She got up and went back into the kitchen and got another sack out and sat
on the stool until about one in the morning, and her hands were finished
by half twelve and she went on anyway.

---

It cost her more than she had.

She had known it was going to and she had costed it wrong, because she had
costed it in hours and it did not come out of hours, it came out of
everything.

Her hands were the first thing. Sack-mending is a needle through folded
hemp four hundred times an evening and it is the exact set of muscles the
palm is, and by the eighteenth she was going to Kuriya's with hands that
had already done a night's work and had not had a day off in a week.

The eighteenth went badly. She knew it at about the fortieth and there was
nothing she could do except not push, and she did not push, and she took
her hands off at ninety and counted the forty seconds and it was about
half of what it should have been and both of them knew it.

Kuriya did not say anything for a bit.

Then he said, "You're tired."

"I'm all right."

"You're grey. What are you doing at night?"

"Sacks," said Uzuki. "There's about forty of them and they've wanted doing
for two years and I said I'd do them, and I'm doing about nine a night,
and I'm not going to not finish them now I've said."

It came out completely smoothly and it was completely true and it was, in
every way that mattered, a lie, and she noticed none of those three things
at the time.

"Do six," said Kuriya.

"Nine."

"Do six and come Thursday as well and I'll pay you the same, and that's
not charity, it's that I'd rather have three-quarters of you three times
than half of you twice."

*He's giving me a night off.*

*He's giving me a night off and he doesn't know he's giving me a night
off, and the night off is the thing I can't take.*

"All right," she said.

---

The nineteenth: nothing. The twentieth: nothing. The twenty-first:
nothing.

Eleven nights.

She wrote nothing down about any of it, because there was nothing to
write; a column of nothings is not a column. She kept it in her head with
the other one.

*Eleven.*

*And the gaps were nine, five, three, four.*

*And it's been eleven.*

---

On the twenty-second she went to bed at nine.

She did not fall asleep at the table and she was not sent and nothing
happened to her. She stood in the kitchen at about ten to nine with a sack
in her hands and her eyes going, and she looked at the stool, and she made
a decision.

*One night.*

*I've done eleven. My hands are finished and I've got Kuriya tomorrow and
if I do tomorrow the way I did the eighteenth he's going to start asking
again and I have not got a second answer.*

*One night. It's eleven days. It was nine, five, three, four and then
eleven, and it doesn't go from eleven to one.*

She put the sack down and said goodnight and went to bed and was asleep in
under a minute.

---

It happened that night.

She came up out of it at the chair — not the sound of the chair, she never
heard the chair, she came up out of sleep into the middle of the after
part, the low level voice going on, the same as before, four feet the
other side of a wall.

She lay absolutely still on her mat in the dark.

*No.*

*No, I went to bed. I went to bed at nine and I went to bed on purpose,
and it's eleven days, it doesn't go from eleven to —*

*Get up.*

*Get up. Get up and go in. You said at ninety and you didn't, and this
time you don't even have to decide, this time you already know it works,
you know it works because you've done it eleven times —*

She did not get up.

She lay on the mat with her hands over her face and she did not get up,
and it stopped, and somebody moved about in the kitchen, and the house
went quiet in the ordinary way.

---

She got the bag out afterwards and wrote the mark.

Then she sat on the floor with her back against the brick and did the
thing she could not not do, and it was the worst arithmetic of her life
and it took her about a minute and it was correct.

*Tenth to the twenty-second is twelve.*

*Nine, five, three, four, twelve.*

*Twelve.*

*It was coming down and it was coming down fast and then it went to
twelve, and the only thing that changed was that a girl sat on a stool in
a kitchen with a bag of sacking for eleven nights.*

*I bent it.*

*I bent it by nine days.*

She put her head back on the wall.

*So there's a fifth thing on this bag now and I'm not going to write it
down.*

*The ninety is me. The money is me. The days is me. The marks are her.*

*And the fifth one is the gaps, and the gaps are me as well.*

**I am in it. I am one of the things that decides how often it happens,
and I found that out by sitting on a stool.**

*And it means the eleven nights weren't me being good.*

*They were me finding out what the price is.*

She sat on the floor of her own room for a long time.

*And I have got to pay it every night for the rest of my life, and I am
ten, and I lasted eleven.*
