# Chapter 1 — Two Courses

*Book two. Act one, months 1–2. Ten scenes, 16,900 words.*




*Act 1, month 1. Shioiri, the workshop on the third course. Tadayoshi
Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~2,100 words.*

---

The frame was older than anybody in the compound and it did nothing at
all, and he set it on the bench in front of him anyway, because the
kitchen window looked down onto the workshop door.

It was copper, and it had been beaten thin by somebody dead, and it was
the shape of a small open hand with the fingers spread. You laid the
grit in the palm of it. You put your own hand under. The clan called it
the lattice and had called it the lattice for four hundred years, and
his great-aunt could recite eleven lines about it that nobody had ever
been able to explain to him.

He put three fingers of grit into it out of the tray and levelled them
off with the side of his thumb.

*Six done. Nine to do. Two hours of light, less if the rain comes back.*

The rain came back.

---

The grit was from the inlet, off the third bar down where the sand ran
coarse, and it had been sieved twice and dried on the stove and it was
the only material in the trade that cost the family nothing. That was
the whole reason the Ruri had lasted at Shioiri and not somewhere with
better weather. You could stand on your own doorstep at low water and
shovel up the raw stuff for a year's work, and the tide put more back
every day, and nobody had ever thought to tax the beach.

He put his left hand under the frame and his right hand up.

Ram. Snake. Ox. Hare.

The seals were the part nobody outside the family ever asked about,
which he had worked out was because nobody outside the family thought
about the trade at all — they thought about the stone, which arrived
finished, in a shop, on a tray, and did not appear to have been made by
anybody. Four seals, in that order, held for about a second and a
quarter. He had timed it against the drip off the workshop eave, which
came at eleven to the minute in steady rain, and a second and a quarter
was near enough five drips to be worth knowing.

His hands were cold. They were always cold from about the tenth month
to about the fourth, and there was nothing to be done about it, because
the one thing you could not do a seal in was a glove.

The grit went white, and then went clear, and then stopped being grit.

---

*One.*

He laid the first course fast, because the first course was only ever
the shape. It came out of the grit in about the time it took to think
about it — a drop, tapered, the length of the last joint of his little
finger, with the eye at the narrow end already open because closing an
eye afterwards was a job for somebody with better tools than a bench and
a knife.

It sat in the palm of the frame and it had one note in it and it would
have shattered if you had breathed on it in the right key.

*Two.*

The second course was the difficult one, and it was difficult for a
reason that would have sounded to anybody in the world like a boast, so
he had never said it to anybody in the world.

The second course was difficult because it had to be worse than he
could make it.

He knew what the family's grade looked like. It looked like this: two
courses, laid by somebody feeding chakra through a hand and a copper
plate, so that the feed came out uneven the way water comes out of a
squeezed hose uneven — a little fast, a little slow, thick here and thin
a quarter of a turn round. That unevenness was what everybody in the
Land of Water had been buying for four hundred years without knowing it
was unevenness. It was what made a Ruri drop look like a Ruri drop. Hold
one up in a shop and it had a sort of grain in it, a rippling, a thing
in the light.

The grain was a fault. It had always been a fault. He had worked that
out when he was eight and it had taken him about six weeks, and the six
weeks had mostly been spent not believing it.

So now he laid the second course and put the fault in on purpose.

That was the part that took the time. You could not simply be careless,
because careless came out wrong in the wrong way — blotched, or soft,
and a soft drop failed in the tin on the road to Ōura and then his
father had to stand at a counter and be given eleven for twelve. You had
to be careless *evenly*. You had to make the feed stutter at intervals
and make the intervals irregular and make the irregularity look like a
hand rather than a decision.

It was, he had decided some time ago, the hardest thing he did.

---

He held it up when it was done and turned it against the window light.

Grain. A rippling in it, coming and going as it turned, exactly the way
it was supposed to.

Then he put it down and ran his thumb along the taper, up and back,
which was a thing his hands did without asking him and which he had
noticed himself doing perhaps a hundred times and had never once
managed to stop, because it told him something and nothing else did. A
piece with two courses had a note. You could not hear it with your ear.
You could feel where it lived, a sort of thinness under the skin at one
place on the taper, and if a man with a hammer ever found that place the
piece would come apart in three or four pieces and not more.

The place was where it should be.

*Seven.*

---

The tin was open on the bench beside him with the month's work in it,
and one of his mother's was on top, because he had put it there that
morning and he put one there every morning.

He was not supposed to think of it as a sample. He thought of it as a
sample.

It was a drop of the same size. She had made it on the Tuesday, and it
had grain in it, and it had two courses in it, and both of those things
were true of the one he had just put down, and the difference between
them was that hers had come out of her hands like that and his had been
built to.

He set them side by side on the wood, hers on the left because it always
went on the left.

*The taper on hers is a fraction fuller a third of the way down. Not
because she chose it. Because she pushes with the heel of the hand at
the start and gets tired of pushing.*

*I do not get tired.*

*So I have to.*

He picked his own back up and did four seconds of work on it that made
it, in one specific and invisible respect, worse.

Then he put it in the tin.

---

There had been a Tuesday two years ago, in the eighth month, when he had
come up from the workshop with a drop in his fist and run the whole way
to the kitchen because he had not been able to make himself walk.

He had laid it on the table in front of her and said, *Look at it, look
at it in the light, look at what happens when you turn it* — and she had
picked it up and turned it, and turned it again, and it had not done
anything at all, because there was nothing in it to do anything. No
grain. No rippling. Six courses laid so flat and so even that the light
went into it and came back out as light.

She had held it for a long time.

Then she had put it down on the table and closed her hand over it and
said: *Nobody sees this. Not your father. Not Meno. Nobody.*

He had asked why. He had been eight, and he had asked why the way he
asked everything, straight out and expecting an answer, because up until
that afternoon that had been what happened when he asked.

And she had said: *Because of my uncle.*

That was all of it. That was the whole of what he had ever been given.
He had gone and found out afterwards that there had been an uncle, that
his name had been Kongō, that he had been taken away by the village a
very long time ago and not brought back, and there the road ended,
because Meno would not talk about it and his father had not been born
into the family and his mother had said the one sentence and no others.

*A rule with no reasoning in it.*

He had not put it in those words at eight. He had put it in those words
about a year later and had been faintly, permanently annoyed by it ever
since.

He kept the rule. He had kept it for two years, every working day, nine
pieces at a time.

---

The door went.

"You're in the dark," his mother said.

"There is light for another twenty minutes."

"There is light for another six, and you'll ruin your eyes and then I'll
have two of us squinting." She came in shaking the rain off her sleeves.
She was not a big woman. She had never been a big woman; the trade did
not build you, it wore you at the ends, and her fingertips had gone
smooth years ago in the way that meant she could set a stone in a mount
by feel and could not tell a five-ryō piece from a ten without looking.

She stopped at the bench and looked into the tin, which she did every
evening, and which he had stopped being nervous about a long time ago.

Then she reached in and took one out.

It was his.

He watched her do it and did not move and did not say anything, and the
part of him that had spent four minutes that afternoon putting a
deliberate tiredness into a taper stood very still and waited to find
out whether four minutes had been enough.

She turned it over. She turned it against the last of the window.

"Hm," she said.

The rain went on the roof. Down the hill somebody was calling somebody
in for the evening, two courses down, a voice with the words worn off it
by distance.

"That's not a bad one," his mother said. She turned it once more, and
gave it a small approving push of the thumb along the taper, up and
back, and he watched his own hands' habit happen on the end of her arm
and understood, with a jolt that had nothing whatever to do with the
drop, where he had got it.

"I don't know what I did differently," she said.

And she put it back in the tin, on top, and wiped her hands on her
apron, and said something about the fire wanting bringing in, and went
out.

---

He sat on the stool for a while after that with his hands between his
knees for the warmth.

*She could not tell.*

*She held it and she turned it and she put her thumb on the exact place
where the note lives, and she could not tell, and she is the only person
alive who has been making these for thirty years.*

He got up and lit the lamp, because it was true about the light, and set
the two drops out again on the wood — hers on the left, because hers
always went on the left, and his beside it.

In the lamp they were the same object twice.

He picked them both up and put them both in the tin and could not
afterwards have said which was which, and there was no reason at all why
that should have mattered, because they were going to Ōura in three days
in the same tin to be sold by weight and grade to a man who bought by
weight and grade.

*Eight to do,* he thought. *Nine, if I count the one she liked.*

He counted it.

*Act 1, month 1. Nakadō, in the Land of Hot Water. Nozaki Uzuki, age
ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,560 words.*

---

"Thursday," Uzuki said.

"No."

"You haven't heard it yet."

"I've heard it four times." Her father put his hand flat on the top of
her head and pushed it forward over the bowl, which was how the
conversation was going to go from now on, because with your head over a
bowl you could argue but you could not win. "Chin down. You'll have it
in your eye and then it's my fault."

The water was already brown. It went on being brown for the whole first
month after a doing, and then it went sort of clay, and then by the end
of the second month it was nearly clear when he rinsed and that was how
you knew it was time again. She could have told anybody the schedule of
it. She had been having her hair done at that bowl since before she
could remember having it done, the way other people had been having
their nails cut.

"Thursday is Ōgi and back," she said, into the bowl. "That's eleven
miles and you take all day over it."

"I take all day over it because I stop at Fusano and there's a man at
Fusano who owes me for a barrel and I intend to be there long enough to
be a nuisance about it."

"I could do Ōgi in half the time."

"You could do Ōgi in half the time," her father agreed. "That's not the
argument you think it is."

Something cold went down behind her ear and she flinched and he said,
mildly, "Ah," and mopped it with his sleeve, and she thought — not for
the first time — that if her mother did this it would take twenty
minutes and be neat, and that she would rather have this.

He coughed.

He got it into his shoulder, away from her, and it went on a bit longer
than a cough wants to go on, and when it stopped he said "Chin down"
again in exactly the voice he had used before it, and she put her chin
down.

That cough had been in the house since about the harvest. It got worse
in the cold and better in the sun and everybody had said *winter* about
it so many times that the word had stopped meaning weather and started
meaning *don't*.

---

"Why do we do it?"

She asked it every time. She had established, around the age of seven,
that if you asked a question every single time you were eventually going
to catch an adult on a day when they were tired, and she had been
running the experiment on this one for three years without result, which
by her own reckoning meant her father was either very consistent or
never tired.

"Because red's a rich colour," he said, "and we're not rich people."

"That's not a reason."

"It's a perfectly good reason. A girl walks into Ōgi market with hair
like a lacquer box and every third person in the square wonders what
else her family's got. And what we've got is a mule and a bad roof."

"Nobody would think that."

"Everybody thinks that. It's what a market is."

"Hair is hair," Uzuki said, to the water.

"Mm," said her father, which was what he said when he had run out and
was not going to admit it, and he tipped the jug over the back of her
neck and the argument was over for another two months.

She had asked her grandfather twice, when she was small and he was
still alive, and got less than this. He had not even given her a reason
that was wrong. He had put his hand on her head, the same way, in the
same place, and said something about how it was a good colour and it
was not a good time for it, and then he had looked at the door for a
while and she had gone off to do something else, because she was six
and there was a cat.

She had thought about that a lot since. Not the cat.

---

Her father towelled her head roughly, the way you dry a dog, and said,
"Tuesday."

"What?"

"Tuesday you take the Ōgi run. On your own."

She came up out of the towel.

"With the little cart?"

"With the little cart, and the two parcels for the mill and the letter
for the man at the crossroads, and you will not take the shortcut over
Kannomori because I will know."

"How will you know?"

"Because you'll be back three hours early and lying about it."

She was already doing the arithmetic. Eleven miles was nothing. Eleven
miles was *nothing* — she had done eleven miles carrying a full sack for
the fun of it, up the long way, in the wet, and got back before he had
finished the loading he had told her would take him till noon. She was
the fastest thing in the house and everybody knew it and nobody had ever
yet been willing to make anything of it.

"On my own," she said.

"On your own. Don't look at me like that. It's a mill and a letter."

"It's not, though," Uzuki said. "Is it."

Her father looked at her for a second with the towel in his hand and
then made a noise that was mostly laugh and turned it into a cough about
halfway, and she watched him have it, and it went on a bit longer than
the last one, and he braced his knuckles on the table and got it done
and straightened up.

"No," he said. "It's not."

---

Her mother came in with the door banging behind her and the smell of the
outside on her, and stopped.

"You've done her hair."

"I have."

"You've done half her hair. There's a whole hand's worth behind her
right ear you've missed and it's going to be sitting there like a signal
flag by Friday."

"Then it's a good job we've got you."

"It's a good job you've got me," Sae Nozaki agreed, and put down what
she was carrying, and rolled her sleeves, and took the comb off him
without asking, and did the hand's worth behind the right ear in about
ninety seconds with her mouth in a straight line.

Her mother did it faster and neater and it hurt more and there was no
conversation in it at all, and Uzuki, who loved her, would not have
traded her father's version for it in a hundred years.

"There," said her mother.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me, thank the man who half did it."

"Thank you," Uzuki said to her father, gravely, and he bowed from the
waist, sitting down, which was a thing he did, and her mother told them
both they were idiots and went to see about the fire.

---

They ate late because they always ate late, at the table with the leg
her father had mended twice and would mend again rather than let anyone
else near it.

It was a good evening.

She would think about that afterwards for a long time — not that
anything happened in it, because nothing did. Her mother said the man at
Fusano was never going to pay for that barrel and her father said he
knew, and that being a nuisance was its own reward, and her mother said
that was the truest thing he had said all year. Uzuki told them about
Kannomori being passable now the stream was down and got told, by both
of them at once and in the same words, not to go over Kannomori.

Her father coughed twice more. Once in the middle of the meal and once
at the end.

The second time her mother got up to put more water on, and did not say
anything, and did not look at him, and was standing at the stove with
her back to the room for a bit longer than putting water on takes.

Uzuki noticed it and put it away somewhere and went on eating, because
she was ten and it had been a good evening and there was nothing in the
room that said it was the last of anything.

---

Later she went out to tip the bowl.

You did it in the ditch at the side, not on the path, because it went in
the grass and stained and her mother had opinions about it. She carried
it out with both hands and the water slopped and it was cold on her
wrists and the sky had cleared off after the rain the way it did inland,
all at once, with the stars very hard and small.

She stood there for a second with the bowl held out.

It looked like nothing in the dark. It looked like water.

She tipped it, and it went into the ditch and away, and by the time it
got to the corner of the wall it was just the ditch again, and the thing
that had been in it was somewhere out under the field with everything
else the house had ever put down a drain.

"Uzuki!"

"Coming."

She went in with the empty bowl banging against her leg, and shut the
door on the cold, and did not think about it again for two years.

*Act 1, month 1. Shioiri, the low house on the second course. Tadayoshi
Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,890 words.*

---

His father came back off the Ōura road on the Thursday with the mule and
an empty tin and a face he was holding still on purpose, and everybody
in the compound knew what the money was before a coin came out of the
bag, because the coming up the hill had been slower than it should have
been by a good four minutes.

*Nine minutes from the gate to the second course. It is normally five.
He walks slower when he is deciding how to say it.*

They ate first. That was the rule and nobody had ever written it down:
you did not open the bag at the table until the bowls were off it,
because Meno said money on a table with food on it was how families
learned to eat like creditors.

So they ate, eight of them, in a room that had been built for a great
deal more than eight.

---

You could see the arithmetic from the doorway if you knew what you were
looking at, and Tadayoshi had known what he was looking at since he was
about six.

Shioiri went up the hill in courses. Six of them, cut into the slope
above the inlet, each one a flat step wide enough for a row of houses
and a path, and the whole thing stepped up out of the water like — his
great-aunt's phrase, and she was not a woman given to phrases — like a
piece somebody had laid properly.

The first course flooded on spring tides and had nothing on it but the
grit sheds. The second and third had the workshops and the houses that
were still lived in. The fourth had two houses that were still lived in
and four that were not. The fifth and sixth had nothing lived in at all,
and their shutters had been closed, in most cases, by people Tadayoshi
had never met.

Eight people were keeping the rain out of thirty-one roofs.

They did not do it well. That was the thing nobody said at meals: they
did not do it *at all*, really, past a certain height. Above the fourth
course the moss had got into the ridge lines and two winters ago the end
house on the fifth had dropped its whole eastern eave into the path in
the night and made a noise that woke the entire family, and in the
morning they had gone up and looked at it, all eight of them, and come
down again, and nobody had said one word about it on the way down.

He had counted the tiles. There were about nine hundred of them and they
were still there.

---

"Right," said his father, and put the bag on the table, and Meno took
the last bowl off it.

Ruri Tadaharu was thirty-eight and had married in and was the only adult
in the compound whose name did not have a stone in it, and he counted
money the way he did everything on the road side of the business, which
was out loud, in stacks, with his thumb.

"Four dozen and one drops. Grade came out at two and a low three."

"A low three," said Meno.

"Kanaya's man made a face at the eleventh of the fourth dozen and I made
a face back and we agreed on a low three." He set the stacks out.
"Fourteen and a half."

There was a small silence.

"It was seventeen in the eighth month," said Hisui.

"It was seventeen in the eighth month because there was a wedding in
Sunaba and the whole coast wanted drops," his father said. "Fourteen and
a half is fourteen and a half."

Koishi, who was six, and who had got up onto the bench beside Tadayoshi
without anybody noticing because that was her method, leaned over the
table on both hands and looked at the stacks.

"Who wears them?" she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"The drops," said Koishi. "Who wears them, after."

"Kanaya buys them," her mother said.

"That's who buys them."

"That's — " Hisui stopped. "Well. Somebody. Somebody in Sunaba. They go
on from Sunaba."

"On to where?"

"On to anywhere, love. That's what a port is."

Koishi thought about this with her chin in her hands, apparently found
it inadequate, and looked at Tadaharu, who was the one who actually went
places.

"Where do they go?"

"I take them to a counter in Ōura," said his father, "and a man behind
the counter gives me money, and what he does with them after that is his
business, and if I started asking he'd start wondering why I wanted to
know." He put the last coin on the last stack. "That's the trade, poppet.
You don't follow it. You get paid."

*Filed,* thought Tadayoshi, who had never in his life heard that
question asked and was faintly annoyed that it had been asked by
somebody who was six.

*We do not know where our work goes. Nobody in this family has ever
known. Four hundred years and nobody has asked, because the man behind
the counter would wonder.*

He put it away with the other things he had no use for yet.

---

"Fourteen and a half," said Hisui, "and the fifth course lost another
eave in the storm."

"Hisui," said Meno.

"I'm saying the number. You can't be angry at a number."

"I can be extremely angry at a number, girl, I've had seventy-one years
of practice."

"Then be angry at it," said Hisui. "Because it's the ninth time I've put
it on this table and every time it's a bit smaller."

She was twenty-nine and she had Koishi's chin and she was the only one
of them, Tadayoshi had decided, who ever came into a room having already
decided what she was going to say in it.

"There is a house in Ōura," she said. "Rent. Two rooms and a yard. I've
been to see it twice. We could take the trade with us — it isn't the
hill, it's *us*, we could work in a yard in Ōura the same as we work in
a shed here, and we'd be a day and a half nearer the money and nobody in
Ōura has to keep the rain off thirty-one roofs."

"And the grit?" said Akagane.

"Carted."

"Carted," said Akagane, mildly. He was twenty and he had a piece of
copper in his hands, because he always had a piece of copper in his
hands, and he had not stopped working on it to speak. "That's a cost you
haven't costed."

"Then I'll cost it."

"You'd have to cost the water too."

"Then I'll cost the water."

"You've costed nothing," said Meno, "because you can't. You can't cost
what it is. This is where we are from."

"This is where thirty-two people *were* from," said Hisui.

---

The room got very quiet.

Down on the second course somebody's shutter was banging, which meant it
was one of the empty ones, which meant nobody was going to go and shut
it.

"Say the rest," said Tadaharu.

"Tadaharu — " said Mikage.

"No, she's not wrong, and everybody at this table has been letting her
be half-wrong for nine goes now because half-wrong is comfortable." His
father put both hands flat on the wood. "I married into this family. I'm
allowed one outsider's opinion a year and I'm spending it.

"You don't stay small because it's safe. You stay small because you got
frightened once, a long time ago, before any of us, and being frightened
turned into being careful and being careful turned into being *this* —
and now you've got a family that makes a worse piece than it can, sells
it for less than it's worth, to a man who makes a face at it, and calls
that surviving." He looked round the table. "It isn't. It's just slower."

"And what would you have us do," said Meno.

"Be worth something."

"People who are worth something," said Mikage, "get collected."

She said it flatly, without heat, the way you would report a tide table,
and she did not look up from her hands while she said it.

Tadaharu opened his mouth.

"Kenji," said Iwao.

---

Everybody turned.

His brother was leaning back on two legs of his stool with his arms
folded, thirteen years old and half a head taller than he had been in
the spring, and he had that look he got — the one that meant a thing had
arrived in his head and he had decided it was worth more to the room
than to him.

"Kanaya's man," said Iwao. "The one who makes the faces. His name's
Kenji."

"So?"

"So he made a face at the eleventh drop of the fourth dozen." Iwao
pointed at his father with two fingers. "And *you* made a face back. And
I want everybody at this table to imagine that. Two grown men. In a
shop. On a Tuesday. Pulling faces at a bead."

"It's a considered professional expression — "

"It's pulling faces at a bead."

"There is a *technique* to it — "

"Show us the face," said Iwao.

"I will not show you the face."

"Show us the face, Da."

And their father, who had come up the hill four minutes slow because he
had been deciding how to say fourteen and a half, put down the money and
showed them the face.

It was a very good face. Koishi screamed with laughter and fell sideways
into Tadayoshi's ribs and had to be set upright. Akagane put his copper
down for the first time all evening. Meno made a noise like a gate and
put her hand over her mouth, which was as far as Meno went.

Hisui did not laugh.

She sat with her hands in her lap and looked at the table for a moment
and then got up and started stacking the bowls, and that was the ninth
time, finished, without anybody having to finish it.

---

Afterwards Iwao caught him on the path outside and put a hand on the
back of his neck, which he did about four times a day and which
Tadayoshi had never once liked and had never once said anything about.

"You were doing the face," said Iwao.

"I do not have a face."

"You were doing the little one. The one where you're writing something
down." He squeezed, and let go. "What was it?"

*It was that Koishi asked where the drops go and not one person in this
family could tell her, and that I have been laying two courses for two
years so that a man in Ōura can make a face at a bead, and that Hisui is
right and everyone knows she is right and it will not matter.*

"Nothing," said Tadayoshi.

"Liar," said his brother, pleasantly, and went in.

---

The money stayed on the table until Meno swept it into the tin, because
that was also a rule: it sat out for one evening so that everybody had
looked at it.

Fourteen and a half, in four stacks and a loose one.

Tadayoshi went past it on his way up and counted the loose one on the
way, because it annoyed him not to, and it came to eleven.

*Act 1. A room. A boy, age twelve. POV: the boy. ~1,290 words.*

---

The door opened three times on an ordinary day.

Once for the water and the bowl. Once, later, for the bucket. Once at
the end, for the bowl going back, and after that it did not open again
and he could stop listening.

Three was ordinary. He had gone a long time on three.

Today it opened a fourth time before the bucket, and he was standing up
before he had decided to stand up, because four was not a number that
belonged to any day he knew.

He used it that night.

He set the lamp, and put three fingers of grit into the dished palm, and
levelled them off with the side of his thumb, and put his left hand
under — and had to stop, and adjust, because the balance was wrong.

Not wrong. Different. It was lighter and it sat further forward and the
rolled edge that took the pressure off the web of his thumb also, as it
turned out, moved his whole under-hand about a third of an inch nearer
the work than it had been for two years.

Which changed nothing about the feed, because he was not using the
frame to feed.

But it changed where his hand was.

So he laid the first drop and it came out with the note a fraction high,
and he broke it and put it back in the tray, and laid another, and that
one was right; and then he sat there for a while and worked out that he
was going to have to spend the next four or five days relearning the
distance from his under-hand to a piece of grit.

*Cost: four days.*

He got the ledger out and wrote it in the fourth column, in the narrow
one, because he had ruled it narrow and he was already finding that
narrow was where the true things went.

Then he put the old frame in the bench drawer, at the back, under the
rag, where it stayed.

He laid eight more drops before the lamp went, and every one of them had
grain in it.
