# Chapter 4, Scene 4 — Three Doors Up From the Bridge

*Act 1, month 4, the seventh. Kanaya's counter, Ōura. Tadayoshi Ruri,
age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,760 words.*

---

He went in at about ten in the morning and the bell hit the door.

There were two people at the counter and he stood behind them with the
tin under his arm and his hood up and looked at the floor, and it took
eleven minutes, and in the eleven minutes he heard Kenji say *good
morning* twice and *that's a nice one* once, and there was nothing at all
in any of it.

Then the door went and it was the two of them.

He put the tin on the counter and took his hood down.

---

Kenji did not say anything.

That was the first thing and it went into Tadayoshi like cold water,
because Kenji said something to everybody — Kenji had a greeting for a
man buying a lace and a greeting for a woman looking at a tray she was
not going to buy from — and what he did when a Ruri came into his shop
was *Ruri-san*, always, eleven years of it.

He looked at the tin. Then at the boy. Then, for about a second and a
half, at the door.

"Shut that," he said.

"The — "

"The door. Shoot the bolt. Top one, it's stiff."

Tadayoshi went and shot the bolt.

---

*He has heard.*

*Obviously he has heard, it is four days, and a household under order
stops sending work, and a shop that buys from a household under order is
a shop that has to write something on a return.*

*So he knows there is no more Ruri work coming to this counter and he
knows why, in whatever version of why they gave him.*

*And he has just shut his own shop in the middle of a Friday morning.*

He came back to the counter.

Kenji had the lid off the tin and had tipped it out on the black cloth
and was going through it with the spatula, fast, in fives, without saying
the grades out loud.

"How much do you want for it," said Kenji.

"I do not know what it is worth."

"Yes you do."

"There are thirty-one pieces and eleven of them are in the — " Tadayoshi
stopped. "Eleven of them are the ones you graded up last month."

"Twelve."

"Eleven."

"I've got twelve here," said Kenji, and put two aside with the spatula
and did not look up. "You've done another one since and you didn't know
it. There's your eleven and there's this."

---

He did the whole thirty-one in about four minutes.

Then he stood back from the cloth with the spatula in his hand and said,
"Fourteen."

"That is too much."

"It is not too much."

"Four dozen and three was fifteen. This is thirty-one and eleven of them
are threes and the rest are twos and a low three at best, and that is
about nine and a half, ten if the boat has started moving."

"The boat has not started moving."

"Then it is nine and a half."

"It's fourteen," said Kenji, "and I'll tell you once that I'm not going
to discuss it, and you can take it or you can put the lid on and go and
try Marukiya, and Marukiya will give you six and then somebody will be
round at Marukiya's asking who sold them, which they will answer, because
they are not fools either."

Tadayoshi stood at the counter.

*He is buying the tin at half again what it is worth and he is doing it
fast and he has bolted his own door.*

*That is not kindness. A kind man asks whether I have eaten.*

*That is a man buying an object off his premises.*

"All right," he said.

---

Kenji counted it out.

He did it the way he did everything at that counter, which was quickly
and in stacks and without any ceremony at all, and he pushed it across
and Tadayoshi put it away in four places on his person because he had
worked out on the road that four places is better than one.

Then Kenji swept the thirty-one into the tin and put the lid on and put
the tin under the counter and not on the shelf.

He put both hands on the counter and looked at them.

"They came here yesterday," he said.

---

Tadayoshi stood absolutely still.

"Two of them," said Kenji. "About four in the afternoon. In here. Bolted
it themselves, which I've never had done to me in my own shop in twenty
years."

*Do not say anything.*

"They asked me — " He stopped and started again, in a different place.
"They asked me whether I knew a Ruri Mikage. I said yes, I've bought off
that household eleven years, her husband walks it in. They asked me how
long I'd known her. I said eleven years, same answer. They asked whether
I'd ever been to Shioiri."

"Have you?"

"Once. Fifteen years ago, before this counter, when I was doing the
collections myself." His hands were flat on the wood and they did not
move. "And they wrote that down, and they asked me the year, and I had to
stand here and *work out the year*, in front of two men, about a walk I
did fifteen years ago."

He looked up.

"And then they asked me a question about a woman I have met four times in
my life, and I'm not going to say what it was, and I have not slept, and
my wife has not slept, and this morning I stood at that window at six
o'clock and watched the road."

---

*The year.*

*Fifteen years ago.*

*Iwao is thirteen.*

*They asked him the year and they wrote it down and then they asked him a
question about my mother that he will not repeat, in a shop, in front of
his own shelves, and the only question that fits that shape is —*

He did not finish it.

He got about four fifths of the way down it, standing at that counter,
and then it went somewhere he could not follow it, because there was a
piece missing and he did not know he was missing it and he never in his
life got it from the front.

*They think somebody at this counter — *

*Why.*

*Why would anybody in the world think that.*

"Kenji-san," he said, "I do not know what — "

"No," said Kenji.

"I do not — "

"*No*," said Kenji, not loudly. "I don't want it. I've had two days of
wanting to know and I've had a night of working out what happens to a man
who knows things about a household on a list, and I've come out the other
end of it and I don't want it, and I'm telling you that plainly because
you're ten and I'd rather say it than have you standing there working out
how to be honest at me."

Tadayoshi shut his mouth.

---

Kenji got a cloth out from under the counter and wiped the black cloth
down, which did not need doing.

"I'm going to say one thing to you," he said, "and then you're going to
go out of that door, and if you come back here I'll not open it."

"Yes."

"I don't know what your family did."

He folded the cloth.

"That's not me being polite. I've turned it over for two days. I have
bought beads off Tadaharu Ruri for eleven years and he's a bore about
mules and he pays his own way and he's never once tried it on with me
over a grade, and your mother makes — made — a very ordinary drop that I
have never had a complaint about in a decade.

"And there's a thing gone through the whole of that household and taken
every last one of them, and I'll tell you what I know about why. Nothing.
Not a word. They didn't say. They came in here and asked me eleven
questions and answered none, and I've been to two shops and the customs
man and nobody on this street knows either, and I'll be asked about it
for the rest of my life and I will not have an answer."

He put the cloth away.

"So don't tell me. I've got a good reason not to know, and I've made my
peace with it, and I'm forty-five and that's what it's cost me."

---

Tadayoshi picked up the empty tin.

He stood there for a moment with it and could not think of one single
thing to say that was true and safe at the same time, and he had never in
his life had that particular problem, and it turned out to be the whole
of the rest of his life arriving four days early.

"Thank you for fourteen," he said.

"Nine and a half," said Kenji. "You said it yourself."

"Yes."

"Then don't thank me for fourteen. I'd rather you didn't." He came round
the end of the counter. "Go on."

He drew the bolt back himself and opened the door about eighteen inches
and looked up and down the street before he let the boy through it, which
was the only thing in the entire eleven minutes that Tadayoshi thought
about afterwards with anything like warmth, and which Kenji did without
appearing to notice he was doing it.

"Kenji-san — "

"Go on," said Kenji.

---

He went up the street with the empty tin and did not look back, and about
forty yards along he heard the bolt go over behind him.

He walked to the end and round the corner and stood against a wall.

*Fourteen.*

*Four and a half more than it is worth, from a man who is frightened of
me, to get me out of his shop, and he would not be thanked for it.*

*And he does not know why. He genuinely does not know why. He has been
asked eleven questions by two men and told nothing and he is going to
carry it for thirty years.*

*And so am I, and I was there.*

He put his hand flat against his coat where the drop was, and stood in the
alley off the bridge road at Ōura with fourteen thousand ryō in four places on
his body, and understood that he now had money and no plan, exactly as
forecast, and that the forecast had been the only correct thing in his head
for four days.
