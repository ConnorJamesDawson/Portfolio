# Chapter 6, Scene 7 — The Going Rate

*Act 1, month 6, the fourth. Kuriya's kitchen, Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki,
age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~2,300 words.*

---

She had watched him for eleven days before she did anything about it,
which was ten days longer than she wanted and about right.

He came down past the shrine every morning and he went up past it every
evening and on the way up he stopped at the second turn, always, and put
his hand on the wall, and stood there for about as long as it takes to
say a sentence, and then went on.

He did not do it going down.

*Going down's fine and coming up isn't, and it's always the same place,
and it's the second turn and not the third, and the second turn's where
it gets steep.*

*And he doesn't sit down at the shrine, ever, and everybody sits down at
the shrine.*

*Because he can't get up again in front of people.*

---

She argued it out on the third, at night, on the corner of the table,
because her father had taught her to argue things in steps and because
she was not going to do this on a feeling.

*Anzai said nobody. He said it four times and he said it different ways
and the last way was the one where he stopped being nice about it.*

*So. What did he actually say.*

*He said: nobody is ever to be told that I taught you. That's the thing
he said. He said it about him, not about me.*

*Right, but that's a lawyer's answer and I'd be ashamed of it in front of
him.*

*Try again. What's the reason under it.*

*The reason under it is that a medic who teaches a village girl out of a
kitchen loses his list. And a village girl who goes about with hands
gets talked about, and if she gets talked about then somebody says where
did she get it, and there's only one man within four hours who could
have given it to her.*

*So the risk isn't me doing it. The risk is me being talked about.*

She sat with that.

*And Kuriya's the only man in this village who doesn't talk.*

*He said so himself at the gate. He said the only thing he's got that's
any good is being the same for everybody. A man like that doesn't tell
people things.*

*That's not certain. Nothing's certain.*

*But it's the best one there is, and if I don't do it with the best one
there is I never do it with anybody, and then I've got a thing I can do
and I'll do it for nothing for the rest of my life.*

She put the pen down.

*And it isn't the reason. Say the reason.*

*The reason is that we've got until the autumn and I've made a fortieth
nine times.*

---

She went to his house on the fourth after he had come up the hill and
before he had lit anything, and she knocked, and when he opened the door
she said it straight out because there was no other way she had.

"I can do something about your knee."

Kuriya stood in the doorway for a while.

"Can you," he said.

"Yes."

"Who says?"

"Me."

"Who taught you?"

*There it is.*

"I'm not going to tell you that," said Uzuki. "And I'm not going to tell
you that if you ask me again in a minute in a different way, and I'm not
going to tell you if you say you won't have me in the house unless I do,
because if I'd tell you I'd tell somebody else, and then I'd be no good
to you anyway."

He looked at her for a good long moment.

*Say something. Don't stand there.*

*No. Don't. You've said the thing. Let it be the thing.*

"Come in," said Kuriya.

---

He sat on the bench with his leg out and she got down on the floor beside
it and put both hands on and did not do anything at all for about a
minute.

That was the part nobody would ever believe was the work.

*Hand on. Nothing in. Take something out first.*

*What's different.*

She went round the knee the way Anzai had made her go round it — over
and under, the front, the two sides, behind — and did not press and did
not squeeze and just held, and let the difference come to her instead of
going after it, because going after it is how you find what you expected.

*It's hot at the front and it's hot on the inside edge and it's thick.*

"Where does it hurt?"

"There," said Kuriya, and put his thumb on the front.

*Right.*

*And it isn't there.*

She kept her face doing nothing, which she was getting better at.

*It's the hip.*

*The hip's wrong. The hip's the thing that's wrong and the knee's the
thing that's shouting, and he's had a bad hip for probably years and he's
been walking on the outside of that foot to get up a hill and the knee's
been paying for it.*

*Do those two agree? No. They don't agree at all.*

*And I don't know what to do about that.*

She thought about it for about four seconds, which was all she had.

*I could go at the hip. He'd not know. He'd not know and if it went wrong
he'd think I'd hurt him for nothing and I'd never get in another house in
this village.*

*And I've got one thimble.*

*Do the knee. Do the knee properly and do it well and put the hip in your
head and keep it there.*

---

It took twenty-five minutes and it was ninety separate goes.

Little. Stop. Wait.

The stopping was the work. It had been the work since the day Anzai first
made her do it with a cup of water and told her she was pouring, and it
was the work now: a thimble, and then *stop*, on purpose, as an action,
with as much in it as the going had, and not trail off and not lean.

Little. Stop. Wait.

At about the twentieth she stopped being able to feel her own hands as
hands.

At about the fortieth Kuriya said something to her — she never afterwards
knew what — and she did not answer, and he did not say anything else for
the rest of it, which she was grateful for and did not have any room to
be grateful about.

At the sixtieth it stopped working.

She knew that with total certainty. She knew it the way you know it is
raining. She had been going at it for twenty minutes and there was
nothing happening under her hands and there had never been anything
happening under her hands and the whole thing was a story a man had told
her in a kitchen in another village.

*Sixtieth.*

*He said this. He said this happens and he said it's at about the
sixtieth and he said the whole of what he was teaching me was what to do
in the next four seconds.*

*Don't push.*

She did not push.

Little. Stop. Wait.

Seventy. Eighty. Eighty-nine.

Ninety.

She took her hands off and sat back on her heels on the floor and had
absolutely nothing left, in a way that was new and that she quite liked,
and put her hands in her lap because they were shaking and she did not
want him to see it.

---

You cannot tell during. It is forty seconds after that you find out.

She counted the forty seconds.

Then Kuriya said, "Hm," and bent the knee.

Then he stood up.

He stood up the way people stand up, in one, without the hand on the
bench and without the noise, and he did it before he had thought about
it, and she watched him realise about half a second afterwards what he
had just done.

He walked to the door and back.

He did not say anything for a while.

"How long's that for?" he said.

"About four and a half hours."

"Four and a half hours."

"Then it comes back and I do it again."

---

He went and got a box down off the shelf.

"No," said Uzuki. "Take it off the rent."

"I'm not taking it off the rent."

"Then—"

"Listen." He put the box on the table. "If I take it off the rent it's
not work. It's a favour, and it's a favour between me and your mother
about a house, and then I'm a man who's had a favour off a child instead
of a man who's paid one. I'd rather pay you. Do you understand the
difference?"

*Yes.*

*I understand the difference perfectly and I would very much like you to
be a worse man for about one minute.*

"Yes," she said.

He counted out a quarter of a ryō and put it in her hand.

*A quarter.*

*Ten fortieths. That's ten days of stones in twenty-five minutes.*

*That's — that's good money. That's actually good money.*

She stood in his kitchen and let herself have about three seconds of it,
and then did the sum, because she could not not do the sum.

*One a day. That's all there is in me, one a day, and I've just had to
sit on the floor after one.*

*Thirty a month is seven and a half.*

*The hole's three and a half.*

*I could do it. I could do the whole of it and have some over.*

And then the other side of it arrived, the way it always did, about four
seconds late and completely unanswerable.

*Who?*

*Who are the thirty?*

*There's Kuriya. There's Ine's brother, who's got money and a chest. And
there's the priest, and he hasn't got money.*

*That's three, and one of them's in this room, and the other two would
not let me put a hand on them if I stood in the road and shouted.*

---

"Can I come back?" she said.

"When's it come back?"

"Tonight. Late."

Kuriya sat down again, carefully, which she noticed.

"Every day?" he said.

"If you want."

"No," he said.

*There.*

"Not the money," he said. "I've got the money. It's that I'd be a man who
has a girl of ten come up to his house every evening, and there is no
version of that which is all right, and there isn't a person in this
village who'd think it was, and half of them would be wrong about it and
the other half would only be wrong about *why*, and it would land on your
mother and not on me." He rubbed his hands together. "Twice a week."

"Twice a week."

"Twice a week, and you come in daylight, and if anybody asks, you're
doing the step."

*Twice a week.*

*That's eight a month. Eight quarters is two.*

*Two, off one man, and the hole's three and a half.*

She had got about a second and a half of that before he took it off her.

"And not a quarter a time," said Kuriya.

"You said—"

"I said a quarter for that one and I meant it and I'd say it again." He
put his hands on his knees. "A quarter's a man's day. You'd be having two
men's days a week off me for fifty minutes, every week, until one of us
is dead. I haven't got that. Nobody in this village has got that."

"Then what."

"Half a ryō a month," said Kuriya, "and you come twice a week, and you
come whether it's bad or it isn't, and if I'm away you don't lose it."

*Half.*

*Half a month, for eight of them. That's a sixteenth each.*

*He paid me a quarter an hour ago.*

She stood in his kitchen and did the sum four times looking for the place
where he had cheated her, and there wasn't one.

*He hasn't. That's what it's worth.*

*A quarter was what it was worth once, when he didn't know if it worked
and he'd have paid anything to find out. A sixteenth is what it's worth
every week for the rest of his life.*

*It's the same hands. It's the same twenty-five minutes and the same
ninety and the same sitting on the floor after.*

**The minute a thing stops being a wonder it starts being a price.**

*Half a ryō against three and a half.*

"And I'll tell you the other thing now," said Kuriya, "so you're not
sitting up waiting for it. I'd like to send you to Ine's brother. I'd
like to send you to about six people. And I'm not going to, and you know
why, and it isn't because I don't rate you."

"Because they'd ask who taught me."

"Because they'd ask who taught you," said Kuriya, "and because the first
thing that happens to a girl who mends people is that she stops being a
girl who mends people and starts being a thing there's a word for."

---

She went down the hill in the dark with a quarter of a ryō in her fist
and her hands still not right.

*So it works.*

*It works and I'm good at it and it's worth a quarter, and there is
nowhere in the world to sell it.*

*It isn't the hands. The hands are fine. The hands would do thirty.*

*It's that I'm ten.*

*And that's the one thing on the whole list I can't do anything about,
and it's the only one that's ever going to fix itself, and it fixes
itself in about five years.*

She got to the gate and stopped and looked at the house with the one lamp
in it.

*Five years.*

*The rent's due in four weeks.*
