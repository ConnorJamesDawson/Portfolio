# Chapter 1, Scene 10 — The Frame

*Act 1, month 2, the last week. Shioiri, Akagane's shed on the second
course. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,730 words.*

---

"Don't come in yet," said Akagane. "Stand there."

"I am standing here."

"Stand there and shut your eyes."

"Why."

"Because I've asked you to and I'm twenty and you're ten, and those are
the only two arguments I've got." A pause, and some quiet metal noise.
"Right. Open them."

---

It was on the bench on a folded cloth, which was the first thing that
was wrong, because Akagane did not fold cloths.

It was a frame.

It was a frame the way the old one was a frame in the same sense that a
sentence and a book are both writing. Copper, beaten thin, the open hand
with the fingers spread — and every one of the fingers had been drawn
out and rolled and worked so that it tapered along its length, thick at
the root and thinner at the tip, and there was a fillet where each one
met the palm, and the palm itself was not flat.

"It's dished," said Tadayoshi.

"It's dished."

"How much?"

"Four in a hundred. I did seven first and seven's too much, the grit
runs." Akagane had not put down what was in his other hand, which was a
scraper. "Pick it up."

He picked it up.

It was lighter than his. That was the second thing. It was lighter than
his and it sat differently across the fingers of the under-hand, and
there was a rolled edge along the wrist side that took the pressure off
the web of the thumb, which was where his own hand ached at the end of
a nine-drop afternoon and always had, and which he had never once
mentioned to anybody.

"You've made the wrist edge — "

"You rub it," said Akagane. "You do it about every twenty minutes.
You've done it since you were seven. I don't think you know you do it."

---

Tadayoshi stood in the doorway of the shed with it in both hands.

*He has watched me work for three years.*

*He has watched me rub my own hand and gone away and thought about a
rolled edge.*

"How long did this take you?"

"Nine weeks."

"Nine — "

"Not all of it. On and off. Winter's quiet." Akagane turned the scraper
over and put it down at last and leaned his hip on the bench, and there
it was: the thing he did about once a year, which was to allow himself
to be pleased in front of somebody. "The dish is the bit. Do you want to
know about the dish?"

"Yes."

"You'll be the only person who does."

"I know."

"Right, well." He took it back and held it up to the shed window. "The
frame's job is the feed. Everyone knows that much — you push through
your hand, it comes off the plate, plate makes it come off *even*. Only
it doesn't. Not properly. It comes off the fingers faster than it comes
off the palm, because the fingers are thinner, and that's why a Ruri
piece has grain in it. It's the fingers running ahead."

He turned the frame a quarter.

"So I tapered them. Thick at the root, thin at the tip. Slows the root,
lets the tip alone. And then the palm was coming off too slow so I
dished it four in a hundred and now — " he tilted it, and the light ran
across the copper and off the tip of the little finger — "now they arrive
together. As near as I can get."

"You've evened the feed."

"I've evened the feed," said Akagane.

---

*He is right.*

That was the first thing, and it was not a small thing, and Tadayoshi
made himself stand still inside it for a moment because it deserved
that.

*He is completely right. He has got it from the outside, off the metal,
with no theory at all — he does not know what a course is, he has never
laid one, he cannot and never will — and he has looked at four hundred
years of grain in the family's work and worked out that grain is the
fingers running ahead of the palm.*

*Nobody in this compound knows that. Meno does not know that. My mother
does not know that. It is in no liturgy, and if you said it in front of
the old ones they would tell you the lattice has been the lattice since
before the sea came up.*

*He has done in nine weeks with a scraper what I did in six weeks with a
library, and he has done it on the wrong side of the problem, and he
does not know it is the wrong side.*

Because the second thing was also true, and the second thing was that it
did not matter.

*The frame evens the feed. I do not need the feed evened. I have not
needed the feed evened since I was eight years old.*

*I carry a frame because the kitchen window looks down on the workshop
door.*

*He has spent nine weeks of a winter making a better version of a thing
whose entire purpose, in my hands, is to be visible from a window.*

---

"Well?" said Akagane.

"It's dished four in a hundred and it will change the whole grain of
anything laid in it."

"It will."

"Kanaya will notice."

"Kanaya will notice on about the third dozen and he'll put the grade
*up*," said Akagane, with enormous satisfaction, "and he will not say
why, because saying why would mean admitting he's been paying two and a
low three for uneven work for eleven years."

"Have you made one for my mother?"

"Making one. Hers is different, she's got a shorter reach, I've got to
take a quarter-inch out of the palm." He scratched his jaw with the back
of the scraper. "Yours first because you're the one who'd notice."

---

Tadayoshi stood in the shed with the frame in his hands.

He got as far as the name.

"Akagane — "

"Mm."

*Say it.*

*Say: it does not do what you think it does. Say: the feed does not need
a plate at all, it needs a hand that knows what the plate is for, and a
hand can be taught that in a winter, and I would teach you, except that
you cannot lay a course and never will, so what I would actually be
teaching you is my mother — and she can lay two, and if she knew what
you have just told me she could lay six, and the family's grade would go
from a low three to whatever the top of the scale is called.*

*Say: you have solved it, and you have solved it from the outside, and
there is a person eleven feet from where you are standing who has the
other half.*

*Say: I have had the other half for two years.*

The rain had come on again on the shed roof. Akagane had picked the
scraper back up, because Akagane could not hold a conversation and
nothing at the same time, and he was waiting with the mild, unbothered
patience of a man who has never in his life been in a hurry about
anything.

*And she said: nobody sees this. Not your father. Not Meno. Nobody.*

*And she said it because of an uncle whose name is on a piece in a box
under a shelf, and she has never told me anything else about it, and I
have never once been given a reason.*

*I am ten. I have been given a rule.*

He felt the whole of it go past him. It took about a second and a half —
about a second and a quarter, he thought afterwards, which was four
seals, which was the length of a substitution — and it went past him and
it was gone and it was not coming back.

"Akagane," said Tadayoshi, "it's the best thing anybody in this family
has made in my lifetime."

---

Which was true.

That was the thing he took away with him and could not put down for a
long time afterwards: that it had been true. He had not lied. He had
looked for the truest available sentence that was not the sentence, and
he had found one, and it had been sitting right there, and it had cost
him nothing at all to say.

His cousin went red to the ears and said, "Get off," and turned round to
the bench and started doing something to a piece of stock that did not
need anything doing to it.

"I mean it."

"Yes, all right."

"I'm going to use it tonight."

"Use it tonight," said Akagane, to the bench. "Tell me what the grain
does."

---

He used it that night.

He set the lamp, and put three fingers of grit into the dished palm, and
levelled them off with the side of his thumb, and put his left hand
under — and had to stop, and adjust, because the balance was wrong.

Not wrong. Different. It was lighter and it sat further forward and the
rolled edge that took the pressure off the web of his thumb also, as it
turned out, moved his whole under-hand about a third of an inch nearer
the work than it had been for two years.

Which changed nothing about the feed, because he was not using the
frame to feed.

But it changed where his hand was.

So he laid the first drop and it came out with the note a fraction high,
and he broke it and put it back in the tray, and laid another, and that
one was right; and then he sat there for a while and worked out that he
was going to have to spend the next four or five days relearning the
distance from his under-hand to a piece of grit.

*Cost: four days.*

He got the ledger out and wrote it in the fourth column, in the narrow
one, because he had ruled it narrow and he was already finding that
narrow was where the true things went.

Then he put the old frame in the bench drawer, at the back, under the
rag, where it stayed.

He laid eight more drops before the lamp went, and every one of them had
grain in it.
