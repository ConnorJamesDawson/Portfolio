# Chapter 4, Scene 10 — Eleven

*Act 1, month 4, the sixth — day eleven. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten.
POV: Uzuki. ~1,750 words.*

---

She came in the back way, because she always came in the back way, and
she was talking before she was through the gate.

"Da — Da, listen — no, I've got to say it in the right order or it won't
be — "

The barrow was not where the barrow lived. She saw that and thought
*somebody's moved the barrow* and went past it.

"— because I've worked something out and you're going to do the thing
with your eyebrows and I want it on the record that I said it before you
did — "

She got the cart in against the wall and got the handle down quietly out
of eleven days of habit and did not manage the rest of it quietly at all.

"— and Ma's going to say we're both idiots — "

She came up the step and through the passage in the dark bit by the rack,
with the cloth from Chiyo under one arm and her coat half off, and put
her head round the kitchen door.

"Da, he *taught* me it. Anzai said somebody's taught you to hold back and
I said my father and I didn't — "

---

There were two women in the kitchen and neither of them was her mother.

One was Iwasa's wife and one was the woman from the end house and they
were at the table and there was cloth on it, and they both looked up at
exactly the same time and neither of them said anything at all.

Uzuki stood in the doorway with her coat half off.

"— didn't understand what he meant," she said.

Nobody answered her.

---

Her mother came in from the front room.

She came in the way you come into a room you have been standing outside
of, and she stopped just inside the door, and she had her sleeves down,
which she never had, and her hair was done properly, which she did on the
first of the month and at weddings.

"Uzuki," she said.

That was all of it.

---

Afterwards, for a great many years, Uzuki could not get past the fact
that she had understood it from her own name.

Not from the two women. Not from the cloth on the table or the barrow
being in the wrong place or her mother's sleeves. From her name, said
once, at eleven in the morning, in the doorway of her own kitchen, in a
voice she had never heard her mother use and never heard her use again.

She stood there.

"When," she said.

"Uzuki, love — "

"When."

"The fourth," said her mother.

---

*The fourth.*

She did the sum. She could not have stopped herself doing the sum. She
had been doing sums for eleven days and it went straight through and out
the other side before anybody could get a hand on it.

*The third was the afternoon. I left Kayano at half seven on the third
and walked till about midnight.*

*The fourth I did twenty-two miles and stopped on a wall in the middle of
the day and did the ninety.*

*I did the ninety on the fourth.*

*I sat on a wall with my own arm in my hands and did it ninety times and
wrote "22 good" on the back of a chit and was pleased about it, and put
it away, and got up, and walked eight more miles.*

She stood in the doorway of the kitchen.

"I was on a wall," she said.

"What?"

"Nothing," said Uzuki.

---

She did not cry then and she was never able to explain that either.

What she did was take her coat the rest of the way off, which she was
still in the middle of, and hang it on the hook, because it was in her
hands and there was nowhere else to put it — and Iwasa's wife made a
small movement as though to get up and the woman from the end house put a
hand on her arm, and neither of them said anything, and Uzuki was
grateful to both of them for the rest of her life without ever once
mentioning it.

"Is he — " She could not find the end of that. "Where — "

"The fifth," said her mother. "Yesterday morning. Tazu and Iwasa and the
men from the mill."

"Yesterday."

"Yesterday morning."

*So it is done.*

*It is done and it was done yesterday and I was in a hedge somewhere
south of the fork because my legs made me stop, and I got up before it
was light and did eight miles before breakfast because I wanted to get
here, and while I was doing that it was already over and had been for
four hours.*

"I ran," said Uzuki.

Her mother's face did something.

"I mean — I did. I ran, on the road, on the fifth. With the cart. About
two miles, and it was stupid, because you can't run with a cart, and I
did it because I'd worked out about Da and the forms and I wanted to — "

She stopped.

"I wanted to tell him," she said.

---

Her mother came across the kitchen and put her arms round her.

She did it badly. That was the thing Uzuki thought at the time and was
ashamed of for a decade and then, at about twenty-two, stopped being
ashamed of, because by then she had learned what two days of being the
only person who knew does to a woman's arms.

Her mother held onto her too hard and not for long enough and let go
first.

"You'll want to sit down," she said.

"I don't want to sit down."

"No," said her mother. "No."

---

They stood in the kitchen.

"You said," said Uzuki.

"What?"

"You said if I was eleven days you'd come and get me. You said you'd
stand in the road at Ōgi and shout."

She heard it come out and could not get it back and did not, in fact,
particularly want to.

Her mother stood in the middle of her own kitchen with her sleeves down
and her hair done, and her face did not change at all, and she said:

"I know."

"You said it in front of him. You said it and he said you wouldn't get
four miles and you said you'd get to Ōgi."

"I know what I said."

"Well — "

"Uzuki," said her mother, and it was not sharp, and that was what stopped
it. "I have been here for two days. Ask me tomorrow."

---

She got the food out of the bag onto the table because it was in the bag
and because her hands wanted something.

Chiyo's cloth. The end of Ine's ham. The half loaf. The thing wrapped in
leaves that she was not to open, which was for her mother, and which she
put down in front of her mother without saying what it was, and which
neither of them opened that day or the next.

And then she put her hand inside her coat on the hook, and got the twist
of cloth out, and put it on the table.

It made the noise money makes.

---

Her mother looked at it.

"That's the hundred," said Uzuki.

"I know what it is."

"You said if nothing happened I was to bring it back and put it where it
was. So I've brought it back." She could not make her voice do anything
useful. "It's all there. I didn't take any of it. There was — I had a
bowl of barley off a woman in the lane and Chiyo fed me twice and Ine
gave me the ham, and I bought a — I bought nothing. It's all there."

"All right."

"It's a hundred. You can count it."

"I'm not going to count it, Uzuki."

"You should count it," said Uzuki, and her voice went, "because it's a
hundred and it should be *two hundred and forty*, and I did that, I did
that in the second month, I took a hundred on account off a man at a
crossroads and came home and you put it on the shelf and you never said
one word about it and I have known since about the fourth day out and I
have *known*, and — "

She stopped, because she had run out of air.

Her mother stood on the other side of the table.

"Yes," she said.

"That's why you said no. In the kitchen. That's why it was four seconds."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you *tell* me?"

Her mother put both hands flat on the table beside the twist of cloth.

"Because you'd have carried it," she said, "and it wouldn't have got him
to Kayano either."

---

She went out into the passage at some point after that.

She did not decide to. She was in the kitchen and then she was in the
dark bit by the back door with her hand on the wall, and the two women
were talking very quietly behind her in the way people do when they have
been given something to do at last.

The rack was where the rack was.

The top peg had her father's bokken on it, where he had put it every
evening of her life, and where it had been since a Sunday in the second
month when he had handed it to her and told her to take them in and had
sat down on the barrow and had not got up.

The bottom peg was empty.

She had her own in her left hand. She had carried it eleven days and two
hundred and some miles and had done the third form eleven times in a road
with three men in a wood and had not once got the eighth count clean, and
it had been under the sacking in the cart since Sanzu.

She put it back on the bottom peg.

Then she stood in the passage in the dark and looked at the two of them
up there, one above the other, the way they had been on every single
evening of her life.

*There.*

*That's the pair of them back.*

And she stood with her hand still up on the peg and found that she could
not think of one single person in that house who was ever going to take
either of them down again.
