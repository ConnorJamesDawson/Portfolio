# Chapter 12 — The Consignment

*Book two. Act one, month 12 — and it closes the act.*
*11 scenes, 20,150 words. Tadayoshi ×7 · Uzuki ×3 · Kimimaro ×1.*



*Act 1, month 11 the twenty-seventh to month 12 the seventh. Ōtoma.
Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,750 words.*

---

He took it down to the yard on the twenty-seventh and found Ōtsuka doing
something to a hoop, and read the whole docket out loud to him, which he
had never done with anything.

> *9 crates. Kadono-dera. Return required.*
>
> *9 days.*

"Nine days," said Tadayoshi.

"That's what it says."

"It's nine days there. It doesn't say anything about coming back and it
says return required."

Ōtsuka put the hoop down, took the docket, licked the pencil, crossed out
the nine and wrote eighteen, and gave it back.

The whole of it took about six seconds and he did not look at the paper
while he did it.

---

Tadayoshi went up the ladder with that and sat with it for a while,
because he had got something and could not work out what.

*He did not check it.*

*He did not check it against anything and he did not ask me how I got
eighteen and he did not think about it for a second and a half, and I have
watched that man argue for four minutes about a sixteenth.*

*A number nobody checks is a number nobody is going to read.*

He turned it over.

*Which means either the number does not matter because the job does not
matter, or the number does not matter because somebody has already got
what they wanted out of the docket existing.*

He could not choose between them and he wrote both down, and then, because
it was the honest thing, he wrote the third one underneath.

*Or he is busy and it is a Tuesday.*

---

He did the sheets on the first of the twelfth month, because that was the
instruction and the instruction had a date on it and he had already
failed it once.

He unfolded them on the boards and read the second line, which was the
left-hander, 17/9, and then the white under it.

And there was nothing to add.

He sat with that for longer than the nothing warranted.

*Nothing at the ford since the twenty-first. Nothing at Shioya. Nothing at
Mikura, and Mikura was five days in a town.*

*So the whole of the eleventh month is empty and it is empty because it
was empty.*

He got the pencil out and ruled a line under the left-hander and dated it
the first of the twelfth month and wrote three words in the middle of the
white, and it was the first thing he had put on those sheets in
seventy-six days.

> *Nothing. Checked.*

---

He looked at it for a bit.

*And now I know.*

*I did not write the ford one because I did not write it. It was not that
there was nothing to write in the eleventh month and I have just found
that out by writing the nothing, which means the instrument works, which
means the twenty-first of the tenth is the only thing on these sheets that
is missing.*

He did not put that down. There was no column for it and he had four
columns.

He folded the sheets and put them back under the throwables.

---

The money took him three days and he did not solve it either.

He had six thousand in the tin and four thousand inside the frame and
about eighteen thousand against his ribs, which had gone up by the Shioya
and Mikura fees and by nothing going out except four hundred and eighty
for boots, and he was about to be nineteen days away from every one of
those places.

*The tin is under a board in a loft that four people can get into.*

*The frame is going with me, because the frame always goes with me, so the
four thousand is fine and I have made an object I need in a fight ring
wrong to keep it.*

*And eighteen thousand against my ribs for nineteen days on a mountain is
eighteen thousand that is on me when somebody takes the pack.*

He went round it for three days and there was no answer, because there is
no answer: there is nowhere in the world for a boy of ten with no house
and no name to put eighteen thousand ryō, and a bank is a person who
writes your name down, and the whole of his life was not being written
down.

In the end he split it four ways in four places and hated every one of
them, and the last thing he did on the sixth was walk past the cooperage
in the dark and look at the boards where the tin was and make himself
leave without checking it, because checking it in front of somebody was
worse than losing it.

---

He did the provisioning properly and it was the only part of the week he
enjoyed.

**The boots**, which had done four hundred miles since the twenty-eighth
of the tenth month and were exactly as good as the man with the bench had
said and about half again as good as the pair they replaced, and which he
had greased twice and would grease again at Ōgi.

**The old boots**, which he took, because a wet boot in the twelfth month
is a foot you lose and two pairs is the whole of the answer, and which
went in the bottom of the pack and weighed four pounds he did not want.

**Food for nine days and not eleven**, because there is food on a road
until about the fourth day out of Ōgi and then there is not, and he had
walked the first two thirds of that road in the sixth month and knew
exactly where it stopped.

**Fire.** *A twist of oiled rag, and the flint, and a bundle of split
kindling from the cooperage that was going on the fire anyway.* **Nine
nights above four thousand feet in the twelfth month is nine fires or it
is not nine nights**, and he costed the weight of the kindling against
the chance of finding wood dry above the pass and took the kindling, and
was right, twice.

**And the coat**, which was the one he had had since the sixth month and
which he had priced three replacements for on the twenty-ninth of the
tenth and had not bought, and which he now took up the highest road in the
country in the twelfth month.

He noticed that on the second evening out and put it in the ordinary
column with the date and no comment.

> *Coat. Own fault.*

---

Nobody said it was a punishment.

That is the thing about it and he had it in his hands the whole week and
could not do anything with it. Ōtsuka was exactly as he always was, which
was correct and not friendly and not unfriendly. Hōki had gone. Nobody in
that yard looked at him differently, and two of them asked him what he had
got and he told them and they said *cold* and went on with what they were
doing.

*If it were a punishment somebody would be pleased about it.*

*And nobody is. It is a docket and it is on the boards and it has got a
number on it that nobody read.*

He got to the bottom of that one on the fifth, sitting on the boards with
his pack half-loaded, and it was worse than the other answer.

*It is not that they have decided to be hard on me.*

*It is that this is what there is, and putting me on it did not cost
anybody a thought, and Hōki said the true thing in the back room and I
heard the wrong half of it. He did not say I was being punished. He said I
had got expensive.*

*An expensive thing is not a thing you are angry with. It is a thing you
put down carefully somewhere it cannot break anything.*

---

He left on the seventh at about four in the morning, in the dark, which
was not necessary and which he did anyway.

The pack was fifty-one pounds and he weighed it, because he weighed
everything, and fifty-one pounds is a comfortable load for a grown man
and it was about half what he weighed, and he had nine days of it in front
of him and the last three of them going up.

He went out of Ōtoma on the east road and had the light by seven and was
past Sakabe by the middle of the afternoon and did not stop in it.

And at some point on the second day, on a road he had walked in the sixth
month, going the other way, with a parcel and no fee and eleven towns he
had never seen, he had a thought about the eighteen days that he did not
like and did not put down anywhere.

*Eighteen days is the front half of a month.*

*And when I come back it will be the first month, and it will be a new
year, and I will be eleven.*

He put the strap higher on his shoulder and went on.

*Act 1, month 12, the first to the fourteenth. The Kadono-dera. Nozaki
Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,390 words.*

---

They started arriving on the second and by the sixth there were nine of
them, and she was under all of it.

She heard the first one come in and did not know what she was hearing for
about an hour. It was feet. It was feet in the long building over her
head, in the part she had slept in for eighteen days in the tenth month,
and there were more of them than there had been at any point since she had
been down the steps.

Then the bell, and then more feet, and then, on the third, a voice.

*Somebody is up there.*

---

She got the whole of it by the fourth and she got it from Tokuji, who
brought the bowl himself twice that week and who answered questions if you
asked them one at a time.

"How many now?"

"Six."

"Is it the winter ones?"

"It's the twelfth month," said Tokuji.

He had said that to her in the tenth month, on the vegetable ground, with
the last of the daikon coming up, and it had meant *you are five weeks
early* and had been the best news she had ever had.

Now it meant six people were sleeping eleven feet above her head.

---

She worked at it for two days and could not make it into anything.

*There are people up there who do not know I am here.*

*Or there are people up there who know I am here.*

She could not choose between those either, and it was the third pair of
readings she could not choose between in a fortnight and it was starting
to be the thing she noticed about her own head.

*Everything comes in twos now and I cannot get at either of them, and that
is not because I am stupid. It is because I have got nothing to test them
with. I have got a door and a bowl and a bell.*

She lay on the mat and listened to a man cough directly above her at about
nine in the evening on the sixth of the twelfth month.

*And they cannot hear me, because if they could I would have heard
somebody stop.*

---

The date had gone on the thirtieth and she had spent the first four days
of the twelfth month with two answers and no way in, and on the seventh
she chose one.

She knew she was choosing. That was the part she made herself write down.

*I have got no new information and I am picking anyway, and the reason I
am picking is that I cannot go on holding both of them.*

*So: nobody came, so it fell through.*

She turned it over.

*Which means I am a girl in a cellar that nobody has a plan for, and the
arrangement is the arrangement because it has not been changed.*

**And she was wrong, and she was wrong for a reason that would have
embarrassed her if she had ever found out, which was that of the two
readings she had taken the one she was in.**

---

The charcoal ran out on the ninth.

She had been getting it off the wood store since the fifteenth of the
tenth month, a bit at a time, in her sleeve, and she had not been able to
get to the wood store since the second of the eleventh, and what she had
was what had been in the twist of cloth when they moved her, and on the
ninth of the twelfth month she used the last of it on the fourth column
and there was nothing left to make a mark with.

She spent an hour looking for something.

There was nothing. There was a mat and two blankets and a bowl that went
back and a lamp that came for an hour and a wall of stone and earth, and
the lamp was a wick in oil in a dish and the wick did not char in a way
that came off on anything, and she tried that, and it did not.

So she sat with the sacking on her knees and worked out what she was going
to keep.

---

*Four columns.*

*The ninety is dead. It has been dead since the twenty-sixth of the eighth
month and it is a hundred and fifteen numbers and I can hold the last one
without writing it.*

*The money is eleven things and it has not moved in six weeks and it is
three hundred and ninety and I am not going to forget three hundred and
ninety.*

*The fourth column has got one thing in it and the one thing has gone
past.*

She looked at the third column.

**1,542.**

*And that one changes every day, and it is the only one that changes, and
if I put that in my head it is going to be the only thing in there that
moves, and I know what happens then because I did it for a fortnight on
the road and I hated it.*

She sat with it for a long time.

Then she did the thing she had learned to do in a kitchen at Nakadō, which
was to work out what the actual question was.

*The question is not which column do I keep.*

*The question is what am I going to be doing tomorrow.*

---

She did not have an answer for that and she found one in about four days
and it was the wrong one, and she knew it was the wrong one and did it
anyway, because it was the only thing available.

She started counting the building.

Feet, first: how many pairs, and which were the same pair twice. Then
where the boards gave and where they did not, which told her where the
mats were. Then the bell, and who rang it, which was not always the same
person and which changed on the eleventh.

Then the times: what happened at what hour, and how tight it was, and
which of the nine were up before the bell and which were not.

By the fourteenth she could have drawn the long building from underneath.

*Nine of them. Four on the north end, five on the south, and the two on
the south corner are together and one of them is a child.*

She stopped when she got to the child.

---

*There is a child up there.*

She lay on her back on the mat under nine people in the twelfth month and
had the worst hour she had had since the wood store, and it was not about
the child.

*They came in the ordinary way. Somebody asked them if they had eaten and
they said no and somebody said right and got them a bowl.*

*That is what happened to me.*

*And then I did eight trips instead of eleven and four ranks under the
eave and the north end and the floors, and I did all of it fast, on
purpose, so that they would want to keep me.*

*And there is a child up there who has not done any of that and is
upstairs.*

She turned that over four or five ways and could not make it come out as
anything except the one thing, and the one thing was not about her being
worse.

**It was not what she had done. It was what she had on her head, and she
had known that since the twenty-eighth of the tenth month, and it had
taken until the fourteenth of the twelfth for it to arrive properly.**

*They are not keeping me because I am useful.*

*I was useful for fifteen days and it did not come off the number.*

She turned over on the mat, facing the wall, with the bell going for the
last time above her.

*And it does not come off it now either. It is the same building and I
have gone from one to the other and nothing about me changed.*

She counted the bell out of habit and did not write it down, because there
was nothing to write with, and found that she had done it anyway in her
head, and that it had cost her nothing at all.

*Right.*

*Fine. In my head, then.*

*1,537.*

*Act 1, month 12, the seventh to the sixteenth. Ōtoma to the Kadono-dera.
Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,900 words.*

---

The road was empty and it was empty the whole way, and that was the first
thing about the twelfth month and it took him four days to stop noticing
it.

He counted, because he counted.

On the second of the sixth month he had walked the Ōtoma-to-Sakabe stretch
with a parcel and had passed, by his own book, forty-one people in a day
and had been passed by nine. On the eighth of the twelfth month he did the
same stretch and passed six.

*Six.*

*And two of those were the same two, going up and coming back.*

He put it in the operational column that night with no conclusion under
it, because there was no conclusion in it, and it was a true observation
about roads in winter and he had nothing to do with it.

---

He got to Ōgi on the twelfth in the middle of the afternoon and did the
four things he had planned to do there, in the order he had planned to do
them.

**Grease.** *Both pairs, properly, sitting on a wall with the pot warmed
against his chest, and it took him fifty minutes and he begrudged none of
it.* **Five days of a wet boot is a foot and a foot is the whole trip.**

**Food for the last four days**, because the road out of Ōgi to the north-
east goes up and there is nothing on it after the second day. *Millet,
salt, and a hard black thing a woman on the bridge sold him that he did
not have a word for and which turned out to be the best purchase he made
all winter.*

**Fat**, for the fire and for the boots and for his hands. *A quarter of a
pound in a wooden pot, forty ryō, and he did not haggle, and he noticed
that he did not haggle, and put a mark by it.*

**And a hat**, which he had never owned.

He looked at hats for about ten minutes and hated the whole of it, because
a hat is a thing you cannot cost: it either keeps your head warm or it does
not, and there is no way to find out in a shop. In the end he bought a
plain one at ninety because a man buying one at the next stall bought that
one, and put it on, and walked out of Ōgi feeling like somebody wearing
somebody else's decision.

He wore it for four days and did not take it off once.

---

The lane behind the tannery is at the north end of Ōgi and the north road
goes past the top of it.

He came up level with it at about four o'clock on the twelfth with the hat
on and the pack at fifty-one pounds and four days of mountain in front of
him.

*Four hundred yards that way.*

He went on north.

---

The road out of Ōgi is a road for two days and then it is a track and then
for the last day and a half it is a set of stone steps and a cut in the
side of a hill, and the whole of it goes up.

Day one out of Ōgi was farms and it was fine.

Day two was the last of the farms by noon and then a long valley with
nothing in it, and he did eleven miles and made a fire at four because the
light went at four, and the fire was the first one where the kindling
mattered, and it mattered exactly as much as he had costed it.

*Everything above this valley has been wet for six weeks.*

*I have got two pounds of dry wood in the bottom of a pack and it is the
best two pounds in it.*

He allowed himself to be pleased about that for about a minute and then
worked out that being pleased about it was cheap, because he had bought it
with an hour of arithmetic in a cooperage yard and the arithmetic was the
work and the pleasure was afterwards.

---

Day three was the one that cost him.

It went up all day and the last two hours of it were a cut in the side of
a hill with the wind coming along it, and it began to sleet at about one
and did not stop, and at about three he understood two things at the same
time in a way he did not enjoy.

*The coat is not enough.*

*And I knew that on the twenty-ninth of the tenth month in a doorway with
the good one in my hands.*

He kept walking, because the answer to being cold on a mountain is to
keep walking, and he did an inventory while he did it, which was the only
thing available.

*What I have got: two shirts, both on. The coat. The hat, which is
working, and it is working better than anything else I own and it cost
ninety. Mittens from the sixth month, which are the second-best thing I
have got and I bought them out of guilt in a market at Fukae and could not
have told you why.*

*What I have not got: a coat that is not this coat.*

He got to the top of the cut at about half past three and got in under an
overhang and made a fire with the second-to-last of the kindling and sat
in front of it for two hours getting the shirts dry one at a time, and it
worked, and he did not sleep much.

And he took the whole of it out on the ledger before he slept, which was
the only place it could go.

> *3rd day out of Ōgi. Sleet from about 1 in a cut with a wind along it.*
>
> *The coat did not do it. I priced three on 29/10 and did not buy one and
> that decision cost me four hours today and would have cost me a foot in
> another six.*
>
> *What it cost: not a coat. Being able to say why I did not buy one.*

He looked at that last line for a while and then left it, because it was
true and because there was no fourth column in this book either.

---

Day four was better and shorter and he did it in six hours, and the last
mile of it was stone steps, and there were eleven flights of them cut into
the side of the hill in different centuries.

He counted them going up.

He counted them because he counted things, and then, at about the seventh
flight, because he had started to get an answer.

*A hundred and forty steps so far and the treads are between nine and
fourteen inches and four of them are worn hollow in the middle.*

*No cart comes up this.*

*No cart has ever come up this and no cart is ever going to.*

He stopped on the eighth flight with his hand on the rock and put it
together, which took about four seconds and was the whole of the next two
days arriving early and unrecognised.

*So whatever I am collecting comes down these steps on somebody's back.*

*And there is one of me.*

He turned that over and could not get anything out of it, because he did
not know what was in the crates and had never in his life been told what
was in anything, and because a docket is a docket.

*I will look at it when I have got it in front of me.*

He went up the last three flights and came out on the shoulder at about
eleven in the morning of the sixteenth of the twelfth month, and there was
a bell on a frame under its own little roof, and a hall, and a long low
building along the north side with the shutters up.

---

One more thing about those nine days, and it is the thing that mattered
most and he did not know it at the time and never wrote it down.

He was warm at night, he ate on eight of the nine days, his feet were
sound, and he arrived at the Kadono-dera on the sixteenth in good order
with a pack that still had two days of food in it.

**He had got very good at being a boy alone on a road in winter.**

Nobody had taught him any of it. He had bought it, item by item, out of
his own fees, over seven months, and every single piece of it was
something he had got wrong once and corrected in a book: the boots at
Anazawa, the mittens at Fukae, the kindling in a cooperage yard, the hat
off a man at the next stall.

And the one thing he had not corrected was on a rail in a shop in Ōtoma at
one thousand four hundred ryō, and he had eighteen thousand split four
ways in four places, and he was cold for four days because of it.

---

He stood on the shoulder and read the ground for about ninety seconds
before he moved, because reading the ground was the first thing and always
had been.

*Eleven men. Twelve.*

*Somebody in the hall and somebody behind it.*

*And nine or ten in the long building who are not men.*

He put that down and did not do anything with it, because a working temple
in the twelfth month is a temple with winter people in it and that is what
they are for.

Then he went down onto the ground toward the man on the beds, who had seen
him first.

*Act 1. A room. A boy, age twelve. POV: the boy. ~1,300 words.*

---

He had broken the fourth one on the bowl after he got the number, because
there was no reason to wait and waiting was not a thing he did with
anything except answers.

Ninety-one bowls since.

*And it is coming.*

He felt along the back of his left hand every morning and it was coming
and it was coming at about the rate the third one had come, and the third
one had taken a hundred and twenty-nine, and ninety-one out of a hundred
and twenty-nine was most of the way, and the whole of it was going exactly
as he had worked out that it would.

He was pleased about that in a way he had no word for.

*I said a thing about what was going to happen and then it happened.*

*That has not been true before.*

---

The bowl changed on the ninth.

He did not know it was the ninth of anything. He knew it was the eight
hundred and something-th bowl of his life in that room and he had stopped
counting the total a long time ago because the total was not useful, and
what he had was the ninety-one, which was.

What he knew was that it was different, and he knew it before he had it in
his hands, and he knew it off the weight of the man's arm coming down.

*That is not the same coming down.*

Then it was on the floor in front of him and he sat and looked at it and
did not touch it.

*It is more.*

---

He was very careful about that, because it was the sort of thing you can
want.

*I have been hungry for two years and a hungry person looking at a bowl is
the worst instrument there is.*

So he did what he did with everything, which was to find a way to be
wrong about it.

*It might be that it is heaped differently and it is the same amount.*

*It might be that I am hungrier today and everything looks like more.*

*It might be that it is the light.*

There was no light. That one went in about a second and he let it go and
did not mind, because ruling out the stupid one first is how you find out
whether you are thinking.

He ate it. He was not going to not eat it.

And afterwards he sat with his back to the wall and had a thing happen to
him that had not happened in that room, which was that he was not hungry.

He sat with it for a long time.

*I do not like this.*

*And I cannot say why I do not like it and that is because I have not got
anything to say it with.*

---

He needed a way to measure a bowl of millet and he had four days to get
one, and he got it on the second day and it took him most of that day to
be willing to do it.

He had four pieces on the mat now.

Three of them were bent or short or both, and one of them — the third, the
one that had given him the number — was three and a quarter thumbs and
straight the whole way, and he had put it at the end of the row because
that was where it went.

He looked at it for most of an afternoon.

*It is a straight thing of a known length.*

*And I have not got another one.*

---

He did it on the third day.

He turned the bowl so that the crack in the glaze on the outside was under
his right hand, which meant the bowl was the same way round every time,
and then he put the piece in at the edge, upright, until it touched the
bottom, and put his thumbnail on it where the millet came to, and took it
out and looked at it.

Then he ate.

He did that four days running, at the same one of the three bowls, and on
the fourth day he had four marks on a piece of his own hand and he put
them in a row against the light from the gap and looked at them.

*Three the same and one under.*

*And the one under was the day the man came late and I think it was
knocked.*

*So it is three the same.*

---

He put the piece back at the end of the row and sat with his hands in his
lap and had a fact.

*It is more, and it is the same more every time, and it has been the same
more for four days.*

He knew what that meant on its own, because he had got there in the sixth
month about something else and had never had to get there again.

*A thing that is the same every time is a thing somebody decided.*

*So somebody decided it. On a day. Before the ninth.*

And then he came to the end of what he had, and he came to the end of it
fast, and the coming to the end of it was the whole of the scene and he
would not have called it anything.

*And I do not know anything about the person who decided it.*

---

He went at it anyway, because it was there.

*More food is a thing you do when you want a thing to be bigger.*

*Or when you have got more food.*

*Or when the person who does the bowls has changed and the new one does it
differently, except that it is the same man, and I know the arms.*

*Or when something is going to happen and the thing that is going to
happen wants me to be able to walk.*

He stopped on that one, not because it frightened him — nothing in the
room frightened him, and he did not have very much to be frightened with —
but because he had noticed something about the four of them.

*I have got four readings and I cannot get at any of them.*

*And the reason is not that they are hard. It is that every single one of
them is about somebody upstairs, and I have never once been told anything
about anybody upstairs, and I have been here for two years.*

He turned his hands over and looked at the backs of them.

*I have got a hundred and twenty-nine and a hundred and fifty-one and a
hundred and seventy-eight, and I got all three of those off my own hand,
and they were right.*

*And I cannot tell you one thing about the man who brings the bowl except
how his arm comes down.*

---

He did not conclude anything.

That should be said the way he would have said it if he had had the words,
which he did not: **he did not conclude anything, and it was not a failure
of nerve and it was not a failure of thinking. There was nothing to
conclude with.** A boy who has been told nothing for two years has a
perfect instrument for measuring a bowl and no instrument at all for
measuring a decision, and he had just found that out, at twelve, on a mat,
with a piece of his own finger in his hand.

So he put it where the true things went.

*It is more. It has been more for four days. Somebody decided it. I do not
know why and I am not going to.*

And she did the other thing, and she did it fast, and she had it ready.

"I'll be worth having."

He stopped.

"I know the water and the ground and the wood and I can do a floor and I
can mend and I have got eight trips instead of eleven out of a step nobody
had looked at in nineteen years. I do not eat much. I am not going to
slow you down and if I do you can leave me, and I will not say anything
about it, and I will not follow you if you say now that I am not to."

She said all of it without stopping and without her voice going anywhere,
and she had plainly had it in her head for some time, and she got to the
end of it and stood there.

---

He thought about it for about four seconds and the four seconds were not
about any of that.

*None of that is why.*

*She is going to follow me. She said she would not, and she will, and she
said it because she has worked out that offering not to is what makes it
possible for me to say yes, and she is right, and she has done that in
about a minute in a room with six dead men in it.*

*So the actual question is what refusing costs.*

*Refusing costs a girl on a mountain road in the twelfth month with nine
people who are going to walk to Ōgi and say a thing, and it costs me
somebody who knows this building walking down that road four days behind
me talking to whoever asks her.*

*And allowing costs a bowl and a half a day and being two people on a road
instead of one.*

He got to the end of it and there was nothing after it.

*Two people on a road in winter are a father and a child, or a brother and
a sister, and nobody has ever asked a father anything.*

He did not notice where he had got that from. It had been said to him in
that room ninety minutes earlier by a man who was four feet to his left on
the floor.

"Get whatever you've got," he said.

"I haven't got anything."

"Then we go now."

---

They went out through the south door at about twenty past eleven and
crossed the ground and nobody came out of anything.

He went past the top of the four steps at the north end and did not look
down them.

She stopped for two seconds at a place on the ground about eight feet from
the top of them and picked up a piece of sacking with charcoal marks on it
and folded it twice and put it inside her coat, and did not explain it,
and he did not ask.

Then they went down the steps out of the Kadono-dera, eleven flights of
them, cut in different centuries, and the light was flat and white and it
was the seventeenth of the twelfth month.

*Act 1, month 12, the seventeenth to the twenty-ninth. The mountain road,

They did four miles on the seventeenth and eleven on the eighteenth and
she did not complain once, and he had not expected her to and had not
expected anything else either, and it turned out that having no
expectations about a person is a poor way to travel with one.

She was slower than him going up and faster going down, which he worked
out on the first afternoon and did not understand until the third, when he
watched her come down a set of steps and saw that she was going down the
outside of them where the tread was not worn.

*She is not being careful. She has found the good line and she is on it.*

*And she found it in about a day and a half on a mountain she has never
been down.*

He put that in the operational column that night and did not put a name on
it because he did not have one.

---

The other thing about the first three days was that she talked, and he had
no procedure for that either.

She did not talk at him. That was the part he could not get hold of. She
talked at about a fifth of the time, in short pieces, mostly about what
was in front of them — where the water was going, why the wall on the
uphill side was built the way it was, that the frost had come out of the
south side of a rock face and not the north — and when he did not answer,
which was most of the time, she stopped and went on walking, and started
again forty minutes later about something else.

On the second evening he worked out what it was and it took him a while
because he had never seen it before.

*She is not asking me anything.*

*Not once in two days. She has said about two hundred things and not one of
them was a question, and she is not doing that by accident.*

He thought about it for a while and did not arrive anywhere, and put it
down, and the answer would have cost her a great deal to give and he never
asked for it.

---

They came into Ōgi on the twenty-first and he bought her a coat.

It took four minutes.

The man wanted six hundred and Tadayoshi got it at four hundred and
fifty, and he got it by doing nothing at all except stand there and say
four hundred and look at the door once, and the man came down in two
steps, and the whole thing was over so fast that he was outside on the
street with a coat in his hands before he understood what had happened.

He stood in the street and had it.

*I did not want it.*

*That is the whole of it. That is the entire thing and it took a boot and
four hundred and eighty ryō and eight weeks to get to and it is one
sentence: I could not buy boots because I wanted them, and I can buy a
coat because I do not.*

He looked at the coat.

*And the correction I wrote on the twenty-eighth of the tenth month was
**do not touch the thing first**, and that is not the correction. That is
a way of hiding it. The correction is that I am the leak, and there is no
procedure for not being the person who wants a thing.*

He gave her the coat and she put it on and it was too big, deliberately,
because he had bought it too big on purpose, and she looked at the sleeves
and then at him and did not say thank you.

She said, "How much did you get off him?"

"A hundred and fifty."

"Right," she said, and did the cuffs over twice, and that was the whole of
the conversation about it.

They were in Ōtoma on the twenty-eighth of the twelfth month.

He gave Ōtsuka his half of the receipt and told him, standing in the yard,
in about ninety seconds, and it was accurate and in order and he did not
leave anything out.

That the crates could not be moved before the spring and had never been
intended to be. That the consignment on the sheet was not the consignment.
That the client was dead, along with five other people, and that he had
done it, and that he could not give an account of the middle of it.

That there was no fee.

That there were nine or ten people who had walked out of that valley by
now and had almost certainly been to Ōgi.

And that there was a girl.

---

Ōtsuka listened to the whole of it with the half-receipt in his hand and
did not interrupt, and at the end of it he looked at the girl for about a
second and a half and then back at Tadayoshi.

"Right," he said.

"That's it?"

"What do you want me to say?"

"Something," said Tadayoshi, and it came out of him without any decision
in it, which was the second time in two months and he noticed it while it
was happening and could not stop it.

Ōtsuka put the receipt in his coat.

"I'll write it up," he said. "You'll want to be somewhere for a bit."

Then he went and did something to a barrel, and that was the whole of it,
and nobody in that building said one word to him about the seventeenth of
the twelfth month for the rest of his life.

He got to it on the twenty-ninth.

He was on the boards in the loft in the afternoon with the ledger open
doing the ordinary thing, which was to close the month, and he came to
the back of the book to check a date on the list headed **PEOPLE WHO WILL
TELL SOMEBODY**, and he turned two leaves too many.

The page had five lines on it and a date in the top corner and no heading.

He read it.

> *Not the ones who did not choose.*

> *I do not know what did not choose means. That is the stroke the whole
> of it hangs off and I cannot say what it is for. Whoever comes to widen
> this will come at that word and they will be right that it is loose.*
>
> *No exceptions. If there is an exception I will find it, because I am
> good at finding them, and it will arrive with the paperwork correct.*
>
> *This is not backwards from today. It does not undo the twentieth. I

> head, because it is the first exception and it is on the same sheet as

>
> *I am writing this after. I know I am writing it after.*

He sat and looked at it for a long time.

*I did not look at this.*

*I stood on a hall floor on the seventeenth of the twelfth month and did
the three questions and got to the end of them and said all right, and
this was in the coat I was wearing, two leaves past a list I had written
myself, and it did not come into my head.*

*It was thirty-seven days old.*

He went at it the only way he had.

*Does it cover her?*

*She is ten. She was in a cellar for forty-five days. She was priced on
the twenty-eighth of the tenth month by a man she had never met and she
was not asked about any part of it.*

*Yes. Obviously yes. It is the clearest case there will ever be and I would
have got it in about a second if anybody had put it to me on a sheet.*

*So why did it not come.*

---

He found the answer in about four minutes and it was worse than not
finding one.

*Because it says not the ones who did not choose, and I wrote it about a
boy I might have killed, and I have read it ever since as a rule about
killing.*

*And I was not going to kill her.*

He put his hand flat on the page.

*That is a technicality.*

*That is a technicality and it is the exact shape of the technicalities I
take apart for money, and if a man in a room said that sentence to me
about somebody else's rule I would have the load-bearing stroke out of it
in four seconds and tell him what he had done.*

*I wrote **no exceptions** on this page thirty-seven days ago and the first
one came thirty-seven days later and it did not even feel like one. It
felt like the rule not being about it.*

---

He tried to write the correction and he could not make it be the right
shape.

He got as far as the obvious one and put it down and looked at it and knew
immediately that it was not it.

> *Read this page before every job. Not after.*

*That is not the correction.*

*Reading it would not have done it. I would have read it on the sixteenth
and gone in on the seventeenth and it would still have been a rule about
killing, because that is what I think it says, and I would have read it
and not seen it.*

*So the correction is not about when I read it. It is about what I think
it says. And I cannot correct what I think a thing says by writing another
thing.*

He left the line where it was and put a small mark beside it, the mark he
used for a number he had made and not counted, which he had never once
used on a sentence, and which meant, when he used it: *this is what I have
got and it is not the answer.*

Then he shut the book on it, because there was nothing else in him.

---

She was on the boards by the wall with her back against it and had been
for about two hours, doing nothing, in a coat that was too big, and had
not said anything for the whole of it.

He got the book out again and turned two leaves on from the page with no
heading and ruled a fresh one, because a person you are carrying goes in a
column and he did not have one.

Then he stopped, because he did not have the first thing you need.

"What's your name?"

She looked up.

It took her about a second and a half, and he did not know what was in the
second and a half and never found out, and what was in it was that in two
hundred and seventy-three days — on a road, and in a house with a brick
wall in it, and on a hall floor, and in a cellar — the last person who had
asked her that had written it down and then put a number under it.

"Uzuki," she said.

"The rest of it."

"Nozaki."

He wrote it.

He wrote it at the top of a page in the small exact hand, the way he wrote
everything, and then he sat with the pen over the paper for a while
because there was supposed to be a heading above it and there was not one,
and he tried for about four minutes the way he had tried in the eleventh
month and got nothing that was not either a lie or a description of a
feeling.

So he left the top of the page white, and the name under it, and nothing
else on it at all.

> *Nozaki Uzuki.*
