# Chapter 15, Scene 7 — The Pile He Did Not Open

*Act 2, year 1, month 3, the fifteenth to the eighteenth. Ōtoma.
Tadayoshi Ruri, age eleven. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,600 words.*

---

He got the frame out again on the fifteenth and this time he knew exactly
what he was looking for, which is the part that makes it worse.

He had it as a sentence before he lifted the boards.

*Somebody who writes like that has written like that before.*

*So: find one.*

*And if I find one, then I have got two, and two is a line, and a line is
a thing you can follow.*

---

He set it up properly, because he set everything up properly.

He cleared the boards and put the four bundles out in front of him and
untied them and made two piles, and he made the two piles before he read
anything, because sorting is the first thing and he had known that since
he was nine.

On the left: the nine, and the two he had turned down.

On the right: the sixty-one.

And then he sat back on his heels and looked at the two piles and did the
thing that decided the next three years, and it took him about ninety
seconds, and he did it out loud in his head in order like everything else.

---

*Which pile.*

*Start with what I have got. I have got a sheet that is written to be
forgettable. Somebody sat down and took the memorable parts out of it on
purpose, and taking the memorable parts out of a document is work, and you
do not do work for nothing.*

*So: why would you.*

*Because there is something in it you do not want remembered.*

He was pleased with that and he was right about it, and it was the last
correct step he took for five days.

*And a thing you do not want remembered is a thing that matters.*

*So it is a job that matters.*

He looked at the left-hand pile.

*Which is that one.*

---

He did the freight objection because he did every objection, and this is
the part that is creditable and it is why it took five days and not one.

*Unless it is in the freight.*

He sat with it.

*Sixty-one of them. And I have read all sixty-one and I have never once
found one strange.*

*So either there is not one in there, or there is one in there and it did
not look strange to me.*

He went at the second half of that, and he got most of the way, and where
he stopped is exactly here:

*Freight is the most standard writing there is. Four clerks in this town
write the whole of it and they all write it the same and they have all
been doing it for years, and it is dates and weights and yards and it is
the same eleven phrases in the same order sixty-one times.*

*So a strange one would stand out in freight more than anywhere. A strange
one in a pile of identical ones is the easiest thing in the world to spot,
and I would have spotted it, because I read them all and I remember them.*

He turned it over once more to be sure it held.

*And a job that matters is not freight. Freight is boxes. Nobody writes a
careful sheet about a box.*

It held.

He put the sixty-one back in a stack, squared them, tied the tie round the
short way, and set them aside on the boards about eighteen inches from his
knee, where they stayed for four days while he worked.

---

He did the nine on the sixteenth and the seventeenth and he did them
thoroughly and it is good work and it is worth setting down what good
work looks like when it is aimed at the wrong thing.

He took each one and he did the reduction on it. He found the load-bearing
item. He wrote the item down beside the date. Then he read the sheet again
for the hand and for the shape and for anything that made his chest do the
thing it had done on the fifth.

**Anazawa, 26/9.** *West lane.* Nothing.

**The 2/10 one.** *The room above the stable and not the house.* Nothing.

**The 9/10.** Nothing.

**Terasaka, 20/10.** Fourteen thousand, three sheets, a map on the back of
the second one drawn by somebody who had been there. Nothing. It was the
least forgettable document he owned.

**The 24/10.** Nothing.

**3/11.** Nothing.

**The nine hundred, 5/1.** He sat with that one longer than the others,
for the reason he always sat with it longer, and it was not this reason
and he knew it was not, and he put it down.

**And the two he turned down.** Nothing and nothing.

---

He checked three of them a second time on the eighteenth — the two big
ones and the nine hundred — because three is where he stopped, and he
stopped at three because a thing you have checked twice and got the same
answer twice is a thing you have checked.

Then he sat on the boards with nine dockets in an arc round him and the
Sarugaya sheet on his knee and the sixty-one tied up eighteen inches from
his left leg, and he did the honest thing, which he did every time and
which is the only reason any of the rest of it is worth anything.

*I am wrong.*

*I said: if somebody can write like that, they have written like that
before. That is still true and I still believe it.*

*And I said: the ones before this are in yards. Which is also true.*

*And then I said: and I have got seventy-two of them under the boards,
and that was the mistake, because I have not. I have got seventy-two of
**mine**.*

He looked at the arc of paper.

*Nine of these are the kind of job a person would take trouble over and I
have checked all nine and there is nothing in them.*

*So the ones before this went to somebody else.*

*Which is not surprising and is not information. There are forty men on
those boards in a year and I get about one in six.*

He put the nine back in their bundle and did the tie twice, the way he
did.

> *18/3. Checked the nine + the two. Nothing like it. It is not one of
> mine.*

And he put the mark beside it, because he had reasoned it and not counted
it, and by now he had used that mark on three things in a fortnight that
were not numbers and had stopped noticing that he was doing it.

---

Then he put the frame back.

He put the nine in first because they went at the bottom, and then the two
he had turned down, and then the sixty-one on top of them, still tied,
still squared, and he pressed the whole of it down with the flat of his
hand to get the lid to sit, which he did every time.

The sixty-one went in unopened.

He had had them out on the boards for four days. He had moved them twice.
He had put his hand on top of the stack to square it on the fifteenth and
again on the eighteenth, and at no point in four days had he taken the tie
off, and the reason he had not was that he had thought about it, carefully,
for ninety seconds, and had produced two arguments, and both of them were
sound, and he could have defended either of them to anybody.

He put the boards back down and put the tin on top of them.

He went out to see about the mule.

---

The sheet he wanted was the fourth one down from the top of that pile.

It was dated the twelfth of the eighth month of the year before last and
it was a consignment of dyestuff from Ōtoma to a yard south of Kanda, two
crates, nine days, eight hundred ryō. It was one of the first freight
sheets he ever took. He was ten. He walked it in seven days and was paid
on the ninth and thought nothing whatever about it at the time or since,
and there was no reason he should have.

It has forty-one words on it.

There is no item in it that is in it once.
