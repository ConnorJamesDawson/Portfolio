# Chapter 10, Scene 3 — Eleven Minutes

*Act 1, month 9, the twenty-sixth. Anazawa. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV:
Tadayoshi. ~1,720 words.*

---

He was in the lane behind the shutter-maker's for two hours and eleven
minutes before it started, and he had planned for three, and the two hours
were the job.

That is the thing nobody would have believed if he had told them and he
never told anybody. The two hours were the job. What came after was the
part that took no thinking at all.

He had the ground by the first evening — *packed earth over rubble, and it
does not hold a print in this weather, and the drain at the top of the
lane is the only soft strip and it is four feet wide* — and he had the
routine by the second.

Sakuta shut the front at about seven. Sakuta ate at about eight, upstairs,
where the light was. Sakuta came down at about half past nine and did an
hour in the back with the door to the lane open two inches for the smoke,
because he had a brazier and no chimney and he was not going to sit in it.

*Every night. Four nights out of four. Two inches.*

---

He waited from half past seven.

Eight minutes of the eleven were waiting and he wanted that written down
somewhere in his own hand afterwards, and it went into the book as a time
and not as a sentence.

He waited in the dark against the wall of the yard next door with his
hands inside his coat because his hands were cold and cold hands are slow,
and he did not move and did not shift his weight and did not look at the
door more than about once a minute, because looking at a thing is how you
get seen looking at it.

At twenty past nine a woman came down the lane with a covered pot and did
not see him, and he did not move for her either.

At twenty-eight minutes past, the door went to two inches.

He gave it eleven more minutes because the man had to settle, and because
the brazier had to be up, and because a man leaning over a brazier is a
man with his eyes ruined for the dark.

---

He came in through the two inches at nine forty and did not open it any
further than his own shoulders.

Sakuta was on a stool with his back three-quarters to the door and a
plane in his hand and the brazier at his knee, and there was a smell of
charcoal and shavings and something with vinegar in it that had been his
supper, and he was about forty and going thin on top and he had a good
back for a man who sat down all day.

He did not turn round. There was nothing to turn round for. A door that is
open two inches does not make a noise when it becomes a door that is open
sixteen.

Tadayoshi crossed four feet of floor and put his left hand flat on the
top of the man's head and pulled it back and put the knife in under the
jaw on the left side, in, and across, and out.

---

The blade went through the strap of muscle at the side of the neck and
through the pipe and out the other side of it, and when he drew it back
the wound opened up the way a seam opens when you pull it, and the blood
came out of the artery in a hard rope that hit the wall six feet away and
went on hitting it, and the second one was lower than the first, and the
third was lower than that.

Sakuta did not die.

That is the part he had been told about, at eight, in a compound, by a man
who did not know he was telling a child anything: *it takes longer than
you think and they know.* He had not believed it and he believed it at
nine forty-one on the twenty-sixth of the ninth month.

The man came off the stool onto his knees and then onto his side and put
one hand up to his neck, which is what they do, and the hand did nothing
at all because there was nothing a hand could do, and the noise he made
was not a shout. It was the noise you make trying to breathe through a
thing that is filling up, and it went on, and it was wet, and it changed
about every four seconds as the level in it changed.

His feet were going. Both of them, against the boards, not kicking —
walking. As though the floor were a road and he could get up it.

---

Tadayoshi stepped over him and went to the brazier.

He put the plane back on the bench where the plane went, because a plane
on the floor is a thing somebody notices and a plane on the bench is
Tuesday. He put the shavings that had come off the stool back into the
heap with the side of his foot. He took the pot off the top of the brazier
and put it on the trivet because it had been on the trivet at seven and he
had watched the man move it.

Behind him the noise was still going and the feet were still going.

He picked up the four-inch offcut he had put down on his way in, which had
been on the floor by the door and which he had moved eleven inches so that
it would not be under the swing of the door, and put it back the eleven
inches.

The noise stopped somewhere in there and he did not hear it stop.

---

He did the leaving properly and it took him four minutes and it was the
best work he had ever done.

**The lamp.** It was on the shelf and it was low and it was going to be
out in about half an hour on its own, and a lamp that is out means a man
who went to bed and a lamp that has been blown out means a man who was not
alone. He left it.

**The door.** Two inches. Not shut. He came out through his own shoulders'
width and put his hand flat against it and let it back to two inches with
the pressure on, so that the latch tongue did not travel and did not
click.

**The drain strip**, four feet of soft ground at the top of the lane,
which he had walked round on the way in and walked round on the way out,
and it took eleven seconds longer both times and he had costed the
twenty-two seconds on the first evening.

**The dog** at the corner of the west lane, which had barked at him on the
first night and which he had spent forty minutes of the second night
sitting near, so that on the fourth night he was a thing that had been
there before.

It did not bark.

---

He was on the north road out of Anazawa by ten past ten and he walked for
five hours.

He did not think about it. That is exact and it should stay exact: he did
not think about it, on the road, at any point, and he checked at about
one in the morning whether he was avoiding thinking about it and found
that he was not, and that there was nothing there to avoid.

What he thought about was the twenty-two seconds at the drain, which he
had spent twice and which he now believed had been unnecessary, because
the strip was not as soft as it had been on the first evening and he could
have taken it straight and saved forty-four seconds across the two
crossings.

*Forty-four seconds is not nothing.*

*If there had been anything on that lane on the way out, forty-four
seconds is the difference between being in it and being past it.*

He resolved to check the ground on the day and not on the first evening,
and put that in the book as a correction with the date beside it, sitting
on a milestone at about three in the morning with his hands inside his
coat.

---

There was blood on his boots and on the outside of his right sleeve to
about the elbow.

He dealt with the sleeve at a stream at about four, and did it properly,
in cold water, from the outside in, and it took twenty minutes and it did
not all come out and he had known it would not.

The boots he did at the same stream and made a worse job of them, because
the welt takes it and the welt does not give it back, and at about half
past four he sat on the bank with one boot in his hand and looked at the
seam of it in the dark and worked out that it was going to be in there for
weeks and that there was nothing to be done except own a second pair.

He put that on the list of things to buy with the three thousand six
hundred, under *a coat that is not this coat.*

---

The entry went in on the twenty-seventh, in the morning, in the ordinary
column.

> *26/9. Anazawa. Sakuta, shutter-maker, west lane.*
>
> *Done. 3,600 due.*

And under it, ruled off, in the operational column where he kept the
things he had learned:

> *Read the ground on the day. The drain strip was not what it was on the
> first evening and I paid 44 sec for a condition that had changed.*
>
> *The dog cost 40 min on the second night and was worth it.*
>
> *Second boots.*

He read the page back before he shut the book, which he always did.

Two lines and a number, and three corrections, and a note about
footwear.

**And nothing at all on the sheets**, which had a column on them called
*what it cost*, and which he had ruled on the third of the seventh month,
and which had two lines on them, and which he did not open on the
twenty-sixth of the ninth month, or on the twenty-seventh.
