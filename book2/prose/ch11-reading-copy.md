# Chapter 11 — A Reason I Can Take Upstairs

*Book two. Act one, months 10 and 11.*
*11 scenes, 19,750 words. Tadayoshi ×6 · Uzuki ×4 · Kimimaro ×1.*



*Act 1, month 10, the thirteenth to the sixteenth. Ōtoma and Ōgi.
Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,700 words.*

---

He said yes on the Thursday morning at the bottom of the ladder, which
was where Ōtsuka was standing, and he said it before Ōtsuka had asked.

"Yes."

"Right," said Ōtsuka.

That was the whole of it and there was nothing else in it, and the reason
there was nothing else in it was that he had done all of the something
else two nights before, on the boards, in the dark, and had come out the
other side of it with a thing standing in the road that he had no name
for and no column for. He had looked at the thing for two days. It had
not moved and it had not resolved and it had not stopped him, and at some
point on Wednesday night he had understood that it was not going to do
any of those, and that a thing which does not stop you is a thing you
have gone past.

So: yes.

Then he asked the question.

---

"Who's in the house?"

Ōtsuka had already turned to go and he did not finish turning.

Tadayoshi watched him not finish it and made a note of it in the place
where he kept things he did not understand yet, which by the tenth month
was getting to be a large place.

*He has heard that before.*

*No. He has not heard it before from me, and he has heard it before from
somebody, and the two of those are not the same and I cannot tell which
one that face is.*

"Why?"

"Because the fee is fourteen thousand and Anazawa was three six, and the
only rule I have got is that the fee is for who the man was. So either
Terasaka is four times Sakuta, which I do not believe, or it is not one
man, and if it is not one man I need to know how many it is, because a
house with four in it is not a house with one in it and it is not four
houses either."

Ōtsuka looked at him for a bit.

"You'll get it Saturday," he said.

---

He got it Saturday, on a strip of paper with no rule on it, in a hand he
did not know, and he read it in the loft with the door shut.

> *Terasaka, ab. 60.*
>
> *Son, ab. 30.*
>
> *Son's wife.*
>
> *Brother, farms the top field, sleeps in the house.*
>
> *A boy, 7.*

He sat with it a long time and the first thing he did was the arithmetic,
because the arithmetic was there and it wanted doing.

*Fourteen thousand over four is three and a half.*

*Anazawa was three six. Sakabe was two nine. Nunobiki was four one for a
man who was worth four days of somebody else's waiting.*

*So four at three and a half is the line. It is exactly the line. Whoever
priced this priced it off the same series I have got, or off the series
that made mine, and it comes out at four.*

He put his thumb on the last line.

*Which means the boy is not in it.*

---

He wrote that down, later, and it is worth being exact about the order,
because the order is the whole of it:

**He did the division first. Then he found the boy on the paper. Then he
worked out that the boy was not in the price. And then he decided that
the boy was not in the count.**

Those last two are not the same statement and he did not notice that they
were not the same statement, and nobody in that building ever told him,
and the reason nobody ever told him is that he did not say it out loud
to anybody. He said it in a loft with the door shut, to himself, in the
tone he used for arithmetic, and arithmetic does not get argued with.

*Four in the count. One not.*

He turned the strip over. There was nothing on the back.

---

He went down at about four and found Ōtsuka in the yard with a barrel.

"There's a boy on it."

"I know."

"He's seven."

"I know that as well."

Tadayoshi waited, because waiting was usually the cheapest way to get a
thing, and Ōtsuka went on with the barrel and did not give him anything
at all, and after about fifteen seconds Tadayoshi understood that he was
not going to and that this was information.

*He is not going to tell me the boy is in it and he is not going to tell
me the boy is out of it.*

*So either he has not been told, or he has been told and has been told
not to say, or there is nothing to say because nobody has thought about
it.*

*Three, and no way in.*

"Where is he usually?"

"Ōgi," said Ōtsuka, straightening up. "The mother's sister. That's all
I've got and it came off a man who was paid for it, so I'd not spend it
without looking at it."

---

That was the last useful thing anybody said to him about the job, and it
was, in the end, the only piece of advice he had ever been given by that
organisation that he took entirely and completely and to the letter.

He went and looked at it.

He left Ōtoma on the fourteenth and was in Ōgi on the fifteenth in the
afternoon, and he did the thing he did, which was to find the lane and
find the door and then not go anywhere near either of them for
twenty-six hours.

There is a tannery at the north end of Ōgi and behind it a lane with nine
doors on it, and the fourth one along has a step with a crack in it and a
pot by the step that is not for a plant. He sat on the roof of a
lean-to at the far end from three in the afternoon until dark, and he
came back at first light on the sixteenth, and in that time he saw the
door open eleven times and he saw four children and one of them was a boy
of about seven who was not like the other three, in the way that a child
who lives somewhere is not like a child who is stopping.

*That is him.*

*And that is not enough.*

---

He knew exactly why it was not enough and he had known before he came,
and he had come anyway, because the looking was cheap and because he did
not know how to not look at a thing.

*I have got the fifteenth and the sixteenth. The job is the nineteenth.*

*A boy who is at a door on the sixteenth is a boy who was at a door on
the sixteenth. It says nothing whatever about the nineteenth. If I take
it as saying something about the nineteenth I am doing the thing I take
other people apart for.*

*Watching gives me the past. There is nothing in the world that gives me
the future except somebody telling me.*

He sat on the lean-to with that for about an hour.

*So I have got to ask.*

---

He went to the door at the end of the afternoon on the sixteenth,
because the end of the afternoon is when a door is busy and a busy door
does not remember.

A woman opened it with a cloth in her hand. She was about forty and she
had the sleeves up and there was somebody shouting about something
behind her in a way that was not about anything.

He said he had been sent up from the weir with a word for the boy who
was stopping here, from his grandfather, and had he got the right door.

She said he had.

Then she said the thing, and she said it in the tone in which people say
things that have been true so long they have stopped being facts and
have become furniture, and she said it while looking back over her
shoulder at whatever was going on in the room.

"He stops here for the winter. He has done for three years."

---

He said thank you and that he would come back, and went up the lane and
round the corner and stopped and got the book out and wrote it down.

Not the sense of it. The sentence.

> *16/10. Ōgi, lane behind the tannery, 4th door. The mother's sister.*
>
> *"He stops here for the winter. He has done for three years."*
>
> *Not asked leading. Volunteered. She was looking at something else
> when she said it.*

He looked at the last line for a while and then underlined it, because it
was the part that made it worth anything.

*A thing a person says while looking at something else is a thing they
are not making up, because making a thing up takes attention and her
attention was in the room.*

*Three years is a pattern.*

*A pattern is the only thing I have ever been able to plan on.*

He put the book away and walked out of Ōgi on the north road, past the
shrine at the top of the town, and behind the shrine there was a long
low building with the shutters up and a woman sweeping the end of it, and
he did not look at it twice, because it was a building.

---

He did not go back to Ōtoma, because Ōtoma was a day and a half the
wrong way and the mill ground was four hours up the valley from where he
was standing. He went east and then north and was above the second weir
by the evening of the seventeenth, and gave it the eighteenth and the
nineteenth.

The other thing had happened before he left, on the evening of the
thirteenth, and it was not about the job at all and he had been putting
it off for six days. He had gone down to the cooperage yard and stood in
the doorway until somebody came past, and had asked, in the flat way that
was the only way he asked anything, whether Kajiya was about.

The man said he'd not seen him.

"Since when?"

"I've not thought about it," said the man, and went on with what he was
carrying.

Tadayoshi had gone back up the ladder and got the book and found it in
eleven seconds, because it was in the ordinary column and it was dated.

> *2/8.*

He had sat and looked at it.

*Seventy-one days.*

He had not written anything under it, because he had nothing to write,
and because the column he would have wanted was one he had already ruled
somewhere else and had not opened in a month.

He thought about it once more on the eighteenth, lying in a hedge above a
weir with four people asleep below him, and then did not think about it
again for a year.

*Act 1, month 10, the fourteenth to the twenty-fifth. The Kadono-dera.
Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,500 words.*

---

They were good weeks and she was not going to pretend otherwise
afterwards, and she never did.

There were four things that wanted doing at the Kadono-dera in the tenth
month and she did all four of them better than anybody there, and the
reason was not that she was clever about them. It was that she was ten and
had two working arms and did not stop, and every single other person in
that building was a man over forty who had been carrying water up the same
slope for nineteen years and had arrived at a pace.

Water, first. Two buckets from the tank below the hall to the kitchen and
the hall and the long building, about forty feet of climb, and the tank
filled off the run and the run wanted clearing about every third day.

The ground, second, which was Tokuji's and which he let her into by
degrees.

The floors, which nobody liked.

And the wood.

---

By the eighteenth she had the water down from eleven trips to eight,
because she had worked out that the reason it was eleven was that the
buckets went up half-full, and the reason they went up half-full was the
step at the turn, and the reason the step at the turn was a problem was
that it was worn hollow in the middle and everybody had been going up the
middle for nineteen years.

She went up the outside of it. Full buckets, eight trips.

She told Tokuji about it because she could not not tell people things, and
he looked at the step for a while, and then at her, and said, "How long
did that take you?"

"Four days."

"Right," he said, and went back to the beds.

She was very pleased about that for the rest of the afternoon and could
not have said why, and worked it out that night on the mat, and did not
like the answer.

*Because somebody wanted a thing I can do.*

*That has not happened since the fourth month.*

---

The wood was hers by the twentieth and it was hers by default, which is
how everything is allocated in a place where nobody is in charge of
allocation.

There were two stands of it below the road and the cut stuff came up on a
sled, and it wanted splitting and it wanted stacking under the eave of the
long building before the twelfth month, and the reason it had not been
done was that of the eleven men, four had backs and two had hands and the
rest had other things they were doing.

She could not split much. She was ten and it was a full axe.

She could stack, and she could carry, and she could sort what wanted
splitting from what did not, which nobody had ever bothered to do, and by
the twenty-fifth there were four ranks under the eave where there had been
one, and she knew to about a day how long each of them would be dry.

---

The other thing happened on the seventeenth and she did not plan it and it
frightened her when it was over.

Tokuji had a hip.

She had known it since the first afternoon, because it was the sort of
thing she could not stop seeing: it was the left one, and it was not the
hip, and she had been watching him get up off the beds for four days
before she understood what she was looking at.

*It is not the joint. He goes down onto it fine. It is coming up.*

*And it is not the leg either, because the leg is doing what it is told.*

*It is the back, on the left, low, and the hip is where he feels it, and
he thinks it is the hip, and he has thought it is the hip for years,
because that is where it hurts.*

She sat with that for three days and did not say anything, because she had
learned in the eighth month what happens when you tell people true things
about their bodies that they did not ask for.

Then on the seventeenth he came off the beds badly and had to put a hand
on the stone wall and stand there, and she was four feet away with a
basket, and she said it.

"It isn't your hip."

---

He did not turn round.

"Isn't it," he said.

"No. It's your back. Low, on the left. Your hip is where it comes out."

"And how would you know that?"

"Because I've been watching you get up for four days and your hip does
everything else it's supposed to."

He straightened the rest of the way and turned round and looked at her
properly for about the second time since she had arrived.

"Can you do anything about it?"

"Yes."

"Right, then."

---

She did it that night, in the dark, in the store at the end of the long
building, on the condition she named before she started, which she had
thought about all afternoon and which she gave him flat, like a price.

"Nobody knows."

"Why?"

"Because that's the condition."

He looked at her for a while in the dark, and she could not read what was
in it and never did.

"Nobody knows," he said.

---

It took twenty-five minutes and she was worse at it than she had been.

That was the finding and she made herself have it while she was doing it,
which was against the rule, and she knew it was against the rule.

*Little, stop, wait. And I have not done this since the twenty-sixth of the
eighth month.*

*So the going is fine. The going was always fine. It is the stopping that
has gone off — I am trailing off instead of stopping, about one in six,
and trailing off is not stopping.*

*And at about sixty I am going to want to push, and I am going to push,
and I know that about myself now, so the thing to do is count.*

She counted. She got to ninety and came off, and sat back on her heels in
the dark and waited the forty seconds, and only then let herself have an
opinion about how it had gone.

*Because you cannot tell during.*

---

She did it once more, on the twenty-second, and it was better than the
first one.

And that was the whole of it, and it was the largest thing about her, and
nobody in that building ever knew it happened.

She thought about that on the twenty-fourth, stacking wood under the eave
in the last of the light, and it arrived whole and she did not have to
work at it.

*They know what I am worth up here.*

*Eight trips instead of eleven. Four ranks under the eave. Six hours of
floors. I have made all of that visible on purpose and they have all seen
it and it is why I get the bowl and I am not sorry about any of it.*

*And the thing I did in the store is worth more than every single bit of
that put together, and nobody has seen it, and nobody is going to.*

She put the last of the rank up and squared the end of it with her palm.

*And that is not an accident. I did that. I made the condition and he kept
it.*

---

She did not have a word for why and she had a reason, and it was not the
reason she would have given if anybody had asked.

The reason she would have given was *because it is mine.*

The reason underneath it, which she got to on the mat that night with the
sacking in her hand and could not put on the sacking because there was no
column for it, was older than the Kadono-dera and older than the road.

**Everything about her that anybody had ever been able to see had been
turned into a number by somebody else.**

*The hair. My father had a thing about me that he could see and he dyed it
every two months for six years and would not say why, and then he was
dead, and then it was the debt, and then it was the house, and then Kuriya
was in our kitchen putting a figure on the pans.*

*And Shimoda stood in a yard and said what a mule was worth, item by item,
fairly, out loud, in front of me, while I held the halter.*

*So the things they can see get priced. Every time. That is not a fear, it
is a list, and I have got the list.*

She lay down on the mat, in the room with ten empty mats in it, with four
days left before the rain.

*So they can price the water and the wood.*

*They are not having the hands.*

*Act 1, month 10, the nineteenth and the twentieth. The mill ground above

He gave it two nights, which was more than he had given anything since
Anazawa and less than he wanted, and he was aware of both of those and
wrote them down afterwards.

*Sakabe was forty minutes of setting for one man in a lodging.*

*This is four in a house they have all lived in for thirty years, and
they know where the boards are and I do not, and there is no version of
this where I know the house better than they do.*

*So what I am buying with two nights is not knowledge of the house. It is
the order.*

He had the order by the end of the first night and spent the second one
checking it, and the checking changed one thing, and the one thing it
changed was the only part of the plan that did not survive contact.

---

The house sat on the shoulder above the second weir with the mill below
it and eleven acres running back and up. There was a lean-to off the
kitchen where the brother slept because the brother had slept there since
before the son was born. There was the old man by the fire in the front
room, on a bed, downstairs, because of the hip. There was a room at the
top of the stair on the left with two in it. And there was a small room
at the end of the passage with the door shut, and on both nights the
door was shut and no light went under it and nobody went in or out.

*So the small room is empty and it is the boy's, and it is empty because
he stops at Ōgi for the winter and has done for three years.*

That was the arithmetic and it was correct and it had been checked, and
he did not look at that room again on either night.

---

He went in at twenty past two on the twentieth through the kitchen door,
which had a latch and no bolt because a house with four grown people in
it does not bolt against a valley it has farmed for thirty years.

Upstairs first.

That was the order and it had one reason: a man of thirty who wakes up
and comes down a stair behind you is the entire job. Everything else in
the building could be dealt with lying down. That one could not, so that
one went first, and the price of putting it first was that he had to
cross the whole ground floor past two sleeping people to get to the
stair, and he paid it, and it took him four minutes to go eleven feet at
the bottom step because the bottom step had a voice.

The woman was nearest the door.

He did her with the knife across the throat, left to right, deep, with
his other hand flat on her forehead pushing back and down into the
bolster so the pipe came up under the edge, and she did not make a word.
What she made was a sound out of the opening itself, which is not a
voice and does not carry, and her hands came up and got hold of his
forearm and held on to it hard, with the fingers working, the way you
hold on to a rail, and he let her, because a hand on a forearm is not a
problem and a hand reaching sideways for a person is.

She was still holding on when he stepped away from her, and she went on
for some time after that. He knew she would. He had costed it.

The son was not in the bed.

That was the ninety seconds and it started there, at about half past two,
standing at the foot of a bed with one person in it and a hollow beside
her that was still warm enough to see in the dark.

*He is up.*

*He has been up since before I came up the stair or he got up while I was
doing her, and if it is the second one I have got a man behind me.*

He turned round.

The passage went two ways from the top of the stair and one of them was
the stair and the other was four paces to the small room with the shut
door, and the door was open now, standing in about eight inches, and
there was a man in it.

He was not looking at Tadayoshi. That is the fact and it should sit on
its own: **a man of thirty, in a house where somebody had come in, with
his wife making that noise behind a door eight feet away, was standing
with his back to the passage looking into a room.**

He had gone past the stair. He had gone past the stair while Tadayoshi
was in the bedroom, four feet from the top of it, and had not gone down
it and had not shouted, and had gone to the end of the passage and opened
the door of the small room, and was standing in it, looking at a bed with
nothing in it.

Tadayoshi did him from behind at the base of the neck, up under the
skull, and it was the fastest thing he did that night and it was not the
best.

and got a hand on his shirt, which nobody had managed since the fifth

It was not clean and it took a while. The man made noise the whole way

He got up off him at some point before it stopped.

Tadayoshi did not answer that, because answering it would have been a

The latch on the kitchen door, put back with the pressure on. The pot

the east side that was doing nothing in particular. There was a gate at
the top with a stone by it. There were three sheep in the top field, and
they moved away from him and then stopped and went on with what they were
doing, and he stood in the middle of eleven acres above a weir at four in
the morning with his sleeve stuck to his arm and looked at all of it.

*It is a field.*

*There is nothing on it. There is grass and a wall and a gate and some
water and it is worth fourteen thousand to have four people off it, and I
have been up and down the whole of it and I cannot see the thing.*

He stood there a while longer.

*Which is not a finding. That is the fourth time this month I have gone
looking for a reason and come back with the fact that there is one and I
cannot see it.*

He put it in the operational column later and it went in as three words
and no conclusion.

starting, and he had a bad boot and a worse sleeve and a route north that
he had walked twice and could have walked in his sleep, and he was
thinking about the ninety seconds.

kills you.*

It was a child.

knot on top the way a person ties a thing they have tied a hundred times,

about seven.

*Act 1, month 10, the twentieth. The top lane above the mill ground.
Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,500 words.*

The first thing that happened was the assessment, and it took about four
seconds and it came out clean, and that is worth saying because it is the
only part of the next eleven minutes that worked.

*Forty feet. He has seen me. He has stopped.*

*Light is coming but it is not come; at forty feet in this he has got a

*There is a wall on my right and a drop on my left and the lane is the

That was all of it and all of it was true and none of it required

queued behind it, and that was the first time in his life that had
happened.

maybe half a minute he went four or five steps to the side of the lane

Tadayoshi did not move.

*No.*

He said it in his head once and then did not do anything at all.

He did not do anything for a long time, and there was nothing in his chest
and nothing in his throat and nothing behind his eyes, and it was not
courage and it was not calm and there was no word for it in him.

He had a number. He had never had a number before. Everything he had ever
had was a bowl and then another bowl and no end to them, and now there was
an end to them and it was nine hundred and three away, and nine hundred
and three bowls was a thing you could count toward.

He thought that was better.

*Nine hundred and three.*

He put his hand out and put the three of them back in a row with the bases
lined up, in the dark, by feel, and left them there, and waited for the
bowl.

*Act 1, month 11, the tenth to the twenty-sixth. Ōtoma. Tadayoshi Ruri,

He spent four days building the case for going back and he built it
properly, which meant he built it the way Hōki had told him a case gets
built, which was four lines on a sheet with everything on them checkable.

It took him until the thirteenth and it came out at nine lines, because
there was more in it than four.

> *1. I have been paid.*
>
> *2. The client is correct and I have said so in writing.*
>
> *3. The claim is real. The old man said it himself at 3 a.m. on 20/10
> and I heard it and did not use it. It is in her name and it goes to the
> boy.*
>
> *4. It is a day's work. Ōgi is two days there and two back and the
> house has four children in it and the doing of it is under a minute.*
>
> *5. It costs the organisation nothing and it costs me four days.*
>
> *6. Nobody is going instead. I have asked and been told and I believe
> it.*
>
> *7. The boy has seen me at forty feet in bad light. That is an argument
> for going, not against.*
>
> *8. I have done four in a house in one night this month and did not sit
> down about any of them.*
>
> *9. There is no operational reason and there was no operational reason
> for any of the other four either, and I took the money.*

He read it through twice on the evening of the thirteenth.

*That is the best sheet I have ever written and every line on it is true
and it is the sheet I would write about somebody else.*

*And I am not going.*

---

He went looking for the decision on the fourteenth, because there had to
be one somewhere and he had never once failed to find one, and he spent
the whole of the fourteenth on it and did not find one.

He went at it from the front, which was: at what point did I decide.

*I did not decide it in the back room. I said no before I got to the end
of his sentence.*

*So it was decided before the ninth.*

*Was it decided on the road? — No. I did not think about him on the road.
I thought about ninety seconds and a boot.*

*Was it decided at the stone? — No. I stood there for eleven minutes
producing nothing, and producing nothing is what I did, and if there had
been a decision in it there would have been something in it and there was
not.*

He sat with that for most of the afternoon.

*So there is no decision anywhere. There is a no that came out of me in a
back room on the ninth and there is nothing underneath it, and I have been
down and looked.*

---

He knew the shape.

He had written it down on the eleventh of the tenth month, sitting on the
boards with a folded sheaf of harbour paper open on his knee, about a
different thing entirely.

*Things do not get decided. They get decided once, by accident, and then
they are what there is.*

He got the ledger and found it and read it and put it down.

*That was about a book I stopped writing in.*

*This is about four people in a house and one on a road, and it is the
same sentence.*

**And that was the thing he could not have.**

---

It is worth being exact about what he could not have, because everybody
who ever wrote about him afterwards got it wrong, and the ones who were
kind about it got it wrong in a way he would have found insulting.

He was not sorry.

He went and checked, on the fifteenth, in the way he checked everything,
and he checked it four separate times over about two hours: he put the
front room up in his head with the old man on the bed frame, and the
kitchen boards with the brother under him, and the woman's hands on his
forearm, and the man standing in a doorway looking at an empty bed, and he
waited to see what arrived.

Nothing arrived.

*I have gone and looked and there is nothing there and I am not going to
pretend there is.*

What he could not have was the hole.

*Because there is going to be a next time.*

*There is going to be a docket with a fee on it and something on the
paper that is not in the money, and when that happens I am going to do
whatever I did in the top lane, and I do not know what I did, and I do
not know why, and I cannot check a thing I cannot state, and I cannot
tell anybody what to expect from me, including me.*

*And a thing about myself that I cannot state is the most expensive kind
of information there is not to have.*

---

He got to the answer on the seventeenth and he did not like it and it was
the only one there was.

*If there is no decision under it, then I put one on top of it.*

*Which is backwards.*

*A rule you make before is a decision. A rule you make after is a story
about a thing that already happened, and a story is not evidence, and I
know that better than anybody in this building because taking stories
apart is the whole of what I do.*

He sat on the boards with the ledger shut on his knee for about an hour
over that.

*And a story I can check is still better than a hole I cannot.*

*Because a rule fails in public. If I write it down and then break it, I
will know, because it will be on a page with a date on it and the date
will be before.*

He opened the ledger at the back, past the page headed **PEOPLE WHO WILL
TELL SOMEBODY**, and turned two more leaves, and ruled a fresh one.

He did not put a heading on it. He tried for about four minutes to think
of one and everything he came up with was either a lie or a description
of a feeling, and he did not have either of those to spend, so he left the
top of the page white.

---

The first thing he wrote was wrong and he knew it in nine seconds.

> *Not the ones who are not in the count.*

He looked at it.

*The count is whoever is on the paper.*

*So that rule says: I will do what is on the paper and no more. Which is
not a rule about me. It is a rule about whoever writes the paper, and it
puts the whole of it in the hand of a man in a room I have never been in.*

*And the paper had five names on it and four prices, and the person who
decided the boy was not in the count was me.*

He ruled a line through it, hard, and did not scrub it out, because
scrubbing a thing out is how you lose the record of having been wrong.

---

The second one took him until the nineteenth.

> *Not the ones who did not choose.*

He sat with it a long time and turned it over and could not knock it down,
and then found the flaw in it himself, which took him under a minute,
because finding the one stroke that a whole thing hangs off was the only
thing he had ever been good at that nobody taught him.

*I do not know what did not choose means.*

*That is the stroke. Everything on this page is carried by four words and
I cannot say what they are for.*

He tested it, because testing it was the only honest thing left, and he
tested it backwards over everything he had, in order, because that was the
only data there was.

*The four in the fifth month. They came for me and they were sent and

*Sakuta. He passed names for money for eleven years. Chose. It holds.*

*The man at Sakabe. Chose.*

*Nunobiki. Chose.*

*The old man on the bed frame. He would not sell a field.*

He stopped.

*The son's wife.*

*The boy could not. He is seven and he was sent to Ōgi and he was sent

He read that last line four times.

*That is thin.*

> *Not the ones who did not choose.*
>
> *I do not know what did not choose means. That is the stroke the whole
