# Chapter 10 — What A Client Buys

*Book two. Act one, months 9 and 10.*
*10 scenes, 14,630 words. Tadayoshi ×6 · Uzuki ×3 · Kimimaro ×1.*



*Act 1, month 9, the twentieth to the twenty-second. Ōtoma. Tadayoshi
Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,520 words.*

---

Nobody told him.

He found out the way he found out everything in that organisation, which
was that a piece of paper arrived and was different, and he sat on the
boards of the loft above the cooperage on the morning of the twentieth
with it in his hands and read it four times before he moved.

It was the same form. Same rule down the left, same box at the top right,
same standard hand.

*There is no cargo line.*

He put his thumb where the cargo line went. On every docket he had been
given since the sixth month there had been a line about eleven-twelfths of
the way down that said what was in the crate or the roll or the bag, and
under it a weight, and under that a return instruction.

This one went from the place straight to the date.

---

The second thing he found was the fee, and he did the division before he
did anything else, because that was what he did.

*Three thousand six hundred.*

*The last one was four hundred.*

*That is nine.*

He sat with that for a while and then got the book out and went back
through, because a number without a series is an opinion, and he had four
months of numbers.

Parcels ran between three hundred and five hundred, and the five hundreds
were the ones with two days of walking in them, and the median was four
hundred exactly, and he had known that without looking it up.

*So it is nine times the median and it is not a parcel.*

---

He did the other sum then, the one about what three thousand six hundred
actually was, because a number is not a number until it is in something.

*A day of a man's work on a wall is about eighty. Ninety if he is good and
they are behind.*

*So it is about forty days of a grown man.*

*And I eat on about a hundred and fifty a day if I am paying for a bed,
which I do not always, so call it a hundred and twenty.*

*Thirty days.*

He wrote that down.

**Three thousand six hundred was a month of him, and about six weeks of a
man who lays walls, and it was for one night.**

He did not have anything to put beside that yet. He put it in the book
with a rule under it and a space, which is what he did with numbers he
expected to need again.

---

He went down and found Hōki in the yard at about eleven and did not open
with the fee, because he had learned something in the eighth month and had
written a page about it and had then done it again anyway on the
eighteenth of the eleventh — *no*, that had not happened yet; that was the
kind of mistake he was still two months away from making.

What he said was: "There's no cargo line."

"No," said Hōki.

"Is that a mistake?"

"No."

---

Hōki put down what he was doing, which he did not usually do, and sat on
the end of the trestle.

"Sit down."

Tadayoshi sat down.

"You've been carrying since the third week of the ninth month."

"The nineteenth of the seventh."

"Have you." Hōki did not correct himself and did not apologise. "Right.
Then you'll have worked out what it's for."

"It's for me being able to be given work, which is for finding out whether
I do what I'm told, which is for finding out whether I am worth
anything."

"That's most of it."

"What's the rest of it?"

---

"The rest of it," said Hōki, "is that there's about forty of us in this
part of the country and a bit under half of them are any good, and every
one of them is somebody with a face."

He held up a hand and started counting off, which he had never done
before.

"A man goes into a town twice, he's a man who's been in that town twice.
Goes in four times, he's a man somebody's wife has an opinion about. Six
times, and there's a boy at the gate who could tell you what he's
carrying." He put the hand down. "That's the whole business. That's the
only business there is. Everything else is arithmetic on top of it."

"I know."

"You know it. You don't know what it costs." Hōki looked at him properly
for the first time in the conversation. "Every week you carry a box, that
is a week four other people are on a road being looked at. Every week you
do something else, they are not."

---

Tadayoshi sat on the trestle in the yard behind a cooperage in Ōtoma and
did the arithmetic and it came out immediately and it came out clean.

*One of me being looked at very hard is cheaper than five of them being
looked at a bit.*

*Because the looking is the cost. Not the risk — the looking. A man who is
looked at a bit by four people in four towns is four descriptions in four
briefings and none of them is any good.*

*And one boy who is looked at very hard in one place is one description
that is excellent, and they have already got that, and they have had it
since the fourth month.*

**They are not spending anything new on me. That is the thing. It is
already spent.**

He turned it over once more to be sure.

*So the correct use of a person who is already described is to put him
where being described does not matter, and take four people out of the
open.*

*That is efficient.*

*That is what I would do.*

---

He was pleased. He wrote that down too, that evening, because he wrote
down states and had done since the fifth month, and he wrote it flat and
without any qualification at all.

*Pleased.*

He did not examine it. There was nothing to examine: he had been given an
accurate account of what he was for, by a man who had no reason to
flatter him, and the account was correct, and being correctly used is the
only kind of being wanted he had ever had any experience of.

*They are spending me.*

*And they have told me they are spending me, which nobody has ever done,
and the fact that they told me is the part I am pleased about.*

**He was ten years, five months and eleven days old.**

---

Hōki said one more thing before he went back to what he was doing, and
Tadayoshi wrote it in the book verbatim because he could not tell yet
whether it was operational.

"You'll want to know the fee's not the fee."

"What?"

"What you're getting isn't what it costs. It's not near what it costs.
Somebody paid for that and it wasn't three thousand six hundred."

"Then what was it?"

"Not your line," said Hōki. "Just don't ever say the number out loud in a
room. You'll say it like it's a lot and everybody'll hear a boy who
doesn't know what things cost."

---

He did not sleep well on the twenty-first and he could not work out why,
and he went through it four ways and got nothing.

*Not frightened. Checked that twice.*

*Not unwilling. There is nothing in me that is unwilling and I have looked
for it.*

*Not confused. I know what the job is, I know the place, I know the
window, and I have the ground.*

He lay on the boards and looked at the underside of the roof for about an
hour and gave up at some point after two.

The thing he did not have a name for, and would not have for about six
years, was that he had spent five months being a boy who was hunted, and
had built the entire architecture of himself on the shape of that, and on
the twenty-second of the ninth month he was going to become a different
object in the same country.

---

He read the docket for the last time on the morning of the twenty-second
and put it in the fire, which was standing procedure, and did not need to
look at it again because he had it.

> *Anazawa. Sakuta, the shutter-maker on the west lane. Any night to the
> 28th.*

He went through the towns page before he left, because it had become a
thing he did, and put a twelfth line at the bottom in pencil, and did not
write anything beside it yet.

*Anazawa.*

*I have never been there.*

*After Friday there will be nobody in it who could describe me, or there
will be a great many, and there is no third answer.*

He went out at first light with a knife he had not used for anything, and
four days of road, and three thousand six hundred ryō he had not been paid
yet, and the pencil line at the bottom of the page.

Kiku had given her two things and she had been careful with both of them
for nine days, in the way you are careful with a thing you cannot replace,
which is to say she had taken them out and looked at them about forty
times.

*The almshouse behind the shrine at Ōgi. They take people until the tenth

*And a fortnight at the Kanaya in the eleventh, when the road trade comes
through, and they take anybody then.*

the back of it and found the long building with the shutters, and a woman
sweeping.

"Is this where they take people?"

"It was," said the woman.

---

It was not cruelty and Uzuki knew that inside about four seconds, which
was the worst part of it.

The woman put the broom against the wall and wiped her hands down her
front and explained it properly, the way you explain a thing to somebody
who is going to have to go and do something else about it. They took
people until the tenth month. Not into the tenth month. Until. The

"When did they go up?"

"Yesterday."

"Yesterday."

"They go up on the last day. It's the same every year."

---

*It is the same every year.*

She got out of the yard before she said anything and she got about two
streets before she stopped, and she stood with her back against somebody's
wall and had it out with herself, because she had learned in the last
fortnight that if you do not have it out with yourself standing still, you
have it out with yourself walking, and walking costs.

*Nobody did anything to me.*

*There is a date and the date has gone. Kiku did not know the date. Kiku
has not been to Ōgi in nine years and she said so and I heard her say it
and I put it down anyway as a thing I had.*

*That is mine.*

She did the rest of it properly, because there was no point in doing half
of it.

*The Kanaya is the eleventh month. It is the first of the tenth.*

*That is five weeks.*

*And I have got four hundred and forty.*

---

She had, exactly, four hundred and forty ryō, and she knew that
without opening the bag because she had known it since the twenty-sixth
and it had not moved since the twenty-sixth, and the reason it had not
moved was that she had not eaten anything she had paid for.

Five weeks costs more than four hundred and forty ryō. She did not need to
work that out. She had done the numbers on the road up with a cart in the
third month, when she was nine and had a hundred thousand ryō in a fold of
cloth and a list, and she knew what a person eats in a week and what it comes
to.

*Four weeks and a half, at the cheapest thing there is, is about fifteen
hundred.*

*So I am short by more than I have got. Not a bit short. More than
three times short.*

She stood there and let that be true for a bit.

Then she did the thing she had started doing on the north road, which was
to ask what the actual question was, because *I am four times short* is
not a question and cannot be answered.

*The question is what am I doing between now and the eleventh month.*

*And the answer is not in Ōgi, because I have been here two days and I
have asked nine people and there is nothing here.*

---

Write two days of it whole and let the rest be numbers.

The third of the tenth month she did not eat.

That was not a decision either. She had a hundred and thirty ryō in the
morning and she looked at bread at a stall on the east side and it was two,
and she worked out that twenty ryō was about a fifth of a day of walking, and
stood in front of the stall long enough that the woman looked at her, and went
away, and did the thing she had been doing since the nineteenth, which was to
drink water until the shape of it went.

It goes for about an hour. She had learned that on the twenty-first. It
comes back and it comes back worse and the second time it comes back it
does not go again for anything.

By the middle of the afternoon she had stopped being able to think about
the Kanaya.

That was the finding and she made herself put it down properly.

**Hunger does not make you hungry. It makes you stupid, and it takes the
far things first.**

*I could not do the sum this afternoon. I did the sum this morning and
this afternoon I could not hold the end of it. And the eleventh month is
the far thing. So the eleventh month is going first.*

---

The fifth she ate.

A woman in a yard on the north side of Ōgi had a heap of wood that wanted
splitting and did not want to pay for it, and Uzuki did it for four hours
and got a bowl and a heel of something, and was so glad about the bowl
that she had to stand in the lane afterwards for a minute.

And then she did the other sum, which was the one that mattered.

*Four hours for one bowl.*

*A day is three bowls.*

*So a day of eating is twelve hours of that, and there is not twelve hours
of that in Ōgi, because I have asked, and there is not.*

**It was not that nobody would pay her. It was that there was not enough
of it in the town to pay her with.**

---

---

The sixth: nothing.

The seventh: half a day at a rope-walk, and they gave her forty ryō, and
she bought twenty ryō of bread and kept twenty, which she was proud of for
about a mile.

She did the count that night under the same bridge she had been under
since the second, on her back, with her bag under her head, and found that
she could not do it.

*Nine days.*

*I have got — I had four hundred and forty on the first and I have got
about three hundred and ninety and I do not know what happened to the
rest, because I have not written anything down since the twenty-eighth of
the ninth month.*

She lay there with that.

*I stopped writing it down because there was nothing to write it on and I
told myself I would keep it in my head.*

*And I have kept it in my head for nine days and it has gone wrong.*

---

**That was the finding and it was worse than the money.**

*The bag was not for the money. I have known the money the whole time —
what is in the fold, to the ryō, on any day you like to ask me.*

*The bag was for the days.*

*And the days are the only thing I have got that goes in one direction,
and I have lost about three of them somewhere between the shrine and the
rope-walk, and I am not going to get them back, because there is nothing
to check them against.*

She turned over on the stones.

*So I need something to write on.*

*Not soon. Now. It is the thing I need most and it costs about a
twentieth and I have walked past four places that would have sold it to
me because I have been buying bread instead.*

**She had not, at any point in nine days, been able to spend anything on
a thing that was not food, and had not noticed that this was what was
happening to her.**

*And that is what being hungry does. It is not that it hurts.*

*It is that it makes everything except the next four hours stop being
real.*

She got up before it was light on the eighth and went to look at the road
out.

He was in the lane behind the shutter-maker's for two hours and eleven
minutes before it started, and he had planned for three, and the two hours
were the job.

That is the thing nobody would have believed if he had told them and he
never told anybody. The two hours were the job. What came after was the
part that took no thinking at all.

He had the ground by the first evening — *packed earth over rubble, and it
does not hold a print in this weather, and the drain at the top of the
lane is the only soft strip and it is four feet wide* — and he had the
routine by the second.

Sakuta shut the front at about seven. Sakuta ate at about eight, upstairs,
where the light was. Sakuta came down at about half past nine and did an
hour in the back with the door to the lane open two inches for the smoke,
because he had a brazier and no chimney and he was not going to sit in it.

*Every night. Four nights out of four. Two inches.*

---

He waited from half past seven.

Eight minutes of the eleven were waiting and he wanted that written down
somewhere in his own hand afterwards, and it went into the book as a time
and not as a sentence.

He waited in the dark against the wall of the yard next door with his
hands inside his coat because his hands were cold and cold hands are slow,
and he did not move and did not shift his weight and did not look at the
door more than about once a minute, because looking at a thing is how you
get seen looking at it.

At twenty past nine a woman came down the lane with a covered pot and did
not see him, and he did not move for her either.

At twenty-eight minutes past, the door went to two inches.

He gave it eleven more minutes because the man had to settle, and because
the brazier had to be up, and because a man leaning over a brazier is a
man with his eyes ruined for the dark.

---

He came in through the two inches at nine forty and did not open it any
further than his own shoulders.

Sakuta was on a stool with his back three-quarters to the door and a
plane in his hand and the brazier at his knee, and there was a smell of
charcoal and shavings and something with vinegar in it that had been his
supper, and he was about forty and going thin on top and he had a good
back for a man who sat down all day.

open two inches does not make a noise when it becomes a door that is open
sixteen.

Tadayoshi crossed four feet of floor and put his left hand flat on the

His feet were going. Both of them, against the boards, not kicking —
walking. As though the floor were a road and he could get up it.

It did not bark.

She got the paper before she got the shoes and it cost her a twentieth and
it was the best money she spent that year.

It was not paper. It was a strip off the end of a rice sack that a man at
the south end had cut down for wrapping and had a stack of, and it was

*Nine days in Ōgi and I stopped on about the twenty-eighth.*

*I had sixteen hundred and twenty on the twenty-second and I know that
because I wrote it under a bridge.*

took the lowest one, and put a small mark beside it that meant *this is a
number I have made and not a number I have counted.*

**1,603.**

*Days until I am fifteen.*

She looked at it for a long time.

*Right. That is mine again.*

back.

*They are shoes.*

*They are bad shoes and one of them is not my size and they are shoes.*

worth what it cost.**

---

*It is the tenth month and I have got shoes.*

She got the six miles and then it got dark and the good mood went, in the
way it always went, which was all at once and without anything happening
to it.

*Right.*

*So it is four days to anywhere and I have got three hundred and ninety.*

She kept walking, because the shoes were on and the road was there and
there was nothing else in the whole world she could do with the evening.

took the lowest one, and wrote it, and put a small mark beside it that
meant *this is a number I have made and not a number I have counted.*

**1,584.**

*Days until I am fifteen.*

She looked at it in the dark for a bit, on a piece of sacking, in a room
with ten empty mats in it.

*Right.*

*That is the next thing, then.*

*Act 1, month 10, the tenth to the twelfth. Ōtoma. Tadayoshi Ruri, age
ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,600 words.*

---

He got back on the tenth and had two days with nothing in them, and used
them the way he used everything, which was to go through the numbers.

He had three now. Three is enough to have a shape.

*Anazawa: two hours eleven of setting and eleven minutes of work.*

*Sakabe: forty minutes of setting and twelve minutes.*

*Nunobiki: eleven days, of which four were the client's and two were mine
finding out the four were the client's, and nine minutes.*

He laid them out on the boards with a pencil and did what he always did,
which was to look for the part that was not doing any work.

---

*The setting is coming down.*

*Two hours eleven, then forty minutes, then — Nunobiki I cannot use,
because the four days corrupt it, but the actual setting on Nunobiki was
about thirty.*

*So it is a hundred and thirty-one, forty, thirty.*

*And that is not me getting better at setting.*

He sat with it.

*It is me needing less of it.*

*At Anazawa I did not know what I needed to know, so I got everything.
Four nights, the routine, the dog, the drain, the woman with the pot, the
brazier. Half of it I never used.*

*At Sakabe I knew what I was going to need before I got there, so I got
that and stopped.*

**And what I know now that I did not know on the twenty-second of the
ninth month is which things matter, and I did not learn that from anybody,
and I could not write it down if I tried.**

---

He tried to write it down. That took most of the eleventh.

He got about nine items and none of them was the thing, and he knew none
of them was the thing while he was writing them, and did it anyway because
the writing was how he found out what he had.

At the end of it he had a page of true statements and an absence in the
middle of them, and he put a line round the absence and wrote in the
middle of the line:

> *There is a thing here that I have and cannot state.*

He had never written that sentence before either.

*I do not like it.*

*I do not like it because a thing I cannot state is a thing I cannot check
and cannot teach and cannot correct if it is wrong, and I do not know
whether it is wrong, and the only test I have got for it is whether the
work comes out, and the work has come out three times, which is nothing.*

---

The other thing he did on the eleventh was to sit down and go at the
question properly, because it had been sitting there since Nunobiki and he
did not leave things sitting.

*What has changed in me in fourteen days?*

He went at it honestly and got four answers, and three of them were
nothing.

*I am not frightened. — I was not frightened before. That has not moved.*

*I do not enjoy it. — Checked four times. No.*

*I do not think about them. — True, and it was true after the riverbed as
well, so it is not new.*

Then the fourth one, which took him until about midnight.

---

*The part that got easier was not the killing.*

He put the pencil down.

*The killing was never hard. That is the thing I have been circling for a
fortnight and could not get at, and it is the wrong end of it entirely.
The killing was eleven minutes at Anazawa and twelve at Sakabe and nine at
Nunobiki, and it was not hard at any point, and it was not hard on the
riverbed either when I was ten years and one month old and had never done
it.*

*So there is no *got easier* about the killing. There was nothing there to
get easier.*

He looked at the wall for a bit.

*What got easier is the afternoon.*

---

He wrote it out because writing it out was the only way he had of holding
a thing at arm's length.

*On the twenty-sixth of the ninth month I did the job at nine forty and I
walked five hours and I sat on a milestone at three in the morning and I
did the corrections. And then I was awake until it got light, and I could
not have told anybody what I was doing with the hours, and I did not
sleep.*

*On the second of the tenth I did the job at four fifty-two in the
afternoon and I ate at seven.*

*And on the ninth I did the job and I was asleep by half past eleven and I
slept the whole night, and I woke up on the tenth and the first thing I
thought about was whether the north road would be wet.*

He read that back.

**The first thing I thought about was whether the north road would be
wet.**

---

He went at it for another hour and could not make it into a fault.

*Is it worse work? — No. It is better work. Sakabe was better than
Anazawa and Nunobiki was better than Sakabe.*

*Am I less careful? — No. The corrections column has got fewer things in
it because there are fewer things to put in it, and I have checked that
that is what happened and not the other thing.*

*Is anybody worse off? — The people I was sent to are dead and they would
have been dead if I had not slept.*

*So on every measure I have got, it is an improvement.*

He sat with that for a long time, on the boards, in the dark, at ten years
and five months old.

*And I do not think it is an improvement.*

*And I cannot say why, and I have got no instrument that says why, and
every single instrument I have got says the opposite.*

---

He went and got the sheets.

He had ruled them on the third of the seventh month, in a plantation
outside Hanase, in the wet, out of eleven leaves of the harbour office's
spoiled paper that Nunome had let him take because they were going on the
fire anyway, and they had five columns, and he had had to invent the
fifth one standing there with the pen in his hand.

*What it cost.*

There were two lines on them.

The first was the third of the seventh month, and three of the columns
were a dash, because the whole of it was a question asked politely
through a floor by a man he never saw, and the last column said *one
coat*.

The second was the left-hander.

He looked at that one for a while, because it was the best page he had
ever made and he knew it — the feet that were wrong for a swap, the two
goes at his hands, the forty yards, the three seconds spent looking at
the ground he had opened — and then he made himself look at the top of it
instead of the middle.

*17/9.*

He turned the sheet over. Nothing on the back. He turned it back and read
the date again because he did not believe it.

*Seventeenth of the ninth month.*

*And today is the eleventh of the tenth.*

*That is twenty-four days.*

And then, because he could not stop himself doing it:

*And the docket came on the twentieth.*

*Three days.*

---

He sat with the sheets open on his knee.

*I have done three contracts and stood in a room with Kadoya and been
paid eleven thousand two hundred ryō, and the last thing I put on these
sheets I put there before any of it started.*

*And I did not decide to stop.*

He went looking for the decision, because that was what he did with any
outcome, and there was no decision anywhere, and he recognised the shape
of that immediately because he had seen it before, in the eighth month,
about something else.

*Things do not get decided. They get decided once, by accident, and then
they are what there is.*

He put his hand flat on the open sheet.

*So either nothing has come for me in twenty-four days —*

He stopped there, because the second half of the sentence was standing in
front of him and he did not want to write it down, and he noticed that he
did not want to, and wrote it down.

> ***— or something has, and I have stopped being the kind of person who
> notices.***

---

He did not resolve it.

That is the important part and it should be said flatly: he did not
resolve it, and he did not put a third line on the sheets, and he did not
decide anything at all.

What he did was rule a line under the left-hander, and date it the
eleventh of the tenth month, and write one more line under that, which
was an instruction to himself and which he kept for the rest of his life
and never once obeyed:

> *Check these on the first of every month. If there is nothing to add,
> that is the entry.*

Then he folded them, and put them at the bottom of the pack under the
throwables, which is where they stayed, and did the six minutes on the
roll, and went to sleep at about one, and slept the whole night.

*Act 1, month 10, the twelfth. Ōtoma. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV:
Tadayoshi. ~1,380 words.*

---

The fourth one came up the ladder in Ōtsuka's hand instead of being left
on the boards, and that was the first thing wrong with it.

"Read it here."

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to take it back off you in a minute either way," said
Ōtsuka, "and I'd rather you'd read it than not."

Tadayoshi took it and read it, and then read it again, because the first
read had been about the shape and the second one had to be about the
words.

---

It had a cargo line.

That was the second thing wrong with it and he saw it before he saw
anything else, because he had spent three weeks with dockets that did not
have one and his eye went to the place where it was not.

> *Terasaka. The house on the mill ground, above the second weir.*
>
> *Cargo: the ground.*

He put his thumb on it.

*That is not a cargo.*

*A cargo is a thing you carry. Ground is a thing you cannot carry, so
somebody has written a word in a box where a different word goes, and
nobody who writes these does that by accident.*

"What does that mean?"

"You know what it means."

"I want you to say it."

"No you don't," said Ōtsuka.

---

He read the rest of it.

The fee was fourteen thousand.

He did the division standing up and it came out at nearly four times
Anazawa and three and a half times Nunobiki, and he had a series now and
he knew what a series looks like when a point comes off it, and this point
was not on the line.

*Fourteen.*

*It is not four men's worth of time and it is not four men's worth of
difficulty.*

*So by the only rule I have got — the fee is for who the man was — it is
four times somebody.*

He looked at the paper.

*Or it is not one man.*

---

He was quiet for long enough that Ōtsuka took it back off him.

"Right," said Ōtsuka. "Now sit down, because I'm going to tell you the
part they don't put on it."

Tadayoshi sat down on the boards.

"There's a man called Terasaka who has got about eleven acres above the
second weir, and there is a man in Ōgi who has been trying to buy it off
him for two years, and Terasaka won't sell, and the reason he won't sell
is that it's his and he doesn't want to."

"That's not a reason."

"It is to him." Ōtsuka looked at him. "And the man in Ōgi pays us four
thousand a quarter and has done since the spring."

---

Tadayoshi did the whole of it in about six seconds and it was clean and it
was correct and he did not like any part of the process.

*Four thousand a quarter is sixteen a year. That is more than the three
contracts I have done put together, from one man, for nothing, every year,
just for existing.*

*So he is not a client. He is a subscription.*

*And a subscription does not ask for things very often, and when he does
ask you say yes, because the four thousand a quarter is the four thousand
a quarter whether he asks or not, and the day you say no it stops.*

He looked up.

"The ground is the cargo because he wants the ground and Terasaka is what
is on it."

"Yes."

"And the fourteen thousand is fourteen thousand because it isn't one
man."

Ōtsuka did not answer that one at all, which was the answer.

---

He asked one more question and it was the only one he asked in the whole
conversation that was not about the job.

"Have you given this to anybody else?"

"Yes."

"Who?"

"Doesn't matter."

"What did they say?"

Ōtsuka stood up and put the docket inside his coat, and looked at the top
of the ladder for a bit before he went to it.

"They said no," he said. "Two of them. And they're still with us and
they're still on sheets and nobody's said a word to either of them, and
I'm telling you that so that you know it, because you're ten and there's
nobody else in this building who's going to tell you."

Then he went down the ladder.

---

Tadayoshi sat on the boards until it got dark.

He did the work on it, because he did the work on everything, and he did
it in the order he did everything in, and the order was the problem.

*One. Is it operational?*

*It is not. There is nothing in it about the state and nothing about the
war and nothing about anybody being hunted. It is a man in Ōgi who wants
eleven acres.*

*Two. Does that matter?*

He sat with that one for about an hour and could not make it matter.

*The four thousand a quarter buys boots and sheets and beds and the coat
somebody was wearing at Aku in the ninth month. It is not clean money and
there is no clean money and I have never seen any.*

*Three. Can I do it?*

*Yes. It is a house on a mill ground above a weir and I have got the whole
of it off eleven words and I have not been there.*

*Four —*

---

He did not have a fourth.

That was the thing. He sat on the boards in the dark with a series of
three contracts and a page called PEOPLE WHO WILL TELL SOMEBODY and a
sheaf of hunter sheets with two lines on it, and he went looking for the
fourth question, and there was not one, because he had never needed one
before.

Every job he had ever taken had answered itself on the first three. *Is it
operational, does it matter, can I do it.* Three doors and if you got
through all three you went.

And this one went through all three and came out the other side and there
was still something standing in the road.

*I do not have a name for what is standing in the road.*

*And I have got no column for it, and no instrument that measures it, and
if I take this it will be because I could not name the thing that was
telling me not to, and if I refuse it it will be for the same reason.*

**He had spent five months building a man who decided things by
arithmetic, and on the twelfth of the tenth month he arrived at the first
question his arithmetic could not answer, and he did not know that was
what had happened.**

---

Ōtsuka came back up at about eight with the docket still in his coat and
did not take it out.

"Well?"

"Who's in the house?"

"What?"

"Terasaka's house. On the mill ground. Who's in it."

There was a pause of about three seconds.

"Why?"

"Because I want to know."

"That isn't how you've asked for anything before."

"No," said Tadayoshi. "It isn't."

---

Ōtsuka stood at the top of the ladder for a while and then took the paper
out and held it, and did not hand it over.

"Him," he said. "His wife. And there's a boy."

"How old?"

"I don't know how old."

"Find out."

"That's not —"

"Find out," said Tadayoshi, "and then give me the docket, and I'll tell
you on Thursday."

Ōtsuka looked at him for a long moment, and then put the paper on the
boards, face down, and went, and did not say anything else.

---

He did not touch it.

He sat on the other side of the loft with his back against the wall and
looked at the back of a piece of paper for about two hours, and then got
the book out, and turned to the ordinary column, and did not write
anything in it.

Then he turned to the operational column and did not write anything in
that either.

At about eleven he wrote four words in the margin of a page about
throwables, in pencil, sideways, where nobody who found the book would
know what it was attached to.

> *There is a boy.*
