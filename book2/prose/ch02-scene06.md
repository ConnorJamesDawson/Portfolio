# Chapter 2, Scene 6 — The Case

*Act 1, month 3. Shioiri, the workshop. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV:
Tadayoshi. ~1,520 words.*

---

Aoyagi told him on the Monday, on the way past, in about four words.

"Note's gone up, Ruri."

That was all of it. He said it without stopping, in the corridor, with an
armful of practice staves, in the tone of a man discharging an
administrative obligation that had been sitting in the back of his head
for a fortnight — and it was, Tadayoshi thought afterwards, almost
certainly a kindness. He had been told. He had not had to find out.

He walked home with it.

*A note exists. It says a thing about me and I have never seen it and I
will never see it, and it has gone somewhere I do not know the name of,
and it will sit there.*

*Last year's note said "aptitude" and I was moved.*

*I have now been written down twice by an institution and both times it
was because somebody liked me.*

---

He got the ledger out that evening and did not do any drops at all.

It took him two hours and he wrote it in four passes, which was two more
than he had ever needed for anything, and when he had finished he read it
back and could not find the hole in it, and that was the part that kept
him awake.

---

**PASS ONE. WHAT IS TRUE.**

*Akagane has evened the feed from the outside. He has done it with a
scraper and no theory and he is correct. The frame works. I have used it
for nineteen days.*

*I have evened the feed from the inside since I was eight. That is not a
frame, it is a hand, and it is teachable, because I taught it to myself
out of a book about something else.*

*These are two halves of the same thing and they have never been in the
same room.*

**PASS TWO. WHAT IT WOULD BE WORTH.**

He had the numbers now, which was the difference between this and every
version of the argument he had run in his head for two years. He had them
because a man in Ōura had put a glass on his eye.

*Kenji, said in his own shop, in front of three witnesses: "if he does
the whole lot in that frame next month I'll take four dozen at three and
not blink at it."*

*Four dozen at three, against four dozen and one at two-and-a-low-three,
is fourteen and a half becoming — *

He did the sum three times, twice on the page and once in his head to
check the page, because he was not going to build a case on a slip.

*— about twenty-one.*

*And that is with the frame alone. With the frame and a hand that knows
what the frame is for, on my mother's bench, it is not four dozen at
three. It is whatever the top of Kanaya's scale is called, and I have
never heard anybody in this family say the name of it, because nobody in
this family has ever needed the word.*

*Call it twenty-five and be conservative.*

*Twenty-five, against fourteen and a half, every month, for as long as
there are two of us.*

**PASS THREE. WHAT IT BUYS.**

*Hisui's house at Ōura is rent. She has been to see it twice and has
never once been asked what the rent is, because nobody in this family has
ever intended to find out.*

*It buys the fifth course a roof or it buys leaving. Both of those are
answers to the same question and this family has spent nine arguments
refusing to choose between them because it cannot afford either.*

*It buys my father not walking a day and a half each way with a mule.*

*It buys Akagane a reason to have spent nine weeks of a winter on a piece
of copper.*

He sat and looked at that last line for a while and then did not strike
it out.

---

**PASS FOUR. THE OBJECTION.**

He wrote it in the narrow column, because the narrow column was where the
true things went, and because he had ruled it narrow himself for exactly
this.

*If it works, we produce more.*

*She said: people who are worth something get collected.*

He put the pen down.

That was the whole of it. That was the sentence his mother had said
across a table with her eyes on her hands, flatly, like a tide table, and
it had ended an argument that eight people had been having for nine
years, and it had ended it because nobody at that table had had anything
to say back.

*I have something to say back.*

*I have had something to say back since I was about nine and I have never
said it because I could not put it in an order.*

He picked the pen up.

---

*It is not the being worth something. It is the being FOUND OUT.*

*A thing that is found is a discovery. Somebody arrives and takes it and
there is nothing to argue with, because the person taking it has just
learned it exists and they learned it by looking, and everything they
know about us at that point is that we hid it.*

*A thing that is DECLARED is not a discovery. It is a fact somebody else
has been living with for years. It goes on a list and the list gets old
and a fact on an old list is worth less every year, because the person
who wrote it down has moved on and the person holding it now inherited it
and does not care.*

*We are not hiding a secret. We are hiding a SURPRISE. And a surprise
gets a response and a fact does not.*

*So the correct answer to being worth something is not to be worth less.
It is to be worth something OPENLY, on purpose, on a day of our own
choosing, in a way that is boring.*

He read that back twice.

*It is boring that a family in the second district makes better beads than
it used to. It is boring that a frame was improved. Nobody has ever gone
up a hill for a frame.*

*It is not boring that a family in the second district has been quietly
doing something for two years that it did not mention.*

---

He sat in the workshop with the lamp burning down and the rain doing what
it did.

*I cannot find the hole in it.*

That was the trouble. He had gone looking for the hole, properly, the way
he went looking for the part of a form that was doing no work, and there
was not one — and there was always one, in his experience; there had been
one in the five-seal sequence and one in four hundred years of copper and
one in the price of a drop.

*Either it is right, or I am ten and I cannot see it.*

*And I do not know how to tell those apart from inside my own head, and
there is nobody in this compound I can hand it to, because the only
person who could check it is the person I would be arguing with.*

He looked at the four passes.

*She is not stupid. She has run this bench for twenty-two years and she
knows more about the trade than I will for ten more, and she is going to
sit down and read this and she is going to have an answer.*

*And if she has an answer I want it. That is the actual reason I am doing
this. I want to know what the answer is.*

*I have wanted to know what the answer is since I was eight years old and
she closed her hand over a drop and said a name and nothing else.*

---

He did one more thing before he shut it.

He turned to the front of the ledger, where he had written the first
entry three weeks ago — *nineteen went. nobody says the names.* — and he
looked at it, and then came back to the end and wrote, in the narrow
column, in the last line of the case:

*She will say no.*

*Write it anyway. If she says no I will at least have made her say it to
a page and not to a boy in a doorway, and a page can be kept, and I will
be able to read it again when I am older and find out whether I was
right.*

Then he blotted it and shut it and turned it face down on the bench,
because the lamp was going and because he did not want to look at it any
more that evening.

It sat there face down all night with the rain on the roof above it.

In the morning he put it inside his shirt and carried it about with him
for eleven days before he found an afternoon when the kitchen was empty
and his mother was in it.
