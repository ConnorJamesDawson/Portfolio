# Chapter 8, Scene 4 — Ground You Can Break

*Act 1, month 8, the ninth to the twentieth. Ōtoma. Tadayoshi Ruri, age
ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,870 words.*

---

Hōki was about fifty and had a bad knee he did not favour and hands that
had been broken at some point and set well, and he was the first person
Tadayoshi had ever met whom he could not get a read on in under a minute.

*He is not kind and he is not unkind. That is not two things I have
weighed against each other. It is that neither of them is present.*

*And he has looked at me for about eleven seconds in total across two
days and he has never once looked at my hands, and everybody looks at my
hands.*

"Right," said Hōki, on the ninth. "Walk to the end of that lane and come
back."

Tadayoshi walked to the end of the lane and came back.

"What did you leave?"

---

He had nine.

He gave them in order, because that was how he gave things: a footprint at
the gate where the mud was standing; the gate itself, moved and not put
back; a dog he had made bark, which was not a mark but was a record;
the four seconds he had spent looking at the shut window on the left,
which anybody watching would have seen him spend; where he had crossed,
and that he had crossed at the narrow bit, which is what a person who does
not want to be in the open does; his own tracks doubled, out and back, on
the same side; the wet on the wall where he had put a hand on it; the
time, because it was eleven in the morning and there is no reason for a
boy to be in a lane at eleven in the morning; and the fact that he had
come back at all, which is a thing almost nobody does.

Hōki listened to all nine without moving.

"Three more," he said.

"Which three?"

"That's the lesson," said Hōki, and went indoors.

---

That was how the whole of it went and it took Tadayoshi about four days to
stop hating it.

**Hōki asked questions and did not answer them.**

He did not explain anything. He did not correct anything. He would say a
thing like *what did you leave* or *where would you have gone* or *why is
that man not looking at you*, and then he would wait, and then, whatever
you said, he would say *and* or *three more* or nothing at all, and go
and do something else.

*He is doing it on purpose and he is doing it because it works on me.*

*I cannot leave a question standing. I have never once been able to. He
found that out on the ninth in about a minute and he has been using it
ever since and he has not made one single remark about it.*

*It is the most efficient thing anybody has ever done to me.*

He got the three, on the fourth day, in the middle of eating.

*The lane is a dead end and I did not know it was a dead end when I went
into it, which means I went into a lane without knowing what was at the
end, and anybody watching knows I did not know.*

*I looked at the shut window on the left and I did not look at the shut
window on the right, which tells them which side I think a thing is on.*

*And I came back at the same speed I went, which is the speed of a boy
with nowhere to be, and I had just done a thing and been pleased with it,
and a boy who has just done a thing walks differently, and I made myself
walk the same, and making yourself walk the same is a thing that shows.*

He said all three of them at the table with a bowl in his hand.

Hōki said, "Mm," and went on eating.

---

By the twentieth he had a list of things he had been taught and the list
was not one single technique long.

*How to break contact, which is nine tenths not being in contact.*

*How to read a docket for what it does not say, which is the thing I have
been doing to registers for two years and had never once thought to do to
a sheet about myself.*

*How to leave a town, which is not the same as getting out of one.*

*How to talk to a broker, which I cannot do and which he says I will
never do and which he says I should therefore never do, and that is the
first time anybody has told me not to fix something.*

*And how to price a job, which he has spent four days on and which I am
still bad at, and which he says most people are bad at their whole
lives.*

*None of that is a jutsu. He has not shown me one thing I could do with
my hands in eleven days.*

Then on the twentieth he showed him one.

---

They went out to the top of a bank where the road had been cut and the
face of it was clay over a shattered rotten shale, and Hōki got down on
his knees and put both hands flat on the ground.

Six seals. He did them slowly, on purpose, four times, before he did
anything at all.

Then the seventh time he finished them and about two seconds went by with
his hands still down and nothing happening.

Then a square yard of the bank in front of him came apart.

It did not explode and it did not sink. It went, all at once, from being
ground to being about four inches of grit lying on ground, and the noise
it made was a long low crack that Tadayoshi felt in the arches of his
feet.

Hōki sat back on his heels.

"Fill your tin."

---

Tadayoshi filled his tin.

Then he filled it three more times and tipped it out again, because he
wanted to know what a square yard came to and the answer was about four
tins, and then he got down on his knees and put his hands on the ground.

He had it on the fifth attempt.

It was not clean and it took him nearly three seconds and he got about a
third of what Hōki had got, and the grain came out uneven because he had
put too much into the near edge, and none of that mattered at all,
because the thing had *happened*, and once a thing has happened the rest
of it is only practice.

He sat back on his heels in the road with about a tin and a half of grit
in front of him that had been a bank four seconds ago, and something in
his chest was going that had not gone since the eighth of the fourth month
on the shoulder above the inlet.

*I do not have to buy it.*

*I have never once in my life had a piece of material that somebody did
not sell to somebody. Four hundred years of my family and every single
grain of it came off a merchant, by the sack, at a fortieth a tin, and my
father walked it up a hill on his back.*

*And it is in the ground.*

*It has been in the ground the entire time.*

---

"So now you need ground you can break," said Hōki.

Tadayoshi looked up.

"What?"

"You heard."

*I did hear.*

*I heard it and it went past me, because I was — *

He sat in the road on his heels with the grit in front of him and made
himself go at it, and it came apart in his hands in about six seconds and
it was the worst thing anybody had said to him since a man on a bank above
Nezu.

*I need ground I can break.*

*Which means I need to know what is under the ground before I stand on
it.*

*Which means from now on I am going to pick where I stand on what is
underneath, and I am going to do it whether I decide to or not, because
the tin runs out at about six minutes and this does not.*

*And I have been picking ground on cover and footing since the fourth
month and I did not decide to do that either. I do it. It is not a
decision, it is a preference, and a preference is a thing you cannot see
in yourself.*

*And a preference is a thing that can be written down by somebody who
watches you four times.*

He looked at the bank.

*Shale. Clay over rotten shale. He picked this bank.*

*Not the wall by the barn, which is stone and would have taken him a
minute. Not the top field, which is soil eleven inches deep over nothing.
He walked us forty minutes to a cut bank on a road because it is the best
ground in Ōtoma for it, and he did that this morning, before I had it, and
he did it in about nine seconds by looking at the map in his head.*

**In about a year somebody is going to write down where I stand.**

---

He said it out loud, because he could not not.

"They'll put it in the briefing."

"Yes," said Hōki.

"Not what I do. Where I'll be standing when I do it."

"Yes."

"So the better I get at this the smaller the number of places I can fight
in."

"Yes," said Hōki. "Get up, you've got about eleven of those in you today
and you've done four."

---

Tadayoshi got up.

He was, for the whole of the walk back and most of that evening, in a
state he had no word for at all and did not get a word for until he was
about nineteen, and the nearest he ever came to describing it was that a
man had walked round the outside of the thing he was best at and found a
hole in it in one sentence, without looking pleased with himself, and had
then told him to get up.

*Nobody has ever done that.*

*Meno told me what things were. Aoyagi told me where to put my feet. My
father told me what he thought about a subject.*

*Nobody in the whole of my life has taken a thing I had already worked
out and found the flaw in it, and I have been going round for two years
with my own arithmetic and no way at all of telling whether any of it was
any good.*

*He has just told me it was not, in five words, about a thing I got right
five minutes ago.*

*And he did not enjoy it.*

He was still turning it over at the table when Hōki said, without looking
up from a bootlace:

"The last one I did this with used to sit there like that as well."

Tadayoshi waited for the rest of the sentence.

There was not any more of it.
