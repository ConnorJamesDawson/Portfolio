# Chapter 7, Scene 3 — Whose Are You

*Act 1, month 6, the twelfth. The Kiribata, Fukae. Tadayoshi Ruri, age
ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~2,000 words.*

---

The Kiribata had a front room with six tables in it and four of them had
somebody at one, and he came in at about ten in the morning and sat down
at the empty one nearest the door with his back to the wall.

*Two exits. Front, and the passage at the back that goes past the stair,
and I do not know where the passage goes, so it is one exit and a guess.*

*Four people. One at the window with a bowl. Two together on the far
side who are carters, because of the hands and the smell. One big one on
his own three tables down with a coat on that is better than this room.*

*Nobody has looked at me except the big one, and he looked for about a
second and a half, which is what everybody does to a child in a doorway.*

He put his hands on the table where they could be seen and waited, and
the parcel sat against his back under his shirt, and he had come at ten
because *before noon* meant any time before noon and ten was the hour
when a room like this is at its emptiest and its least interested.

*So. Wait until he comes in, get across the room, ask for him, and go.*

That was the plan and he had had nine days on it and it was a good plan
and it lasted about four minutes.

---

The innkeeper's wife came out from behind the counter with a cloth.

"You want something, love?"

"No."

She stopped, half turned, cloth in her hand.

*That was wrong.*

He knew it was wrong the instant it was out of him, and he knew exactly
why: *no* is what you say to a woman offering you something, and a boy in
a front room is not being offered something, he is being asked what he is
doing there, and *no* answers the surface of the question and refuses the
bottom of it.

"Then what are you doing in my front room?"

"Waiting."

"Waiting for who?"

---

And that was it.

He sat with his hands on the table and looked at a woman with a cloth in
her hand and there was one word that would end this and it was the one
word he had been told not to say.

*Ask for Ōtsuka.*

*In a room. Out loud. In front of two carters and a man at the window and
a woman who will remember it.*

*No.*

*And there is nothing else. There is not one other sentence.*

He went through them anyway, because he could not stop going through
them, and it took him under a second and a half and every one of them
came back the same:

*My mother — no.*

*A man — which man.*

*Somebody's sent me — sent you for what.*

*I'm looking for work — then why are you sitting down, and what have you
got under your shirt.*

**Every true thing I have got opens another question and I have not got
an answer to any of the second ones.**

*So make one up.*

He tried.

He genuinely tried, and it was the first time in his life he had made the
attempt, and what happened was that he got as far as an opening sound —
a breath, and the front of a word — and then there was nothing behind it
at all. Not a refusal. Not a scruple.

*Nothing came.*

*There is nothing there. There is not a mechanism. I have got a hand for
seals and a memory for hands and a book with forty-five entries in it and
I have not got the thing that everybody else in this room has, which is
the ability to open their mouth and put out a sentence that is not true.*

He sat with his hands on the table and said nothing.

---

The silence went on for about three seconds and it was the loudest thing
that had happened in that room all morning.

The two carters had stopped. The man at the window had turned round on
his bench.

"Whose are you?" said the innkeeper's wife, and it was louder now, and
it was not unkind, and that was the worst part of it — **she was not
being nasty, she was doing the thing that a person does about a child in
a building at ten in the morning who will not say whose he is.**

*And she is right to.*

*She is going to ask twice more and then she is going to send the girl
for somebody, and the somebody will be a man with a rank in this town,
and he will not hurt me, and he will take me to a room, and in the room
there will be a book, and somebody will write down a boy of about ten,
and it will go up at month end with everything else that gets written
down in this country.*

*Eight seconds. Maybe ten.*

*And I can see the whole of it and there is not one single thing I can
do about any of it, because everything I can do is a thing you do to
ground and this is a room.*

He had, in that moment, one clear thought, and he was ashamed of it for
about four years.

*I want a wall behind me and forty yards of open in front and I want
somebody to try to hit me, because I would know exactly what to do.*

---

"—and I said to his mother, I said, *he doesn't want the mill*, and did
she listen?"

The big man three tables down was already talking.

That was the first thing about it and it was the thing Tadayoshi came
back to afterwards for about a year.

**He did not start. He was already in the middle of it.**

Halfway through a sentence, at volume, to nobody — and the room turned
round to find a conversation that had apparently been going on for some
time and that they had all somehow missed the beginning of.

"Did she listen. Did she." He got up. He was a big man and he got up the
way big men get up, in stages, using the table. "*Twelve years* that
family has had that mill and there is not one of them can tell you what a
tail race is for. Not one."

"Ōtsuka," said the innkeeper's wife, in a completely different voice.

"And there he *is*," said the big man. "Sitting there. Look at him."

---

He came down the room and he did not come at Tadayoshi, exactly; he came
past the innkeeper's wife and turned as he came, so that when he arrived
he was standing between her and the table, with his back three-quarters
to her, still complaining.

"Sit *up*."

A hand came down on Tadayoshi's shoulder and took a good deal of his
coat with it and put him about a foot to the left and into the other
chair, the one at the far side of the table, and the movement turned him
towards the wall and away from the room.

"Nine days," said Ōtsuka, to the ceiling, to the carters, to everybody.
"His mother says a week. I said to her, I said, a week, from Sakabe, on
those legs? And here he is on the twelfth like I said and she'll tell
everybody it was a week." He sat down heavily on the bench beside him.
"What was the weather?"

*What?*

*That is — *

*That is it. That is the thing. He has put it in the middle of the mill.*

"Hard going on the Monday," said Tadayoshi.

"Course it was," said Ōtsuka, disgusted, to the room. "Course it was. And
she'll have him out again on Thursday. *Thursday.* Woman's never walked
past the shrine in her life."

One of the carters laughed.

"You want feeding," said the innkeeper's wife.

"He wants feeding, I want feeding, we all want feeding, and what I want
is somebody in that family to tell me what a tail race is *for*."

The room came back up.

---

It took Tadayoshi about forty seconds to work out what had happened.

He sat with his back to the room and his hands flat on the table and the
big man beside him going on about the mill to anybody who would take it,
and he was still, somewhere in the back of himself, waiting for the girl
to be sent for somebody.

Then he moved his shoulder.

*The parcel's gone.*

He did not look down and did not put his hand to it; he knew before he
had finished the thought that both of those were things you do not do,
and he had learned that from nobody, and it was one of about four things
he could do that nobody had taught him.

*It's gone.*

*It went when he moved me into the chair. His hand came down on my
shoulder and my coat and it went across my back on the way past and it
went into — *

He let his eyes go along the bench without moving his head. There was a
coat on it, folded, that had not been on it four minutes ago.

*There.*

---

And then the rest of it arrived, all at once, and it was the most
frightened he had been in the whole of the fifth and sixth months,
including the riverbed.

*He turned a room.*

*There were seven people in this room and every single one of them was
looking at me and there was a woman four feet away from me who was about
nine seconds from sending for somebody, and he stood up and started
talking in the middle of a sentence about a mill and now that has not
happened.*

*It has not happened. It is not that it stopped. It is that they have all
got a different morning now.*

He went back over it. He went back over it three times, and each time he
could name every physical thing that had occurred and could not, at any
point, find the place where the thing had been done.

*He never said a word to me until he was already sitting down.*

*He made himself the boring one. He made himself a man everybody in this
room has heard about a mill from before, so that nobody in this room is
going to think about him for one second after he goes out of that door.*

*And he did it as loudly as it is possible to do anything.*

*I have spent five months learning how not to be looked at. He did the
opposite of the whole of it, in about eleven seconds, and it worked
better.*

He sat with his hands on the table.

*I could not do that if I had a year.*

*I could not do it if somebody wrote it down for me and I learned it off
by heart, because it is not a thing you can write down. There is no
sequence. He looked at a room and he knew what it wanted and he gave it
that, and I have watched it happen from four feet away and I could not
tell you what he did.*

**And I did not see it happening. That is the part. I was in it and I did
not see it happening.**

---

Ōtsuka finished with the mill.

He did it the way he had started it — not finishing it, exactly, just
letting it run down into the room and be over — and then he put both
hands on his knees and looked at Tadayoshi for the first time, properly,
with an entirely different face on.

"Right," he said, quietly. "Through the back. Mind the step, it's not
where you think it is."
