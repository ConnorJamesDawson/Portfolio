# Chapter 6, Scene 10 — Where I'd Put A Name

*Act 1, month 6, the twenty-seventh. The kitchen at Nakadō. Nozaki
Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~2,100 words.*

---

The box came out on the twenty-seventh because the twenty-seventh was
when the box came out, and Uzuki sat down on the other side of the table
without being asked to and put her hands flat on it.

Her mother did not tell her to go away.

That was new. It had been new for about three weeks and Uzuki had
noticed the exact evening it started, which was the evening after the
first quarter of a ryō came into the house, and had not said anything
about it, because you do not say anything about a door that has opened.

She read the box upside down, which she was much better at than she had
been in the fourth month.

*In: Iwasa, one and a half. Kuriya — no. Not Kuriya. Just a half, and no
name on it, and it's mine, and she's written it with nothing next to it.*

*And the stones. Nine days at a fortieth. She's put them all together as
one line: 9/40.*

*She's counted them.*

She sat with that for a second because it was, without any question, the
best thing that had happened to her since the fourth month.

*She's counted them. She didn't have to. Nobody would ever have known.*

*Out: rent three. Meal. Oil. Tazu, once, and it's a small one.*

*And H.*

---

"What's H?"

Her mother went on writing for about four seconds.

Then she put the pen down in the tray and did not pick it up again, and
Uzuki understood that she had asked the only question that was actually
in the room and that her mother had been waiting for it since the
fourteenth of the fifth month.

"It isn't a letter," said Sae.

"It's a letter. It's written there."

"It's where I'd put a name," said her mother, "if I'd decided."

---

*Oh.*

She sat very still.

*Ten weeks.*

*Ten weeks of writing a mark in a book instead of a name, twice a month,
in her own house, where nobody but her ever looks at it.*

*She wasn't hiding it from me. She was hiding it from the page.*

"Have you decided?"

"Yes."

"When."

"About the eleventh."

*The eleventh.*

*Sixteen days ago.*

*And she's gone on writing H for sixteen days.*

---

"Say it, then," said Uzuki, and her voice did something on the way out
that she had not authorised.

"Shimoda."

---

She had known. She had known since about the second sentence and she had
known in the way you know a thing you have arranged not to look at, and
it arrived anyway and it went through her exactly as hard as if she had
not known at all.

*The mule.*

*He came for the mule.*

*He came into our yard on the fourteenth and he told us what a mule is
worth, item by item, in front of me, and he was fair about it, and he
told me what a girl of ten is worth in a field, and he was fair about
that as well, and he'd already given her a hundred.*

*A hundred came in before he ever looked at the mule.*

*He did the mule properly anyway. He didn't have to do the mule properly.
He could have given her eight for it and she'd have taken it, and he did
six, and six was right.*

*And that's worse.*

*I don't know why that's worse but it's worse.*

---

"Ask," said her mother.

"Why."

"Because there isn't anything else." Sae put both hands flat on the
table, the way Uzuki had, which neither of them noticed. "I've had it out
every night for ten weeks. There's the rent and there's what's in and
there's what goes out, and I've done it about two hundred times, and
there is no arrangement of those three that gets us to next spring.
There's no cousin. There's no work in this village for a woman of forty
who's never done anything but this house. And Ōgi's four days and a
kitchen in Ōgi means you go and I don't, or I go and you go into
somebody's house here as a girl, and you know what that is, and I'm not
having it."

"I'm earning."

"You are."

"I've got a half a month off Kuriya and I've got the field in the autumn
and I'll have the thinning—"

"Uzuki."

"—and if I get another one like Kuriya it's a whole one, and that's a
third of the rent, and if I get *two*—"

She stopped, because she had run out of the parts she had prepared.

Her mother reached over and turned the flour bag round.

She had it. She had had it for some time. Uzuki did not ask how long and
never found out.

Sae looked at the second column for a while — the nine fortieths, the
quarter, the quarter — and she did not laugh at it and she did not make a
face and she did not say anything about a child doing sums.

"That's very good," she said.

"It—"

"It's four thousand ryō a year."

---

*Four ryō a year.*

*The rent's thirty-six.*

She sat in the chair and let it be true, because there was nothing else
to do with it, and because she had decided some time in the last five
weeks that she was going to be somebody who looked at things, and this
was the thing.

*She's not wrong.*

*She's not being cruel and she's not managing me and she hasn't taken a
single thing off my number to make her point. She added it up right and
she said what it comes to. It comes to four.*

*I did everything there was and it comes to four.*

"All right," she said.

---

"I'm not doing it for you," said her mother.

Uzuki looked up.

"I want that said and I want it said now and not in four years." Sae's
voice had gone very flat, which was what it did instead of going
anywhere else. "If I tell you it's for you, then you owe me. And you'd
carry it. You'd carry it about with you and you'd be good about it, and
somewhere around fourteen or fifteen you'd work out exactly what I'd
charged you for it and what I'd bought with it, and then you'd hate me
for the charging and not for the thing."

*She's done it again.*

*It's the same as the road. It's exactly the same as the road — you'll be
cross with the true thing and not with a nice thing I said in a kitchen.*

*She's decided that the one thing she can still give me is a clean
account.*

*And it is. It's the only thing she's got left and she's giving me all of
it.*

*And I'd rather have the lie.*

She did not say that. She was ten and she was not going to be able to say
that for about eleven years.

"So it's for you," she said.

"It's because there isn't anything else," said her mother, "and I'd have
done it if you'd never been born, and I'd have done it if you were
twenty, and I'm doing it because I'm forty and I can't dig."

---

"What's he like?"

Her mother did not answer that quickly, either.

"I don't know," she said.

"You've known him twenty years."

"I've known him twenty years and I've said good morning to him about four
hundred times and I bought a mule off him and I've been in his yard and
I've been at three funerals with him." Sae looked at the box. "And I
could not tell you one single thing about what he's like in his own
house. Nobody could. Nobody knows what anybody's like in their own house.
That's what a house is *for*."

Uzuki sat with her hands flat on the table.

*Say something back.*

*Say — what. Say don't. Say I've got a feeling. I haven't got a feeling.
I haven't got anything except that he counted a mule's teeth in front of
me and told me the truth about a fortieth and gave her a hundred before
the ground was flat.*

*That's not a thing. You can't put that in front of somebody.*

"Right," she said.

---

"There's one thing," she said, a bit later, when the pen had gone back in
her mother's hand and the room had started being a kitchen again.

"Go on."

"Don't tell him about the hands."

Her mother's pen stopped.

She did not ask which hands and she did not ask what about them, and
Uzuki understood — later, much later, and it was one of about four things
that kept her from hating her mother outright for the rest of her life —
that Sae had known for weeks and had not asked her one question about it.

"I wasn't going to," said Sae.

"Ever."

"Ever."

*Right.*

*That's the first thing we've decided together since he died and it's a
thing we're keeping off him.*

*I don't like that.*

*I'm doing it anyway.*

---

She watched her mother do it.

Sae drew one line through the H, a single stroke, not scratched out —
crossed, the way you cross a thing you want to still be able to read
afterwards. Then she wrote the name beside it in her small tidy hand.

Then she went down the page to the hundred, and took the rule, and put it
along under the line she had drawn under it ten weeks ago, and did not
cross that out either; she wrote *in* against it in the margin, and drew
a small arrow into the left column, and added it up.

It took about eleven seconds.

*That's it, then.*

*That's the whole thing. Nobody shook hands and nobody said anything in a
shrine and there's no paper anywhere. A woman put a name in a book and
moved a number from one column to the other, and it's done, and it's been
done since the eleventh, and the writing it down is the last bit.*

*She's the one who did it. Nobody did it to her.*

*I'd like to be angry about that and I've watched her do the sums for
five weeks and I can't.*

---

Afterwards, when the box had gone back on the shelf, Uzuki got up and
went round the table and took her mother's hands off her lap and held
them.

Sae looked at her.

Uzuki did not say anything. She put her thumbs in the middle of the
palms and did what she did, small, and stopped, and waited, and did it
again, and went on doing it — a great deal less than ninety, because
there was not ninety in her that late and because it was not that kind of
hurt — and neither of them said one word about it.

Her mother's hands were about forty years old and had been in cold water
that morning and every morning, and there was a thing in the base of both
thumbs that Uzuki could feel and did not have a name for and was not
going to be able to fix at ten, or at fifteen, or possibly ever.

She did it for about four minutes.

Then she let go and went to bed, and her mother sat in the kitchen for a
while with her hands in her lap, turning them over.

---

She got the flour bag out in the dark.

The first column was the ninety, and the last thing in it was
thirty-four.

The second column was the money, and the whole of it came to about one
since the fourteenth of the fifth month, which she knew because she had
added it up four times that week for no reason at all.

She turned it sideways and started a third one.

*I'm ten and four months.*

*Fifteen is four years and eight months.*

She did it properly, on her fingers and then on the bag, months into days
the way her father had shown her with the mill chits, and she checked it
twice because it was the only sum in her life that was ever going to
matter and she was not going to have it wrong.

*One thousand seven hundred and four.*

She wrote it at the top of the third column and put the date beside it.

Then she took one off.

*One thousand seven hundred and three.*

She put the bag under the stone by the step and went in.
