# Chapter 3, Scene 2 — The Wood

*Act 1, month 3, the twenty-seventh — day two. The north road above Ōgi.
Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,750 words.*

---

The wood started about a mile past the third milestone and it was two
miles through, and she got to the edge of it at half past nine on the
second morning having done eleven miles the day before and slept in a
byre with permission.

She stopped at the edge and looked at it.

*If the road at the wood is wet you wait for a cart and go with it.*

The road at the wood was not wet.

That was the first thing, and she stood there for a good four minutes
being pleased about it, because the rain had come through in the night
and she had gone to sleep certain she was going to lose half a day, and
she had woken up to a hard sky and a road with the water already off it.

*It's dry. That's the condition and the condition isn't met.*

*So I can go.*

She went about forty yards in and then came back out again.

---

There were three men sitting at the side of the road just inside the
trees.

That was all. That was the entire thing, and she went over it about nine
hundred times afterwards and it never got any bigger than that: three
men, sitting on the bank on the left, about forty yards in, where the
trees started.

They were not doing anything.

They had no cart and no barrow and no packs that she could see and no
tools. They were not eating. One of them was lying back on the bank with
his hat over his face and the other two were sitting up, and one of those
two had looked at her, once, without any interest at all, in the way you
look at a bird landing.

And then he had gone on looking at her.

---

She came back out to the edge and stood by the milestone with her hand on
the cart handle and her heart going, and she was furious with herself
about the heart, because *nothing had happened*.

*They're waiting for somebody. People wait for people. Da waits at
Fusano for a man about a barrel and he waits three hours sometimes and
sits on a wall.*

*It's a wood on a carting road and there's three men in it and I have
been on a road for a day and a half and everybody I have passed has been
somebody.*

She looked back in.

The one who had looked at her was still looking.

---

*The condition isn't met.*

She stood at the milestone and had the argument with herself properly,
out loud in her head, in order, the way she had learned to have arguments
in the eleven days before Kayano.

*One. Ma said if it's wet. It isn't wet. So the rule doesn't cover it and
I'm free.*

*Two. Ma said if it's wet because a wet road is a slow road and a slow
road is two hours in there instead of forty minutes. So the rule isn't
about the water. It's about how long I'd be in the trees.*

*Three. So the rule does cover it. She just didn't have the words for
the actual thing, or she had them and didn't want to say them to me at a
table.*

*Four —*

She stopped at four.

*Four. If I go in there and I'm wrong, I don't get to find out I was
wrong. I get to be a girl in a wood.*

*And if I sit here for three hours and a cart comes and I go through with
it and nothing happens, I still never find out. There's no version of
this where I get told.*

---

She sat down on the milestone.

It was, in the end, the most annoying decision she had ever made, and she
made it in about a minute and a half, and the annoying part was not the
waiting.

The annoying part was that she was never going to know.

---

She waited two hours and forty minutes.

She did not sit still for them, because sitting still with three men
forty yards away was not a thing her body was going to agree to. She got
the bokken out from under the sacking and went thirty yards back down the
road the way she had come, to where the bank was clear and she could see
both ways, and she did the third form.

She did it eleven times.

She was rubbish at it. She was rubbish at it in a specific and
identifiable way, which was that the eighth count kept going hard again,
because her hands wanted to be doing something and hands that want to be
doing something spend a bucket.

*Late. Not at the set. At the going.*

Twelfth time. Thirteenth.

She got two of them clean out of the fifteen and neither of them was one
she could have repeated, and she was aware, in a distant and unwelcome
way, that she was practising in order to have somewhere to put her hands.

At about half past eleven she heard a cart.

---

It was a woman with a load of roofing slate and a boy of about fifteen
walking at the horse's head, and the woman had a face like a plank and
did not slow down.

Uzuki got up off the milestone.

"Excuse me — "

"No room."

"I don't want a ride, I've got a cart, I want to walk beside you through
the wood."

The woman looked down at her. Then at the little cart. Then up the road
at the trees.

"How many?"

"Three."

"Sitting or walking?"

"Sitting. On the left, about forty yards in, one's got a hat over his
face."

The woman said something under her breath that Uzuki did not entirely
catch and did not need to.

"Get your cart in behind mine," she said, "and stay on this side of the
horse, and don't talk to me while we're in there, and if I say get up on
the load you get up on the load and you don't ask me why. Tarō."

"Ma."

"Walk on her outside."

---

It took forty minutes and nothing happened.

That was all. They went in with the slate cart making the noise a slate
cart makes, which was an enormous amount of noise, and the boy Tarō
walked on Uzuki's outside with a stick he had picked up without appearing
to pick it up, and the woman walked at the horse's head and looked
straight ahead.

At forty yards the three men were still on the bank.

One of them said, "Morning."

"Morning," said the woman, and did not stop.

And that was it. The bank went past and the trees went on and after a
while they thinned and there was a field and the road went down towards
the water meadows, and Uzuki was through.

---

"Right," said the woman, at the far edge, and stopped, and let out a
breath that she had evidently been holding for two miles. "Tarō, drink."

Uzuki stood in the road with her hands still on the cart handle.

"Nothing happened," she said.

"No."

"They just said morning."

"They did." The woman took the waterskin off her son and drank and gave
it back. "That's what they do. What did you want, a knife?"

"I don't — " Uzuki stopped. "I don't know what I wanted."

"You wanted to find out," said the woman. "So you'd know if you were
being a fool."

"Yes."

"Aye. Well." She wiped her mouth. "You don't get that. That's the whole
trade of being a woman on a road, and I'll tell it you for nothing since
you're ten and you're on one: *you never find out.* You do the careful
thing and nothing happens and you spend the rest of the day feeling
daft, and you do the careless thing and nothing happens and you spend the
rest of your life thinking you're clever, and there's no way of telling
those two apart from inside.

"So you do the careful thing. And you keep doing it. And you never get to
know, and that's the price, and it's not a big one."

---

They went on together as far as the fork at Sanzu because it suited them
both, and the woman turned out to be called Ine and to be no longer
frightening at all once she was out of the trees, and she asked what
Uzuki was doing on that road and got the whole of it.

She did not say it was a good plan. She did not say it was a bad one.

What she said, about two miles later, apropos of nothing, was:

"When you get to Kayano. Don't ask at the gate."

"Why not?"

"Because everyone at a gate makes their living telling strangers where
things are and half of them are wrong and the other half want paying."
Ine flicked the reins. "Find a water queue. Women in a water queue at
about six in the evening. Ask three of them, and take the answer that
comes twice."

"Three of them."

"Three. And take the one that comes twice." She looked down. "You'd have
found that out yourself by the second day and it'd have cost you the
second day."

Uzuki got out her father's stub of pencil, and wrote it on the back of
the mill's chit, standing up, walking, in her own bad hand:

*Water queue, 6, ask 3, take the one that comes twice.*

"There you are," said Ine, and laughed at her, not unkindly, for about a
quarter of a mile.

---

They parted at Sanzu at four in the afternoon.

Uzuki did another six miles before the light went and slept in the lee of
a wall with the sacking over her, which was cold and not as bad as she
had expected, and before she went to sleep she took her hand off the cart
handle and put it flat on her coat over the inside pocket, and found the
twist of cloth exactly where it had been at four o'clock on the
twenty-sixth.

*Two days.*

*Nothing happened, twice.*

She lay under the sacking and thought about that for a while, and could
not work out whether it was a good report or no report at all, and went
to sleep before she got to the end of it.
