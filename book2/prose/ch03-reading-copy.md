# Chapter 3 — Twenty Minutes

*Book two. Act one, month 3 (27th–29th) and month 4 (3rd–5th).
Nine scenes, 16,870 words.*



*Act 1, month 4, the third. The Academy at Kirigakure, and the ferry
stair. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,730 words.*

---

Aoyagi was four sentences into the difference between a substitution and
a reversal when somebody put their head round the door of the hall.

Tadayoshi got the whole of it because he was at the end of the row by the
wall and the door was on that side, and because he had been at the end of
the row by the wall for a year and had learned in that time that the end
of the row saw everything.

It was a woman in a grey overcoat and she did not come in.

She said something with about six words in it. Aoyagi said something with
about three. She went.

Then Aoyagi came back to the front of the hall and picked up his sentence
in the wrong place.

---

*He has not finished it.*

*He said "the reversal wants you to have already decided where you are
going, which is why —" and then the door, and now he has started again at
"the reversal wants you to have," and he is going to go round it a second
time and come out somewhere else.*

*He has never done that. Not once in a year. He finishes sentences the way
he closes doors.*

Tadayoshi wrote the date in the corner of his slate, which was a thing he
had started doing about a month ago and had not yet worked out why.

*Third of the fourth month. Grey coat. Four minutes.*

---

It was four minutes.

He counted them off the eave drip, which was going at eleven to the
minute because the rain had settled in overnight and had that flat sound
it got when it intended to stay, and he got to forty-three and then
Aoyagi said:

"Ruri."

"Sensei."

"Do me a favour and run down to the ferry stair."

---

The hall did not do anything. Twenty-five people went on copying out the
difference between a substitution and a reversal, because a boy being
sent on an errand is the single least interesting thing that happens in a
schoolroom.

"Sensei?"

"There's a man called Kōsuke who does the second boat. Long face, no
hat, he'll be at the bottom of the stair or he'll be in the shelter."
Aoyagi was already looking back at the board. "Tell him the fourth is
confirmed."

"The fourth."

"The fourth is confirmed."

"Is there a — " Tadayoshi looked at the desk, which had a slate on it and
chalk and no paper of any kind on it at all. "Do you want me to take
something?"

"No. Just tell him."

"Sensei, if it is a — "

"Ruri," said Aoyagi, "it's four words and it's raining and if you leave
now you'll be back before the bell and if you stand here asking me
questions you won't. Go on."

---

He went.

He got his coat off the hook in the passage and went out through the yard
and down the eleven steps and along the wall, and he was three streets
away before he stopped being annoyed enough to think.

*He did not write it down.*

*He has never once sent anybody anywhere without writing it down. He
wrote a chit for Ryūta to take four staves across the yard. He wrote a
chit for a bucket.*

*And it is nothing. "The fourth is confirmed" is not a message, it is a
fragment of one — there is no subject in it and no place in it and I do
not know what the fourth is.*

He walked down the hill towards the water with his collar up.

*So it is not a message. It is an instruction with a message painted on
the front of it, and the instruction is: be somewhere else.*

*Which means —*

---

*The note.*

He stopped in the street and stood there for a second, and then went on,
faster, because standing still in the rain achieved nothing.

*The note went up eleven days ago. He told me himself, in the corridor,
in four words, in the same voice he has just used.*

*And a woman in a grey overcoat came to the door of a hall and said six
words to him about a boy in that hall, and four minutes later I am out
of the building.*

*They are moving me again.*

He got about a hundred yards on that and found, distantly, that he was
extremely angry.

*Last year it was a form with "aptitude" on it and I was moved a year up
and I was not told until I turned up on the Monday and there were
different people in the room.*

*This year it is a note that a kind man put up because I did four seals
instead of five, and there is a woman in a grey coat, and somebody has
decided something, and the way I am going to be told is that I will be
sent out of a room while they say it.*

*I would like, once, to be in the room.*

He went down the last of the hill and along the wharf and got to the
ferry stair at about twenty past eleven.

---

Kōsuke had a long face and no hat and he was in the shelter, sitting on a
crate with his boots up on a bollard, doing something to a rope.

"Kōsuke-san?"

"That's me."

"Aoyagi-sensei says the fourth is confirmed."

Kōsuke looked up.

"The fourth what?"

---

Tadayoshi stood in the rain at the bottom of the ferry stair.

"That is the message."

"That's the whole of it?"

"That is the whole of it. He said: tell him the fourth is confirmed."

"Aoyagi." Kōsuke put the rope down. "Little fella, glasses, teaches the
seals?"

"He does not wear glasses."

"No. No, he doesn't." Kōsuke scratched the side of his neck. "The fourth
of what, though. The fourth of the month's gone, that was Tuesday. The
fourth boat's not a thing, there's three. I've not got anything on the
fourth."

"I do not know."

"Well," said Kōsuke, and looked at him for a moment with an expression
that Tadayoshi read at the time as mild irritation at having his morning
interrupted by a child with half a sentence.

"You want to get out of that rain," he said.

"I am going back."

"Aye," said Kōsuke. "Well."

---

He went back up the stair.

He was, he estimated afterwards — and he did estimate it, four months
afterwards, sitting somewhere else entirely with a piece of chalk — about
eleven minutes at the bottom of that stair, all told, including the
walking down and the standing about, and there was no possible way to get
any of them back.

At the top he stopped and turned round and looked at the water.

*He does not know what the fourth is either.*

*So it is not a thing they arranged. It is not a code between them,
because a code has to be agreed and Kōsuke has not agreed anything, he is
sitting on a crate with a rope wondering why a wet child has interrupted
him.*

*Aoyagi picked a name off the top of his head. He picked a man he knows by
sight at the bottom of a hill, and a form of words with no content in it,
and sent a boy fourteen hundred yards.*

Tadayoshi stood at the top of the ferry stair in the rain with his hands
in his sleeves.

He got to the edge of it.

He got to the exact edge of it — he could feel the shape of the thing
sitting there the way you can feel a step in the dark before your foot
finds it — and then the bell went, up the hill, thin through the weather,
and he thought: *the bell,* and then he thought: *I will be late,* and
the shape went away.

---

He was late.

Aoyagi did not say anything about it, which he noticed, and filed, and
assigned to the wrong drawer.

The rest of the morning was reversals and the middle of the afternoon was
the wrist drill, and Aoyagi walked the line behind them with his weight
back and the boards clicking, and Tadayoshi did four seals and put his
palm on the floor and swapped with a bench, and Aoyagi walked past him
without putting two fingers anywhere near his wrist, and did not stop,
and did not look at him.

He filed that too.

At the end Aoyagi said, "Right, off you go," to the room, and started
collecting the slates, and Tadayoshi got his coat.

At the door he stopped.

"Sensei."

"Mm."

"The message did not mean anything."

Aoyagi went on stacking slates.

"Didn't it," he said.

"Kōsuke-san said *the fourth what*. He did not know. He was not expecting
it and he did not understand it and he asked me twice."

"Mm."

Tadayoshi stood in the doorway with his coat half on.

*Ask him.*

*He is standing four feet away with his back to you stacking slates and
he has not looked at you all afternoon and he did not tap your wrist and
he is not going to say anything unless you ask, and if you ask he will
have to answer, because he is the sort of man who answers.*

*Ask him what the woman in the grey coat said.*

"Ruri," said Aoyagi, to the slates. "Go home."

---

So he went home.

It was an hour and ten minutes on the coast road at a walk and he did it
in fifty-five because the rain was coming across sideways off the water
and there was no point in being reasonable about it, and he spent about
forty of the fifty-five composing, in his head, in order, with the
objection written in, the case for being told when a decision was made
about him.

It was quite a good case.

He had got as far as the third pass by the time he came up the long
shoulder above the inlet, and he was thinking about the narrow column,
and about what would go in it, and whether *I would like, once, to be in
the room* was a finding or a complaint, and whether there was a
difference.

Then he came over the top and saw the hill.

*Act 1, month 3, the twenty-seventh — day two. The north road above Ōgi.
Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,750 words.*

---

The wood started about a mile past the third milestone and it was two
miles through, and she got to the edge of it at half past nine on the
second morning having done eleven miles the day before and slept in a
byre with permission.

She stopped at the edge and looked at it.

*If the road at the wood is wet you wait for a cart and go with it.*

The road at the wood was not wet.

That was the first thing, and she stood there for a good four minutes
being pleased about it, because the rain had come through in the night
and she had gone to sleep certain she was going to lose half a day, and
she had woken up to a hard sky and a road with the water already off it.

*It's dry. That's the condition and the condition isn't met.*

*So I can go.*

She went about forty yards in and then came back out again.

---

There were three men sitting at the side of the road just inside the
trees.

That was all. That was the entire thing, and she went over it about nine
hundred times afterwards and it never got any bigger than that: three
men, sitting on the bank on the left, about forty yards in, where the
trees started.

They were not doing anything.

They had no cart and no barrow and no packs that she could see and no
tools. They were not eating. One of them was lying back on the bank with
his hat over his face and the other two were sitting up, and one of those
two had looked at her, once, without any interest at all, in the way you
look at a bird landing.

And then he had gone on looking at her.

---

She came back out to the edge and stood by the milestone with her hand on
the cart handle and her heart going, and she was furious with herself
about the heart, because *nothing had happened*.

*They're waiting for somebody. People wait for people. Da waits at
Fusano for a man about a barrel and he waits three hours sometimes and
sits on a wall.*

*It's a wood on a carting road and there's three men in it and I have
been on a road for a day and a half and everybody I have passed has been
somebody.*

She looked back in.

The one who had looked at her was still looking.

---

*The condition isn't met.*

She stood at the milestone and had the argument with herself properly,
out loud in her head, in order, the way she had learned to have arguments
in the eleven days before Kayano.

*One. Ma said if it's wet. It isn't wet. So the rule doesn't cover it and
I'm free.*

*Two. Ma said if it's wet because a wet road is a slow road and a slow
road is two hours in there instead of forty minutes. So the rule isn't
about the water. It's about how long I'd be in the trees.*

*Three. So the rule does cover it. She just didn't have the words for
the actual thing, or she had them and didn't want to say them to me at a
table.*

*Four —*

She stopped at four.

*Four. If I go in there and I'm wrong, I don't get to find out I was
wrong. I get to be a girl in a wood.*

*And if I sit here for three hours and a cart comes and I go through with
it and nothing happens, I still never find out. There's no version of
this where I get told.*

---

She sat down on the milestone.

It was, in the end, the most annoying decision she had ever made, and she
made it in about a minute and a half, and the annoying part was not the
waiting.

The annoying part was that she was never going to know.

---

She waited two hours and forty minutes.

She did not sit still for them, because sitting still with three men
forty yards away was not a thing her body was going to agree to. She got
the bokken out from under the sacking and went thirty yards back down the
road the way she had come, to where the bank was clear and she could see
both ways, and she did the third form.

She did it eleven times.

She was rubbish at it. She was rubbish at it in a specific and
identifiable way, which was that the eighth count kept going hard again,
because her hands wanted to be doing something and hands that want to be
doing something spend a bucket.

*Late. Not at the set. At the going.*

Twelfth time. Thirteenth.

She got two of them clean out of the fifteen and neither of them was one
she could have repeated, and she was aware, in a distant and unwelcome
way, that she was practising in order to have somewhere to put her hands.

At about half past eleven she heard a cart.

---

It was a woman with a load of roofing slate and a boy of about fifteen
walking at the horse's head, and the woman had a face like a plank and
did not slow down.

Uzuki got up off the milestone.

"Excuse me — "

"No room."

"I don't want a ride, I've got a cart, I want to walk beside you through
the wood."

The woman looked down at her. Then at the little cart. Then up the road
at the trees.

"How many?"

"Three."

"Sitting or walking?"

"Sitting. On the left, about forty yards in, one's got a hat over his
face."

The woman said something under her breath that Uzuki did not entirely
catch and did not need to.

"Get your cart in behind mine," she said, "and stay on this side of the
horse, and don't talk to me while we're in there, and if I say get up on
the load you get up on the load and you don't ask me why. Tarō."

"Ma."

"Walk on her outside."

---

It took forty minutes and nothing happened.

That was all. They went in with the slate cart making the noise a slate
cart makes, which was an enormous amount of noise, and the boy Tarō
walked on Uzuki's outside with a stick he had picked up without appearing
to pick it up, and the woman walked at the horse's head and looked
straight ahead.

At forty yards the three men were still on the bank.

One of them said, "Morning."

"Morning," said the woman, and did not stop.

And that was it. The bank went past and the trees went on and after a
while they thinned and there was a field and the road went down towards
the water meadows, and Uzuki was through.

---

"Right," said the woman, at the far edge, and stopped, and let out a
breath that she had evidently been holding for two miles. "Tarō, drink."

Uzuki stood in the road with her hands still on the cart handle.

"Nothing happened," she said.

"No."

"They just said morning."

"They did." The woman took the waterskin off her son and drank and gave
it back. "That's what they do. What did you want, a knife?"

"I don't — " Uzuki stopped. "I don't know what I wanted."

"You wanted to find out," said the woman. "So you'd know if you were
being a fool."

"Yes."

"Aye. Well." She wiped her mouth. "You don't get that. That's the whole
trade of being a woman on a road, and I'll tell it you for nothing since
you're ten and you're on one: *you never find out.* You do the careful
thing and nothing happens and you spend the rest of the day feeling
daft, and you do the careless thing and nothing happens and you spend the
rest of your life thinking you're clever, and there's no way of telling
those two apart from inside.

"So you do the careful thing. And you keep doing it. And you never get to
know, and that's the price, and it's not a big one."

---

They went on together as far as the fork at Sanzu because it suited them
both, and the woman turned out to be called Ine and to be no longer
frightening at all once she was out of the trees, and she asked what
Uzuki was doing on that road and got the whole of it.

She did not say it was a good plan. She did not say it was a bad one.

What she said, about two miles later, apropos of nothing, was:

"When you get to Kayano. Don't ask at the gate."

"Why not?"

"Because everyone at a gate makes their living telling strangers where
things are and half of them are wrong and the other half want paying."
Ine flicked the reins. "Find a water queue. Women in a water queue at
about six in the evening. Ask three of them, and take the answer that
comes twice."

"Three of them."

"Three. And take the one that comes twice." She looked down. "You'd have
found that out yourself by the second day and it'd have cost you the
second day."

Uzuki got out her father's stub of pencil, and wrote it on the back of
the mill's chit, standing up, walking, in her own bad hand:

*Water queue, 6, ask 3, take the one that comes twice.*

"There you are," said Ine, and laughed at her, not unkindly, for about a
quarter of a mile.

---

They parted at Sanzu at four in the afternoon.

Uzuki did another six miles before the light went and slept in the lee of
a wall with the sacking over her, which was cold and not as bad as she
had expected, and before she went to sleep she took her hand off the cart
handle and put it flat on her coat over the inside pocket, and found the
twist of cloth exactly where it had been at four o'clock on the
twenty-sixth.

*Two days.*

*Nothing happened, twice.*

She lay under the sacking and thought about that for a while, and could
not work out whether it was a good report or no report at all, and went
to sleep before she got to the end of it.

*Act 1, month 4, the third. The shoulder above the inlet, Shioiri.
Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,970 words.*

---

Nothing was burning.

That was the first thing and it was the thing that kept him standing
there for the first eleven seconds, because a hill with people on it who
should not be on it, at four in the afternoon, in the rain, with nothing
burning, is a hill you can still explain.

*A survey. The eave on the fifth went two winters ago and somebody has
finally come about the roofs.*

*A boat come in wrong and the men off it walking up to ask.*

*Hisui. Hisui has gone and done something about the house at Ōura and
these are men come to look at what we have.*

He got three explanations out in eleven seconds and then he stopped
making them, because the sixth course had four men on it and the sixth
course had not had four people on it at one time since before he was
born.

---

He went down off the road and put himself behind the gorse on the
shoulder, and lay on his front in the wet with his chin on his forearms,
and started counting.

That was not a decision. He wanted afterwards to be able to say that it
had been one, and it had not; his body had gone off the road and got
down and his hands had put themselves under his chin, and then he had
started counting because counting was the thing that was there.

*Sixth course: four. Two at the end house, two on the path.*

*Fifth course: two, going down.*

*Fourth: three, and one of them is standing in the middle of the path not
doing anything, which means he is the one who is telling the others where
to go.*

*Third: I cannot see the workshop from here. I have never been able to
see the workshop from here.*

*Second course — *

The second course had the low house on it, and the low house had its door
open, and there were people on the path outside it.

*Eleven. There are eleven of them and there may be more on the third and
in the sheds.*

*They came in at the top and the bottom.*

He knew that from the wet on the top road, which he had just walked, and
which had been churned all down the seaward edge by boots going the other
way — and he had walked over it eleven minutes ago composing a case about
being told things, and had not looked down once.

*They came over the shoulder while I was at the ferry stair and they came
up from the inlet at the same time, and they closed it.*

---

There was a boat at the inlet.

It was tied at the stone and it was not a Shioiri boat. He knew every
boat that had ever tied at that stone; there were four of them and one
belonged to a man at the head of the water who came round for the grit
sieves twice a year, and this was not any of them. It sat lower and it
had a house on it and it had six men on the stone beside it who were not
doing anything except being on the stone beside it.

*A boat with a house on it is a boat that is going somewhere with people
in it.*

He put his face down into his forearms for a second.

Then he lifted it again, because he was going to look, because there was
going to be one chance at this and he was the only one who was going to
be able to say afterwards what he had seen.

---

They brought them down in one group.

It was, he would work out later, about four hundred and forty yards, and
at four hundred and forty yards in that light he could not see a face. He
could see coats, and how people walked, and how tall they were, and that
was all, and it was enough for six of them and not enough for two.

*Meno is the one being carried.*

*She is being carried by two of them with her arms over their shoulders,
which is how you move a woman of seventy-one who cannot go down a course
path at speed, and they are doing it the way you do it when you have done
it before.*

*My father is the tall one at the back with his hands behind him.*

*Akagane is the one in front of him because Akagane walks with his head
down and always has.*

*Hisui is —*

He stopped, and went back, and did the group again from the front.

*Hisui is carrying Koishi.*

That took a long time to be sure of, because Koishi was small and the
coats were dark and the light was going, and what he had was a shape at
Hisui's shoulder that changed the way Hisui walked. He watched it for
about forty seconds until it moved, once, and turned its head into
Hisui's neck.

*Koishi is on the list.*

*Koishi is six and she is not assessed for another two years and she is
on the list.*

---

*Which means the list is the household.*

He lay in the gorse with the rain coming across the shoulder and put that
where it went, and it went somewhere very cold and very orderly and
stayed there.

*It is not a collection. A collection takes a producer. There are two
producers in that family and one of them is on that path and one of them
is lying in the gorse.*

*It is the house. All of it. Every name.*

*Which means it is not about the trade at all.*

He looked for his mother.

He found her third from the front and he was not sure and then he was
sure, because she stopped.

She stopped on the turn between the third course and the second, where
the path goes round the rock, and she turned her head and looked back up
the hill.

Not at him. She could not have seen him; he was six hundred yards off and
behind gorse and lying flat. She looked back up at the courses, the way
you look at a thing you are checking, once, and one of the men said
something and she turned round and went on down.

*She was counting us.*

*She has been in this family for thirty-four years and she has just been
walked down her own hill and she stopped on the turn to count, and she
got seven, and she will have gone down the rest of that path knowing
exactly which one is missing and exactly where he was sent this morning.*

*And she will not say. She will never say. That is the one thing on this
earth I can be certain of.*

---

He did the arithmetic.

He would think about the fact that he had done the arithmetic for the
rest of his life, and he would never once be able to make it come out any
other way, and that was the thing about it.

*If I go down.*

*I am four hundred and forty yards from the second course by the shoulder
path and there are two on the path and three on the fourth and I would
not get past the fourth. Call it ninety seconds.*

*So: I go down and I am on that boat.*

*If I am on that boat, is anybody helped?*

*Meno is not helped. My father is not helped. Koishi is not helped. There
is no arrangement in which a person on a boat improves the position of
the other seven people on the same boat.*

*The list is the household and I am on the list. So going down does not
subtract me from anything. It adds me.*

*Therefore the only version of this in which anybody is helped by
anything at all is the one where I am not on that boat.*

He read it back.

*Therefore: do not go down.*

---

He did not go down.

He lay in the gorse on the shoulder above the inlet, six hundred yards
from the low house, and watched eight people be walked down four hundred
and forty yards of path and put on a boat with a house on it, and the
whole of it took about twenty minutes, and there was no shouting.

He heard three things in the twenty minutes.

He heard a door, on the third course, which somebody shut.

He heard one of the men at the stone laugh at something, and stop.

And near the end, when they were getting them onto the boat and there was
some business about the plank and the step down, he heard a child's voice
say something, twice, at the pitch a child uses when it is asking a
question and not being answered — and it was too far to be words and it
went on for about four seconds and then it stopped.

---

The boat went out at about twenty past five.

He watched it go out past the bar and turn north, which was not the way
to Ōura and was not the way to anywhere he knew about, and he watched it
until it was a thing on the water and then until it was nothing, and it
took a long time, because the inlet was long and the light went slowly
that far up the coast.

He was still lying down.

At some point during it — he could not afterwards say when, and it
bothered him — he had put his hand flat on the ground in front of him and
was holding onto the grass.

---

They went at about six.

The last four came down the courses in no hurry at all, and one of them
went back up for something and came down again, and then they were on the
stone and there was a second boat, smaller, and they got into it and went.

Nobody had gone up above the fourth course except at the very start.

The compound sat there in the dark with three doors open on the second
course and one on the third and no lamp lit anywhere on the whole hill,
for the first time in four hundred years.

---

Tadayoshi lay in the gorse for another hour and eleven minutes.

He did not need to. He worked that out at the time and lay there anyway,
and the reason he gave himself was that men come back, and the reason was
correct, and it was not the reason.

At about a quarter past seven he got up.

His coat had gone through in the front and his hands would not close
properly and there was a place on his left forearm where he had been
lying on a stone for two hours and had not noticed.

He stood on the shoulder in the dark and looked down at the hill with the
doors open on it.

*I did not go down.*

*I have the whole of it. I have where they came in and how many and how
long and which way the boat went and I have Koishi on the list, and not
one of those is a thing anybody would have if I had gone down, and all of
it is correct, and I can defend every line of it.*

*And I have been over it four times and I cannot find the hole in it.*

He stood there a while longer.

Then he went — not down the shoulder path to the second course, which was
the way home and was six hundred yards.

He went along the top, and up, to the sixth.

*Act 1. A room. A boy, age twelve. POV: the boy. ~1,370 words.*

---

The food came warm on the ninth day after the inspection.

It had never come warm. It came at the same times and it was the same
food and it had always been the temperature of the passage, which was the
temperature of everything, and on the ninth day the bowl was warm against
the sides of his hands and he sat with it for a while before he ate any
of it.

He counted the bowl. That did not tell him anything.

Then he ate it, and it was more than it had been.

---

*Eleven days since the inspection. Two days of warm.*

*More in the bowl on both of them. Not much more. The bread is the same
and the rest is more.*

He did not eat it faster. He worked that out on the first warm day and it
took him about as long as it took to eat: **if you eat more the same way
you ate less, nobody has to think about you.** If you eat it fast they
notice you ate it fast and then somebody wonders whether you were hungry
before, and then somebody has a question about a thing that had no

Nothing.

Nothing.

tanneries are always on the water because of the — because of whatever it

*And then I'd be a girl who turned up at a hole in the ground where a

something.*

She stood there.

*He'd say no.*

*He wouldn't even say no properly, he'd say it the way you say it when
you're carrying something — he'd say "not now" and that's worse than no,
because "not now" is a thing you can't argue with and it's a thing you
can't come back from either, because the next time he sees you he's
already said it.*

*I get one go at it. That's the whole plan. The whole plan is that I walk
in and tell a busy person how to have a shorter week, and that only works
if he's got a week.*

She put her hand down.

*So I wait.*

*And I never find out if he'd have said yes tonight.*

She was, she noticed, getting extremely tired of that.

She slept in the doorway.

It was not as stupid as it sounds and she had thought about it: the

the top of her instead of into her, and there was a lane wall on one

The bokken went along her right side under the coat.

there for four days and she had not spent one coin of it, and she was
going to bring it back and put it where it was.

*Four days,* she thought. *And a step down into a doorway.*

*He'll come home.*

Somewhere on the other side of the bridge a bell went for something, and
Uzuki Nozaki went to sleep on the ground outside a green door that had
been blue, four days' walk from a house where her father was awake.
