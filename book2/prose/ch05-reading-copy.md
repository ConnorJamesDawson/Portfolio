# Chapter 5 — Alive If Possible

*Book two. Act one, month 4 (9th) – month 5, end.
Nine scenes, 15,480 words.*




*Act 1, month 4, the ninth. Sunaba. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV:
Tadayoshi. ~1,810 words.*

---

Sunaba had four hundred and eleven boats in it.

He counted them from the head of the north pier on the first afternoon,
which took him about two hours and which he did because he could not
think of anything else to do with the two hours, and which turned out
afterwards to have been the most useful thing he did that week.

*Four hundred and eleven, and about sixty of those are the same shape,
and about nine of them have a house on the deck.*

*Nine.*

*And every single one of the nine is tied up at the far end where the
water is deep, and every single one of them is bigger than the one I saw,
because the one I saw came into an inlet that goes dry at low water.*

He sat on a bollard with his feet not reaching the ground.

*So it was small for its class. It was a small boat with a house on it,
drawing under four feet, which is a boat that can go up an inlet, and
those are not tied up at the deep end and I have not counted them.*

He went and counted those.

There were thirty-one of them and none of them was it, and he had not
expected any of them to be, and doing it took the rest of the light and
he was extremely glad of it.

---

Sunaba was not Ōura.

Ōura had two hundred roofs and a bridge with shops on it and one man at
one counter who knew his father. Sunaba had a bay you could not see the
far side of in weather, and a stone harbour with three arms, and streets
going back up the hill in terraces that reminded him of nothing at all
because they were not courses, they were just streets, and there were
about nine thousand people in it.

He had never seen nine thousand people.

The first thing he had done on arriving, before anything else, was find
out where Kanaya's main house was — a four-storey building on the second
street back from the middle arm, with a yard and a gate and its own
weighbridge — and then work out four routes across the town that did not
go past it.

*Kenji will have written it down. He is a man who writes things down. He
told me not to come back and he meant his counter, and the man who wrote
the return that took my family also works for that house, somewhere in
that building, and I do not know what he looks like.*

*So: not that street. Not the second street back. Not the middle arm
between the sheds and the weighbridge.*

He kept to the north arm after that and did not go into the middle of the
town for eleven days.

---

The harbour office was on the north arm, which was luck and not judgement.

It was a two-storey stone thing at the root of the pier with a clock on
it that was wrong, and it had a counter inside with a rail, and behind the
rail there were three desks and a stove and a wall of shelves with about
two hundred bound books on them.

He stood in the queue for forty minutes on the second morning to find out
what was in them.

---

The queue was masters and mates and one woman with a fish tally and a boy
of about fourteen from a chandler's, and it moved slowly because
everything in that room was written down twice.

That was the first thing he learned and he learned it standing in a line.

*Everything goes in twice. The man at the counter writes it in the day
book at his elbow, and then at some point somebody carries the day book
through to the back and it goes into the bound one, and the bound ones
are on the shelf, and there is one for every year and there are about
eleven years of them and then it changes and the older ones are a
different colour.*

*So there is a book with today in it and a book with four years ago in
it, and they are eleven feet apart, and one of them is on a desk and one
of them is on a shelf.*

He got to the front.

---

"Yes," said the man at the counter, without looking up.

"I would like to see the dues book for the fourth month."

The man looked up.

He was about thirty-five and he had ink on the side of his hand and he
had the face of somebody who has been asked eleven thousand questions.

"Whose account?"

"I do not have an account."

"Then what do you want the dues book for?"

*Do not.*

*You have four days of evidence that says the answer to that question
loses you the conversation. She asked you whose boat it was and you said
you did not know and she stopped being helpful in about a second and a
half.*

*Do not answer it. Answer something else that is true.*

"I am trying to find out where a boat went," said Tadayoshi.

Which was, he understood about a quarter of a second later, exactly the
same answer.

---

"Right," said the man. "Where's your father?"

"He is not here."

"Where's your master?"

"I do not have one."

"Then you're not seeing the dues book," said the man, not unkindly, and
already looking past him at the next person. "It's not a thing you look
at, son, it's a thing you're *in*. Somebody with an account can ask about
their own entries and that's the whole of it. Next."

"May I ask — "

"Next."

---

He went and stood by the stove for eleven minutes because nobody in that
room had told him not to and because eleven minutes by a stove was worth
having.

And he watched.

That was the entire eleven minutes and it was worth more than the forty
in the queue.

*The day book at the counter is the wide flat one with the marbled boards
and it is open all day and he writes in it with his left hand.*

*The bound ones on the shelf are done in the back by the old man at the
end desk, who is copying out of a day book right now — not today's, an
older one, so they are behind — and he is doing it with a rule and he is
doing about four lines a minute.*

*Nobody goes near the shelf except him. He got up twice in eleven minutes
and both times it was to the stove.*

*And the counter man cannot see the shelf from where he stands, because
of the rail, and the partition, and the way the desks are set.*

He filed all of that and did not do anything with it.

Then, on the way out, he got the thing he had actually come for, and he
got it entirely by accident, over the shoulder of a man arguing about
nine ryō.

---

The day book had five columns.

He read them upside down from four feet away in about two seconds,
because he had been reading ruled columns upside down across a table since
he was six years old and it had never once occurred to him that this was
a skill.

*Day. Craft. Master. Cargo. Dues.*

*Same as Ōura. Same five. It is the same form.*

And down the dues column, in among the figures, three or four times on
that page, in a hand that had clearly written it about nine thousand
times:

*Ex.*

---

He got out onto the pier and stood against the wall in the wind.

*Ex.*

*Exempt. It has to be exempt, it is in a money column and it is not a
number.*

*So there are craft that come into this harbour and do not pay.*

He turned that over.

*Who does not pay?*

*Not a chandler. Not a fish boat, there was a fish tally in the queue and
she was paying. Not a coaster, the man arguing about nine ryō was arguing
about nine ryō.*

*So: somebody who is not being charged because the charging would be the
harbour billing somebody who owns the harbour.*

He stood in the wind with his hands in his sleeves.

*The state does not pay dues.*

*Of course the state does not pay dues. You do not invoice yourself.*

*So every state craft that has come into Sunaba is in that book as two
letters, and two letters is what I am going to get, and there will be no
master and no cargo and no —*

He stopped.

*No destination.*

*There is no destination column. There has never been a destination
column. Ōura did not have one either and I read four years of it and did
not notice, because I was looking for a name and not for the shape of the
page.*

*A dues book records what came IN. It does not record where anything
went, because where a thing goes is not the harbour's business and nobody
has ever paid a penny for it.*

---

He stood on the north arm at Sunaba for a long time.

*I have walked two days to a book that cannot answer the question.*

*And I have been building toward this book for six days and it was the
only thing I had.*

He looked at the four hundred and eleven boats.

*All right.*

*Enter what is true and do not enter what it feels like.*

*One. The book cannot tell me where. Nothing in a harbour can tell me
where, because a harbour is a place things arrive.*

*Two. The book can tell me WHETHER — whether a state craft came in, and
on what day. And a state craft that took eight people north past a bar at
twenty past five on the third of the fourth month either came in here or
it did not, and either answer is worth having, and I have no way of
getting either of them from the pier.*

*Three. That book is eleven feet from a rail and the man who writes in it
cannot see the shelf.*

He put his hand flat against his coat over the drop.

*Four. The old man at the end desk is behind. He is copying an old day
book on a Tuesday afternoon with a rule and there is a stack beside him,
and a man who is behind is a man who would take help.*

---

He went back in an hour later and stood at the rail until the counter man
looked up.

"You again."

"I would like a job," said Tadayoshi.

*Act 1, month 4, the seventh. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki.
~1,390 words.*

---

She was up at five and did the yard.

There was no reason to do the yard. The yard had been done — the two
women had done it, or somebody had, in the two days — and she went out
and did it again at five in the morning in the dark because she had woken
up at ten past four and lain there for fifty minutes and then got up.

She swept the whole of it. She took the barrow back to where the barrow
lived, which took a while because it turned out that somebody had put a
sack of something in it and she had to find somewhere for the sack. She
did the water. She went and looked at the mule and the mule was fine.

At about six her mother came out and stood on the step.

"Uzuki."

"I've done the yard."

"I can see you've done the yard."

"It wanted doing."

"It didn't," said her mother, and went in, and left the door open behind
her, which was the invitation.

---

The kitchen was the kitchen.

That was the thing about it. There was nothing wrong with the kitchen at
all; the cloth had gone off the table and the two women had gone home and
the pot was on and the crock was where the crock was, and if you had come
in off a road you would have said it was an ordinary kitchen with two
people in it.

Uzuki sat down at the table.

"Ma."

"Mm."

"You said ask me tomorrow."

Her mother put the ladle down on the rest, squarely.

"I did," she said. "Go on then."

---

"You said if I was eleven days you'd come and get me."

"Yes."

"So why didn't you."

"Because I was never going to," said her mother.

---

Uzuki sat at the table with her hands flat on it.

"You — "

"I want you to let me do the whole of it," said her mother, "because I've
had eleven days to get it into an order and if you stop me I'll not get
it out again and then we'll both be carrying it round this house for a
year."

"All right."

"Right." Sae Nozaki sat down opposite her daughter and put her forearms
on the table. "I knew when I said it that I wasn't going to. I knew it
while it was coming out of my mouth. I've known it every day since."

"Then why did you — "

"Because of the *money*, Uzuki." She said it flatly. "There's the sum
and there isn't anything else and there never was.

"Say you'd been eleven days. Say you'd been twelve. What do I do? I put
on my coat and I walk to Ōgi, which is eleven miles, and then I'm at Ōgi
and it's the afternoon and I've got no money and I've got a man at home
who can't get up the stairs on his own. And Kayano is three more days.

"Three days there and three days back, and something for the road, and
what am I going to *do* when I get there? Stand in a lane and shout at a
green door? You were already inside it or you weren't.

"So I'd have got to Ōgi and I'd have stood in the road at Ōgi, exactly
like I said, and it would have been the whole of what I could do, and it
would have been worth nothing at all except to me."

---

"Then why did you say it in front of him."

Her mother did not answer for a moment.

"Because that's what you say," she said, "in front of a man who's just
been told he can let his daughter go.

"He'd sat up in that bed and said the worst thing anybody's said in this
house and he'd said it in front of you on purpose, and then he'd argued
me down over nine days, and he was *right*, and I'd got nothing left to
put against it except being frightened.

"And a woman who's got nothing left says a thing about standing in a road
and shouting, so that everybody in the room can hear that she'd have come
if she could.

"It was for him," said her mother. "It wasn't for you and it wasn't for
me. It was so he could go to sleep."

---

Uzuki sat at the table and found that she could not get hold of any of
it.

She had come down those stairs with something built. She had built it in
the dark between four and five and it had been quite good: it had had
*you said* in it about four times and it had had the road at Ōgi in it
and it had ended somewhere she had not fully worked out but which was
going to be devastating.

And her mother had taken the whole of it and laid it out on the table
herself, in order, before Uzuki had got to the second sentence — including
the parts Uzuki had not thought of, and including the part where she was
wrong, and had not once said *I'm sorry* and had not once said *you have
to understand.*

"You're not sorry," said Uzuki.

"No."

"You could — " Her voice went somewhere she did not want it. "You could
say it anyway."

"I could," said her mother. "And then you'd have that instead of this,
and in about four years you'd work out I'd said it to get you off the
subject, and you'd be angrier about it then than you are now, and I'd not
be able to fix it because you'd be fifteen."

She got up and went back to the pot.

"You can be as cross with me as you like," she said. "You've got the
right and I'll not argue with you about it. But you'll be cross with the
true thing, and not with a nice thing I said in a kitchen to get through
a Tuesday."

---

Uzuki sat there for a while.

*She's right.*

*She's right and I hate it and she's right, and she was right about the
hundred and she was right about the four seconds and she was right that
if she'd told me I'd have carried it.*

*And she's been on her own in this house for two days working out the
order to say all this in.*

"Ma."

"Mm."

"On the fourth."

Her mother's back did not move.

"Was he — " Uzuki put both hands round each other on the table. "Was he
awake? In the day. Was he — could he talk?"

"In the morning."

"What did he say?"

---

Her mother stood at the stove with her back to the room for a long
moment.

"He asked me what day it was," she said.

"What?"

"He asked me what the date was. About nine o'clock. And I told him it was
the fourth, and he said *right*, and went back to sleep."

"That's — " Uzuki stopped. "That's all?"

"That's all he said all day."

*The fourth.*

*Nine days from the twenty-sixth is the fifth.*

*He was counting.*

*He had it, the whole time. He was lying in that room doing the same sum
I was doing on a road and he asked what day it was so he could work out
how far off I was, and he got the fourth, and he said right.*

*And he went back to sleep, and that was the last thing he ever said, and
it was a date.*

---

Uzuki got up from the table.

"I'm going to go and do the ninety," she said.

"The what?"

"It's a — " She stopped in the doorway. She had been told, on a step in
Kanmuri lane, by a man who had given her three and a half hours he had
not got: *never tell anybody I taught you. Not your mother.*

"It's an exercise," she said. "It's for my arms."

"Right," said her mother.

And Uzuki went out into the yard she had already swept, and sat down on
the wall in the cold with her sleeve up, and put her right hand on her own
left forearm, and started.

*Little. Stop. Wait.*

*One.*

*Act 1, month 4, the eleventh to the twenty-sixth. The harbour office,
Sunaba. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,480 words.*

---

They gave him the stove, the messages, and nine a week.

"And your dinner," said the counter man, whose name was Ide and who had
looked at him for about four seconds before saying yes, which Tadayoshi
did not understand at the time and understood entirely about a year
later, when he had seen enough ten-year-olds sleeping under things.

"Your dinner, and you'll sleep in the back with the coal, and if I find
you've had so much as a nail out of this building you'll be over that
rail on your head. Can you count?"

"Yes."

"Can you write?"

"Yes."

"Everybody says that," said Ide. "Nunome. Give him a pen."

---

Nunome was seventy-something and he had the end desk and he had been
behind for four years.

That was not an accident and it was not laziness; Tadayoshi worked it out
in about two days. The office took the day books from six stations up and
down that coast — the small posts, some of them manned and some of them
not, that sent their books in by boat every month — and one old man with a
rule was supposed to copy the whole of that coast into the bound annual
volumes, and had been supposed to for eleven years, and could do about
four lines a minute.

He gave Tadayoshi a pen and a sheet of scrap and said, "Copy that," and
went back to what he was doing.

Tadayoshi copied it.

He ruled it first, which took a moment, because the scrap was not ruled
and the thing he was copying had four columns in it; and he ruled the
fourth one narrow, because that was how it was done.

Then he did the eleven lines in about three minutes in the small exact
hand and squared it off and put it on the corner of the desk.

Nunome looked at it for a while.

"Where'd you learn to rule?"

"My great-aunt keeps the — " He stopped. "My great-aunt kept books."

"Did she."

"She had four columns and the fourth one was narrower because whoever
ruled the page first thought it would need less room."

Nunome made a noise in his nose.

"That's the only thing wrong with your hand," he said. "It's too good.
You'll slow yourself down being neat and by the eleventh year you'll have
a stoop. Sit there. Not that stool, that one's got a leg."

---

He copied for fifteen days.

He did the stove at six and the messages until about ten and then he sat
at the corner of Nunome's desk from ten until the light went, and he
copied the coast out of six day books into the bound volume for the
year, and by the fourth day he had stopped having to think about it and
by the eighth he was faster than Nunome and neither of them mentioned it.

*This is the best thing that has happened to me since the second month.*

*I should enter that somewhere. It is a finding.*

*I am sitting at a desk in a warm room with a pen doing four hundred
lines a day and my hands have closed up for the first time since the
tenth month, and I have not thought about the hill for*—

He looked at the clock, which was wrong, and then at the tide board,
which was not.

*—four hours and eleven minutes.*

*Enter it and do not enter what it feels like.*

---

The exemptions were on the twelfth day.

He had been waiting for them since the second and had made himself not
look, which was the hardest professional thing he had ever done, and the
reason he made himself not look was that he had worked out what happens
to a boy who opens a bound book at a particular month on his first
afternoon.

*Do it in order. Do it because it is the order. You are copying the
fourth month because the fourth month is what comes after the third
month, and Nunome will hand it to you and there will be nothing in the
room that has happened.*

So he did the first month, and the second month, and the third month, and
he did them at four hundred lines a day and he did not look ahead, and on
the twelfth day Nunome put the fourth month's bundle on the corner of the
desk and said, "That's Nezu and the two on the north shore in there as
well, they came in late," and went to the stove.

---

*Ex.*

There it was, on the second page, in a station clerk's hand that sloped
backwards: a craft in the day column, nothing in the master column,
nothing in the cargo column, and *Ex.* where the money went.

He copied it.

Then he did the rest of the month, all six stations, three hundred and
some lines, in order, without stopping, and he did not let his eye go
forward down a page once, and when he had finished he went back to the
top and counted.

*Three.*

*Three in the whole month. On the whole of this coast, six stations,
thirty days, three craft came in and did not pay.*

*The twelfth, at Sunaba. The nineteenth, at Sunaba.*

*And the third, at Nezu.*

---

He sat at the corner of an old man's desk in a warm room and put the pen
down and did not touch anything for about a minute.

*The third.*

*The boat went out past the bar at Shioiri at about twenty past five in
the afternoon of the third of the fourth month, and it went north, and
Nezu is north.*

*How far north.*

He got up and went to the wall where the tide boards were and the chart
was, and stood in front of the chart with his hands behind his back like
a man looking at a chart, and found it.

*Forty-one miles. About four days walking, more if the road goes round
the head of every inlet, and it does, because they all do.*

He went back and sat down.

*And that is the whole of it.*

*There is no master. There is no cargo. There is no destination column
and there never was one, because a harbour does not care where you are
going and has never been paid a penny to find out.*

*A craft came into Nezu on the third of the fourth month and did not pay
dues, and the entry is nine characters long, and I have been in this room
for fifteen days.*

---

He sat with it for the rest of the afternoon and did another hundred and
some lines, badly, and at about four he got hold of the other end of it.

*It is not nothing.*

*I have been treating this like a name. It is not a name. It is a
NEGATIVE and negatives are answers.*

*Before today I had: north, and a time, and forty miles of coast, and
nine boats with houses on them that were all the wrong size.*

*Now I have: it stopped at Nezu.*

*And a boat that stops has a reason. It stops for water, or for the tide,
or because somebody gets off, and it is forty-one miles from Shioiri
which is about right for the light going and about right for a small
craft that does not want to be in the open at night.*

*So it stopped at Nezu on the third and it did not stop at Sunaba on the
fourth or the fifth, because I have just done the fourth and the fifth
and there is nothing there.*

*Which means it went north again from Nezu, or it stayed at Nezu, or
whatever was on it stopped being on it at Nezu.*

*And every one of those is a thing you find out at Nezu.*

---

He copied until the light went.

That night he wrote it up in the back room by the coal, sitting on his
blanket with the ledger on his knees, and it came out in four lines and
took him about a minute, and it was the first entry since the fourth
month that had gone in without a fight.

*Nezu. Third of the fourth. One exempt entry, nine characters, no master,
no cargo.*

*Three exemptions on the whole coast in the month, so it is a rare thing
and it can be counted, and a thing that can be counted can be followed.*

*Forty-one miles. Four days.*

And then, in the narrow column, because that was where the true things
went:

*A dues book records what came in and never where anything went. Every
book records the thing somebody was paid to record and nothing else.*

*Find out what the book was FOR before you look in it.*

*Act 1, month 4, the twenty-sixth. The harbour office, Sunaba. Tadayoshi
Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,520 words.*

---

Nunome waited three days.

Tadayoshi worked that out afterwards and it was the part that stayed with
him: the old man had watched him stop dead on a page on the Tuesday, and
had said nothing on the Tuesday and nothing on the Wednesday and nothing
on the Thursday, and had spent three days deciding.

On the Friday he put his rule down at about four o'clock and said:

"You stopped on the fourth month."

---

"I did not stop."

"You stopped for a minute and eleven seconds," said Nunome, "which I know
because I was watching the clock, and the clock is wrong but it is
wrong *consistently*. And then you got up and went and looked at the
chart with your hands behind your back like a man waiting for a train,
and you came back and you did the rest of the month and about forty of
them were bad."

Tadayoshi put his pen down.

"Then you went back to the top," said Nunome, "and you counted."

---

The stove ticked. Ide was at the counter with somebody about a bill of
lading and they were both being patient at each other.

"You counted the exemptions," said Nunome.

"Yes."

"Nobody counts the exemptions. In eleven years I've had two clerks and a
harbour master and a man from the revenue in this room and not one of
them has ever counted the exemptions, because there's no money in the
column and money is the only thing anybody has ever wanted out of that
book."

He turned round on his stool, which took a while.

"Who was on it?"

---

Tadayoshi sat at the corner of the desk with his hands in his lap.

*He has gone straight through.*

*Three days. He has had three days and he has gone from "the boy stopped
on a page" to "there is somebody on a boat" without any of the middle,
and he has done it because he has copied out a coast for eleven years and
he knows exactly what an exempt entry is and exactly who looks at one.*

*And I cannot lie to him.*

*I could say nothing. Nothing is not lying.*

He said nothing.

Nunome nodded slowly, the way you nod at an answer.

"Aye," he said. "Well."

---

"I'll not say anything."

That was the sentence.

Tadayoshi heard it and his whole chest went loose for about a second and
a half with a relief so complete that he was ashamed of it for years,
and then the second and a half ended.

*He will not say anything.*

*Which means he has considered saying something.*

*Which means it has been a question in this room for three days, and he
has answered it one way, today, on a Friday, out of kindness — and a
question that has been answered once is a question that exists, and it
can be asked again on a day when he is frightened, or by somebody who is
not him.*

*He has just told me, as a gift, that I am a matter that has been
decided.*

"Thank you," said Tadayoshi.

"Don't thank me," said Nunome. "I'm seventy-four and I'm four years
behind and you do six hundred lines a day. It's not charity, it's
arithmetic," and he turned back round to his rule, which took a while,
and that would have been the whole of it.

---

Except that at about half past four Ide came through from the counter
with the day book under his arm and said, "Nunome. Month end's Tuesday."

"I know when month end is."

"I want the staffing on it by Monday because Sunaba's late twice running
and I've had a letter about it."

"You've had a letter about it because you *are* late twice running."

"Just do the staffing," said Ide, and went back through.

---

Tadayoshi sat perfectly still at the corner of the desk.

"Nunome-san."

"Mm."

"What is the staffing."

"Return," said Nunome, without looking up. "Everything goes up at month
end. Tonnage, dues, exemptions — " he snorted — "arrivals, and the
staffing, which is who's in the building and what we pay them, because
they'll not have anybody on a wage they haven't been told about."

"Who is it sent to?"

"Kiri."

"To whom in Kiri?"

"How should I know," said Nunome. "A room."

---

Tadayoshi looked at the pen in front of him.

*I have been in this building for fifteen days.*

*I am on nine a week and my dinner, which is a wage, which means I am a
line.*

*And the line has a name on it.*

He went back and got it exactly, because it mattered and because he could.

*Eleventh of the fourth month. He said "name" and I said "Ruri
Tadayoshi" and he wrote it in the day book at his elbow with his left
hand and asked me if I could count.*

*I gave him my name.*

in it, and closed the flap, and did not look at it, because there were
four people in that riverbed and he had to know where they were before he
did anything else at all.

---

He got up.

His left shoulder did something on the way and he made a noise and had to
wait, and then went on getting up, and stood in the middle of the channel
with his hands hanging.

His hands were red to about the second knuckle and the backs of them were
cut in about eleven places in short straight lines, and he looked at that
and worked out where the lines had come from and did not put it into
words.

The tin was eight feet away on its side with nothing in it.

The light was wrong. It had been about eleven in the morning and it was
not about eleven in the morning; the shadow of the landward bank was out
across the channel and had two hours in it.

---

*Two hours.*

*Or eleven minutes and the sun does not care what I think.*

*Do not guess. Go and get it.*

He went and got it.

---

The riverbed was full of his work.

He saw that from the middle of the channel and it stopped him for about
four seconds, which was as long as he let anything stop him for the next
two hours: sixty yards of grit shoulder with points standing up out of
it, everywhere, in no order at all — not in fences; there were no fences
after the first four — just points, singly and in patches and in one
place a stand of about two hundred of them together like a thing growing.

*I did that.*

*There is no other explanation available and I am not going to spend any
time on the ones that are not available.*

He crouched at the edge of the nearest patch and ran his thumb up one.

*Two courses. Grain in it.*

*Of course there is grain in it. I have put grain in every piece I have
made for two years and I put grain in these and I was not there.*

He stood up.

*And that is going to be the only funny thing in the whole of this and I
am going to think about it at three in the morning for about forty
years.*

---

Then he did it properly, because the ground was the only witness and the
ground was made of the one substance on earth he could read.

He started at the channel and worked outward, and it took him about two
hours, and he did not hurry it, and twice he had to stop and go and sit
down on the bank for a while and then came back.

---

**THE FOURTH ONE. The one whose name he never got.**

He was four feet from where Tadayoshi had woken up.

He was on his back on the channel stones with his knees up, which is not
a position a person arranges, and his right hand was still open, and it
had been on Tadayoshi's wrist about — about however long ago it had been.

There was one point in him. One.

It had gone in under the jaw on the left side and it was about the length
of a little finger and it was tapered, and there was grain in it, and
Tadayoshi crouched beside a boy of thirteen for a long time and did not
touch it.

*He was on my back.*

*He had my right wrist above the joint from behind and there was a knee
across my shoulder blades and I could not get air and I could not get a
hand.*

*And then something in me got up.*

*And this is four feet from where I woke.*

---

**HOKUTO.** Thirty-one yards, up the shingle, towards the road.

He had gone thirty-one yards on a foot with a hole through it.

Tadayoshi measured it because measuring it was the only thing he had.
He paced it out from the place in the channel where the boot-print and
the mess of it said Hokuto had gone down — and there was a *track*, a
dragged, one-sided track through the grit going up the shoulder, thirty-
one yards of it, made by somebody hauling himself with his hands and one
knee.

At the end of it there were points.

Not one. He did not count them and then he made himself go back and count
them and there were nine.

*He was crawling.*

*He had been crawling for thirty-one yards and he was going towards the
road and there is nothing on that road, there is not a house within four
miles, he was not going anywhere, he was just going away from the middle
of the bed.*

*And he was face down and going away and I put nine of them into him.*

He stood at the top of the shoulder with his hands at his sides.

*Enter it.*

*He could not have hurt me. Not at thirty-one yards, not on that foot,
not with his back to me and both hands on the ground.*

*There is no version in which that was a fight.*

---

**THE THIRD ONE.** Fifty-four yards, at the landward bank.

Nothing in his hands. That was the thing Tadayoshi found and could not
get past.

He looked for it for eleven minutes. He walked the whole of the ground
between the channel and the bank in a grid the way you walk a beach for a
dropped thing, and he found it eventually about nine yards back the way
the boy had come, lying flat in the grit where somebody had dropped it
and gone on.

*He put it down.*

*He put it down and he ran, and he got another nine yards after he put it
down, and I did not stop when he put it down.*

---

**MIYO.** Ninety yards.

She was in the road.

She had got out of the riverbed and up the seaward bank and across the
verge and she was in the middle of the coast road, ninety yards from
where the fence had been, and her feet were what they were from coming
through eleven feet of standing points to get to somebody who was
screaming.

Tadayoshi stood on the road beside her for a long time.

*Ninety yards.*

*Say she was going as fast as a person goes on those feet. Say six or
seven a minute at the outside, and she had a start on me because she was
already running.*

*Ninety yards.*

*That is not four seconds. That is not a thing that happens because
somebody has hold of me and I cannot breathe.*

*That is a minute at least. That is a minute of me going up a bank and
across a verge after somebody who was not coming back.*

He knelt down and did what he had done for the other three, which was to
count.

There were three.

*Three, and one of them is at the back of the knee, and you do not put a
thing at the back of a knee to stop somebody hurting you.*

*You put it there to stop them going.*

---

He sat down on the verge.

He sat there for about twenty minutes and did not do anything at all, and
then he got up and went back down into the bed and finished it, because
there was one thing left and it was the only one that mattered and he had
been putting it off for two hours and had known he was putting it off.

---

*The fourth one.*

*The one at the channel. The one with his hand still open.*

He crouched by him again.

*Order.*

*One. Where did the others die? Thirty-one yards, fifty-four yards,
ninety yards. All of them going away. All of them with their backs to the
middle. All of them after.*

*Two. So there is a middle. There is a moment where the fight stops being
a fight and becomes the other thing, and every one of those three is on
the far side of it, and I can prove it, and I could stand in a room and
prove it to anybody with a rule and a pair of eyes.*

*Three. And this one is not on the far side of it.*

*This one is four feet from where I woke up and he had my wrist and there
was a knee on my back and I could not get air.*

*So: was he the last thing that happened before, or the first thing that
happened after?*

He sat back on his heels in the channel.

---

*Test it.*

*If he was before: then something in me got up because I was being
suffocated by somebody who was holding me down, and it killed the person
holding me down, and everything after that is a separate horror and this
one is a boy fighting for air.*

*If he was after: then I got out from under him some other way — he
shifted, or the shoulder went, or he heard Hokuto and looked up — and he
was off me and standing or turning or getting up, and I put a tapered
point under his jaw at four feet.*

*Which is it.*

He looked at the ground.

*The stones are turned over across about eight feet. That is consistent
with both.*

*His knees are up. That is a person falling backwards, which is
consistent with both.*

*There is one point in him. One. In every other case there are nine and
three and — I have not counted the third one, I counted the third one,
there were five.*

*One.*

He held onto that for about a minute.

*One is the fight. One is a thing that happens because it was the only
thing available and then it is over. Nine is the other thing.*

*So one says: before.*

Then he put his hand flat on the stones.

*No. One says nothing at all.*

*One could be before. One could also be the FIRST one — the first of the
other thing, before it got going, before it had built to nine — and there
is nothing on this ground that separates a last act of a fight from a
first act of what came after, because they are the same size and they
happen four feet apart and they happen inside about a second.*

*And I was not there.*

---

He sat in a dry riverbed with four dead children in it and worked at it
for another half an hour, properly, in order, the way he worked at
everything, and did not get anywhere at all, and knew after the first ten
minutes that he was not going to and went on for twenty more because
stopping was worse.

*I can prove three.*

*I can prove three of them past any argument, and there is not one person
alive who would need convincing anyway, because they are thirty-one and
fifty-four and ninety yards away with their backs to me.*

*And the one I cannot prove is the only one that would tell me anything.*

*Because if he was before, then there is a thing in me that comes up when
I am being killed, and it is horrifying and it is not — it is not — it
started as a person defending himself.*

*And if he was after, then it did not start as anything. It started
because it started, and the boy holding me down was simply the nearest
one, and the whole of it — Hokuto crawling, the one who put his weapon
down, ninety yards of road — the whole of it is what I am, without a
reason at the front of it.*

*And the difference between those two is the difference between the rest
of my life and the rest of my life.*

*And the evidence is my own hands.*

---

He got up eventually because the light was going.

He did not bury them. He thought about it and he costed it and the
arithmetic was that four graves in a riverbed is eleven hours he did not
have and a fresh dug place is a signpost, and he stood there with the
arithmetic in front of him, correct, complete, checked twice.

*Somebody will have to go down and shut the doors.*

He had thought that on a bed frame in a house on the sixth course at two
in the morning and he had gone down and done it, all thirty-one of them,
and had been three hours about it.

He looked at the four of them.

Then he picked up the tin, and put it inside his coat, and walked out of
the riverbed onto the coast road and north, past a girl of thirteen lying
in it, and did not stop.

He got about four hundred yards.

Then he had to go off the road and be sick behind a wall, for a long
time, on his knees, with his forehead against the stone; and after that
he sat with his back to it in the last of the light and put his hand in
his left pocket and took out the thing he had been holding when he woke
up.

*Act 1, month 5, the eighth, dusk. Behind a wall on the coast road.
Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~2,120 words.*

It was a clasp.

He knew that before he had it properly out of the pocket, because his
thumb had been on it for however long he had been kneeling in the channel
and his hand already had the shape of it: a bar, and a tongue, and a
bevelled face about an inch across, with two lugs on the back for the
webbing to go through.

It had a piece of somebody's webbing still on it.

He got that off with the knife and did not look at what was on the strap,
and put the strap under a stone.

Then he held the clasp up in the last of the light with his back against
a wall four hundred yards from four dead children, and looked at it
properly.

---

*Crystal.*

*Which is not remarkable. The whole coast wears crystal. It has been sold
as gemstone for four hundred years and it is in rings and it is on
scabbards and there are eleven pieces of it in a corps kit and everybody
knows that and nobody thinks about it.*

*It is grey-blue and it is a plain bevel and there is no ornament on it
anywhere.*

*Two courses.*

He ran his thumb up the bevel, up and back, from the bar to the tongue.

*The note is at the wide end where the courses finish. It is exactly where
it should be. It is a commercial-grade fitting made by somebody
competent, and there are probably nine thousand of them in the Land of
Water, and I am sitting behind a wall being sick about a bead.*

*Put it down.*

He did not put it down.

He ran his thumb up it again.

---

*The taper is fuller a third of the way down.*

He stopped.

He sat behind the wall with his back against it and the light going and
did not move at all.

*The taper is fuller a third of the way down and it should not be,
because it is a bevel and a bevel is a straight run, and a bevel that is
fuller a third of the way down is a bevel that got MORE for a moment near
the start and then went back to what it was getting.*

*Which is the heel of a hand.*

*She pushes with the heel of the hand at the start and gets tired of
pushing.*

*It is on every drop she has ever made and it is the thing I spend four
minutes putting into mine, on purpose, every day, because I do not get
tired and she does and if I do not put it in then hers and mine do not
match.*

He got the thing about two inches from his face in the last of the light
and went up it with his thumb a fourth time and a fifth.

*It is her.*

*It is her, it is her hand, I know that hand better than I know my own
because I have been counterfeiting it since I was eight years old.*

---

He put it down on his knee and put both hands over his face and sat like
that for a while.

Then he took his hands down, because there was a very large amount of
work to do and no time at all in which to want to be doing it.

---

*Do it in order and do not skip anything because you are frightened of
where it goes.*

*ONE. Is it hers, or is it somebody with the same fault?*

*Anybody who pushes with the heel gets tired of pushing. That is not a
signature, that is an ordinary bad habit and there will be four hundred
makers in this country who have it.*

*So: is there anything else.*

He went over the whole of it again, slowly, with the light nearly gone
and mostly by feel.

*The note is a fraction low. Not wrong — low. It sits about a
sixteenth further down the bevel than it ought to for two courses on a
piece that shape, which is what happens when the second course is laid a
touch short at the finish, which is what happens when somebody is working
to a size and stops at the size instead of stopping at the piece.*

*She does that. She has always done that. Meno used to say it to her at
the table and she used to say Meno had not laid a course in twenty years
and Meno used to say that was not the point.*

*Two faults. Both hers. Both in the same object.*

*I would take that to a board.*

---

*TWO. And this is the one.*

He turned the clasp over on his palm.

*She has never made a fitting.*

*Not one. Not in twenty-two years. She makes drops and she makes beads
and she has made about eleven mounts in her life and she was not good at
them, and the whole of that household's output for as long as I have been
alive is small jewellery going in a tin to a counter at Ōura by weight
and grade.*

*Because that is what we are. We are a gemstone family. We sell to a shop
and the shop sells it on and what happens to it after that is nobody's
business and my father has never once asked.*

*This is not gemstone. This is a bar and a tongue and two lugs and a
bevelled face, and it is not decoration, it is a FASTENER — it is a piece
of equipment made to a pattern to hold a strap on a man, and the pattern
is the same one that is on the other three of them back there, and it
came out of a store with a number on it.*

*She has never in her life made one of these.*

*So somebody has put her in front of a bench with a different order on
it.*

---

He sat behind the wall with the clasp in his hand and got the whole of
it, all at once, and it went through him so hard that he had to put a
hand out on the ground.

*She is alive.*

*She is alive and she is at a bench and she is making military fittings
to a pattern, and she has been doing it long enough to have made this
one.*

*THREE. When.*

*The kit is new.*

*All four of them were new. The webbing was pale where it had not gone
grey, and the plate on the girl at the top had the packing crease still
in it, and one of them had a pouch on the wrong side and had been told
about it and had not moved it, and I stood in a riverbed at eleven
o'clock this morning and read every bit of that off them in about four
seconds and thought: they graduated in the spring.*

*Spring graduation. New issue. Eight weeks, ten at the outside.*

*This clasp came out of a store and onto a boy's chest inside the last
ten weeks.*

*And she was taken on the third of the fourth month, which is five weeks
ago.*

He sat very still.

*It is inside the window either way. It could have been made before —
sixth week of the issue cycle, and she was at Shioiri, except she has
never made a fitting in her life and there is no bench at Shioiri that
has ever had a pattern on it.*

*So: after.*

*She has been at a bench for five weeks.*

---

*FOUR.*

*And this is the one that is going to be the rest of it, so get it right
and do not decorate it.*

He turned the clasp over.

*I can read her.*

*I have just held a piece of her work that she made about a fortnight
ago, in a building I have never seen, in a country the size of this one,
and I have got two faults off it and a grade and a course count, and if
I had another one from the month before I could put them side by side and
tell you which was which.*

*The heel of the hand is fuller than it is on the ones in the tin. Not
much. I would have to have them both to swear to it and I do not have
them both, and I am putting that down as a thing I believe and not a
thing I know.*

*But I believe it.*

*She is more tired than she was.*

He sat behind a wall on the coast road with a fastener in his hand.

*I cannot find her. I have four days of walking and a station called Nezu
and an entry nine characters long, and a book that records what came in
and never where anything went.*

*But she is putting a thing into the world every day that has her in it,
and the things go into a store and out of a store and onto people, and
people walk about.*

*And I am the only person alive who can read it.*

---

He got the ledger out.

It was very nearly dark and he wrote it by feel, the way he had written
on a bed frame on the sixth course, about a quarter of an inch out of
true — and it came out in four lines and it took him about a minute, and
nothing fought him at all.

*8/5. Coast road south of Nezu. Clasp, corps issue, taken and kept.*

*Two courses. Heel-fault at a third. Note a sixteenth low. Her hand,
and I will say that in front of anybody.*

*She has never made a fitting. New issue, ten weeks at the outside.*

*She is alive and she is working and she is more tired than she was.*

He did not write anything about the riverbed.

He noticed that he had not, and sat with the pencil in his hand for about
eleven seconds deciding whether that was a failure, and decided it was
not, and that it would go in tomorrow when he could do it in order.

*(It went in eleven days later, on the road above Nezu, in nine lines,
and it took him two hours, and he did not get up afterwards for the rest
of that day.)*

---

Then he did the other thing.

He went back to the front of the ledger — to the first page, to the
second column, to the entry he had written at a bench at Shioiri on the
twenty-third of the second month with a lamp going down, which said
*nineteen went. Nobody says the names.*

And beside it, in the margin, he wrote a small *1*.

Then he turned the page and wrote a *2*, and a *3*, and went forward
through the whole of it in the dark: the four days of relearning where his
hand was; the frame; the case with its four passes; *she will say no*; the
bed being six foot four and the door five foot ten; *do not ask people,
find where it is written down*; Nezu; *find out what the book was for
before you look in it.*

Forty-one of them.

It took him about twenty minutes and he could not see any of it and he
did not need to, because he knew where every one of them was on the page.

He put the new one at the bottom and gave it a *42*.

He did not, at any point in the twenty minutes, say to himself why he was
doing it.

He would not have been able to. He was ten years and four months old and
he had killed four people that morning, three of them after it was over,
and he had no word at all for the thing he was making — and he went on
numbering the pages in the dark behind a wall on a coast road, carefully,
in order, the way you number a thing that is going to be handed to
somebody.

---

He put the ledger away.

Then he put the clasp in his right-hand pocket, on its own, with nothing
else in it.

The drop with the fault in it was in the left, with the grease tin, where
it had been for thirty-three days.

He sat there for a while with his hands flat on the outside of the coat,
one on each side, which is a thing he would go on doing for a very long
time without once noticing that he did it.

Then he got up and went north, in the dark, towards Nezu.
