# Chapter 7, Scene 6 — Allowed

*Act 1, month 7, the third to the nineteenth. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age
ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,840 words.*

---

They had meat twice in the first fortnight.

Not a bone with something on it. Meat, in a dish, that a person had gone
and got on purpose, twice, in fourteen days, and the second time Uzuki
sat at the table and looked at it for a second before she started, and
her mother saw her do it and said nothing, which was correct.

The roof did nothing when it rained. She had to sit up on the fourth
night to find that out, because she had spent four years listening for it
and could not stop, and she lay there in her own room in the dark with
the rain going hard on brick and tile and nothing happening anywhere in
the house at all, and it was one of the strangest things she had ever
been through.

There was oil. There had always been oil in the out column and there had
never been enough of it, and here there was a jar of it on a shelf and
nobody had drawn a line under anything.

---

The stones stopped.

Not because anybody said so. Iwasa's nine days were done on the
twenty-eighth of the sixth month anyway, and then it was the seventh
month, and there was nothing until the thinning, and Uzuki went up to the
top field on the fourth to see whether there was anything and there was
not, and she came back down.

And Kuriya's half a month went on arriving.

She did him on the sixth and the ninth and the thirteenth and the
sixteenth, twice a week in daylight, and if anybody asked she was doing
the step, and nobody ever asked, and the half a ryō came at the end of
the month the way he had said it would.

And she put it on the table and her mother put it in the box in the left
column and said "thank you, love," and that was all, and Uzuki went out
into the yard and stood there.

*It doesn't do anything.*

*That's what's wrong with it. It goes in the left column and the left
column is fine now and it just — sits there.*

*Three months I have been trying to make a number and the number is made
and nobody needs it.*

She stood in the yard for a while with her hands in her sleeves.

*So what am I for.*

She did not like that one at all. She put it down and went in.

---

Her mother laughed on the eighth.

Uzuki was outside with the mule and she heard it through the kitchen
window and she did not go in.

She could not have told anybody what it was about. It went on for a bit,
and Shimoda said something, and it went on a bit more, and then it went
quiet the way laughing does.

She stood in the yard with her hand flat on the mule's neck and did not
go in for about four minutes, and by the time she did go in they were
talking about a gate.

*Fine.*

*That's fine. That's just a thing that happens in a house.*

She got a bowl down.

*I have not heard that noise since the second month and I have just stood
in a yard for four minutes so that I would not walk into the middle of
it, and I did that on purpose, and I am not going to think about why.*

---

He showed her the fields on the eleventh.

She had asked, and she had asked for a reason which was that she wanted
to know what they actually had, because she had spent three months not
knowing what anybody actually had and it had nearly killed her.

He took it entirely seriously. That was the thing about him: you asked
him something and he answered the whole of it, at the length it took, and
he did not put a voice on for a child.

Two fields the other side of Iwasa's, and the far one was better than the
near one and he told her why — it lay to the south and it had had four
years of the muck off the shed and the near one had had two. He told her
what he got off each and what he had got off them the last three years,
which was not the same thing, and why the difference was mostly the
weather and partly him.

Then he showed her the ditch at the bottom of the far one and said his
father had put it in the wrong place and he had known it was in the wrong
place for eleven years and had never moved it because moving it was nine
days of work to save about a day and a half a year.

"So it stays wrong."

"It stays wrong," said Shimoda. "That's most of farming."

She thought about that for the whole walk back and she was still thinking
about it a year later.

---

And somewhere in the middle of all that she noticed that she had started
to like him, and she stopped in the lane and had it out.

*Right.*

*Say it properly.*

*I like him.*

*And I am not going to pretend I don't, because pretending you don't like
somebody is a thing you do so that you don't have to look at why you do,
and I would rather look at it.*

*Why.*

*Because he tells me things.*

*Because when I asked what a girl my size gets paid he said a fortieth
and told me why it was a fortieth, and it was the worst answer available
and he gave it to me straight in his own yard with my mother standing
there.*

*Because he told her what the mule was worth item by item and he could
have given her eight and she'd have taken it.*

*Because he gets up before he needs to and opens a door for an animal
that isn't his.*

*Because he said I could change my mind at the gate.*

She stood in the lane.

*And because my mother's stopped doing the thing with her hands.*

*And that's the real one and I don't like that it's the real one, because
it means I like him for what he's done to her and not for anything he's
done to me, and that's — *

She could not finish it and did not need to.

*Fine. I like him. I'm allowed.*

*Nobody has to be told and nobody's going to ask, and if Dad could see me
he'd — *

*He'd say get on with it.*

*He would. He'd say that exact thing. He'd say get on with it and then
he'd say something about the ditch.*

She got on with it.

---

The other thing happened on the sixteenth and it took about six seconds
and she had it at the time.

She came in from the water with the bucket and her sleeves up and her
hair wet round the front where it always went, and he was at the table
with the tally sticks out doing the seed, and he looked up when she came
in the way anybody looks up when a door goes.

And then he did not stop.

*Six.*

She had it before it was over. She always had it before it was over. She
put the bucket down on the board and turned round to the shelf and got
the cloth and it ran on its own the way it had run on its own since she
was seven.

*Six seconds.*

*Tazu is four and Tazu is the worst there is and Tazu is four.*

*The priest is one. Iwasa is one. The men in the wood on the north road
were the whole two hours and that was different, that was a job, that was
somebody deciding whether.*

*Six.*

Then he said, "Is that the good bucket or the one that leaks," and got
up and went out to the shed, and that was the whole of it.

---

She did the ninety that night at the brick and got twenty-nine and then
lay on her back in her own room and did the other thing.

*All right. Properly. Out loud, in your head, the whole way through, and
don't be soft with it either way.*

*Fact. He looked at me for six seconds on the sixteenth.*

*Fact. I do not know what he was looking at. The door is behind me. The
line off that table goes through where I was standing and out of the
door, and he had been staring at a stick with notches in it for an hour
and people go blank.*

*Fact. He looked away before I did.*

*Fact. He said something completely ordinary about a bucket about four
seconds later and then he went out.*

She turned it over.

*Against that.*

*Eleven weeks.*

*A fortieth, in his own yard, with the worst answer available, given to
me straight.*

*Six for the mule when he could have said eight.*

*Every morning, the top of the door.*

*Nine people in a yard and: you can change your mind at the gate.*

*My mother's hands.*

*And he's shown me two fields and told me the truth about a ditch his
father got wrong.*

She lay in the dark.

*So what am I saying? That a man who has done all of that is — because of
six seconds — because of a number in my head that I did not decide to
count and cannot stop counting?*

*I count everybody. I counted the priest at his wedding. I have got a
number in my head for a man who came to bury my father.*

**It's a habit. It isn't evidence.**

*And a girl who puts her mother's husband on that list is a girl who has
decided to be unhappy in a house that is finally all right.*

*I'm not doing that.*

*I've watched her be not-frightened for three weeks and I am not going to
take it off her over six seconds and a bucket.*

---

So she took him off.

She did it lying on her back on a mat in her own room on the night of the
sixteenth of the seventh month, deliberately, as an act, the way she did
everything: she went and got the entry and she struck it out.

And she felt, doing it, better than she had felt about herself in
months — grown-up, and fair, and like somebody who could look at a thing
properly and not be a frightened child about it.

*There.*

*That's what a person does. You look at the whole of it and you weigh it
and you don't let one stupid number decide.*

She went to sleep pleased with herself.

She had had that list since she was seven years old.

She had never once, in three years, taken anybody off it.
