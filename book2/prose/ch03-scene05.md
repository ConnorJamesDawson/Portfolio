# Chapter 3, Scene 5 — Do You Say Otherwise

*Act 1, month 4, the fifth. A determination room, Kirigakure. Ruri
Mikage, age thirty-four. POV: Mikage. ~2,860 words.*

---

The table was elm and it had been got cheap.

She knew that from about four feet away and she had known it since they
came in, and she had spent a good part of the last hour and a half with
her hands flat on the edge of it finding out the rest: that it had been
made by somebody in a hurry out of a board that should have been left
another year, that the top had cupped along the grain and been planed
flat twice to correct it and would cup again, and that whoever had put
the moulding on had run the plane the wrong way at the near left corner
and torn it, and had not gone back.

There was a great deal you could do with that.

You could put your thumb on the tear and follow it round and come back
and do it again. You could get the whole length of the near edge into
your hands and know it by the time they got to the fourth name. Elm had
a smell if you warmed it and if you sat long enough your hands warmed
it.

She had been doing that for an hour and a half.

She had not looked at the far end of the room for about forty minutes,
because the far end of the room was where Koishi was sitting on Hisui's
lap with her face in Hisui's coat, and there was a limit to the number of
times a woman can look at a thing and go on being able to hold a table.

---

There were three of them on the other side.

One with the register, who did the reading. One with the ledger and the
pen, who wrote. And one at the end who had a chair and did not use it,
and who had said perhaps eleven words since they came in and every one of
them had moved something.

Nobody had been unkind.

That was the thing she could not get round. She had come into that room
with her hands shaking and something in her chest going like a bird, and
in an hour and a half not one person on the other side of that elm table
had raised their voice or said anything cruel or looked at any of them
with anything except the mild, unfocused patience of people getting
through a list.

The one with the register had a cold. He kept a cloth in his sleeve and
used it and put it back, and once he said *excuse me* to the room, and
Meno had said *bless you*, out of seventy-one years of habit, and there
had been a moment where nobody quite knew what to do.

---

"Ruri Hisui," said the one with the register. "Born in the house. Assessed
in her eighth year. Negative."

The pen went.

"Ruri Koishi. Born in the house. Under age. Presentation due in the eighth
month, two years."

The pen went.

*They are doing the whole family,* thought Mikage, *and they have not
asked a single question about the trade.*

She had been waiting for it since the boat. She had had a night and a day
and another night to have it in, and she had had the whole of the
crossing to get it in order: that they had come for the work, that they
would want to know about the work, that they would ask her how much and
how fast and whether anybody helped her, and that everything after that
depended on how she answered.

They had not asked.

They had asked for names, and years of birth, and assessments.

*It is not the trade. It is the blood. They have not asked me one thing
about a bench.*

And under that, where she had been keeping it flat with both hands since
the turn of the path on the second course:

*Seven.*

*I counted seven on the path. There were seven on the boat and there are
seven in this room, and nobody at this table has said a number out loud
yet, and I do not know whether they have got a number.*

---

"Ruri Akagane. Born in the house. Assessed in his eighth year. Negative."

Akagane said, "Yes."

He said it because he had been saying yes to everything, quietly,
whenever a thing about him was read out, and he had done it four times
now and nobody had asked him to and nobody had told him to stop.

The pen went.

"Ruri Iwao. Born in the house. Son of Ruri Mikage and Ruri Tadaharu.
Assessed in his eighth year. Negative."

The one with the register looked up.

"Is that correct?"

---

It was not a real question.

She knew that, and everybody in the room knew it, and it had been asked
after every single entry for an hour and a half, and every single time it
had been answered by somebody saying *yes* or by nobody saying anything
at all, and the pen had gone.

Her son looked at her.

---

He was thirteen and he had grown three inches since the spring and he was
standing with his hands at his sides and his shoulders back the way he
stood when he was going to be charming at somebody, and he was white to
the lips.

He looked at her for about a second and a half.

And she understood — with her hands flat on the near edge of a cheap elm
table, in a room with three tired people on the other side of it — that
she was being asked something.

*He has got something.*

*He has been sitting on it since the boat or since this morning or since
the man said Hisui's name, and he has got something, and he is asking me
whether he can.*

She had a second and a half.

She did not use it to think. There was no thinking in it at all; it was
the same thing that had happened at eight years old on the second course
when Koishi went off the wall and she had been across the path and under
her before she knew she had moved — the same, only inside out, because
what her body did was *nothing*.

She did not shake her head.

She did not move her hands off the edge of the table.

She let a second and a half go past, and looked back at him, and did not
do one single thing.

And he took it.

---

"That one's wrong," said Iwao.

---

The room did not change very much.

The one with the register said, "Which part," in the voice of a man who
has found an error in a year and is going to have to go back through it,
and the pen stopped, and the one at the end who had a chair and did not
use it turned his head about four inches.

"The parents," said Iwao.

"You are not Ruri Mikage's son."

"No."

"Then whose."

---

She watched him do it.

She had watched him do it at that table with the money on it and she had
watched him do it to his father on a road, and she had never once been
able to say afterwards what any of it had consisted of, and she watched
him do it now with her hands flat on the elm and her heart going and she
saw, for the first time in his life, **the working.**

Because he was frightened. That was what did it. She had never seen him
frightened while he was doing it, and frightened slowed him down, and
slowed down you could see him build.

He said: "My father's Ruri Tadaharu, that's right, that bit's right." A
breath. "My mother isn't."

"Then who is your mother."

"I don't know her name."

And then — she saw him get it — "I was three."

*There it is.*

*He has just given them a reason not to be able to check, and he has
given it to them as a thing about himself and not as a thing about the
question, and there is not one person in this room who will hear it as an
answer to the question it is an answer to.*

"You were told this."

"I was told this by my great-aunt when I was nine and she told me not to
say it and she's sitting over there and she'll tell you I'm not to say
it." He did not look at Meno. "It's why I got assessed and came back
negative and my brother didn't. It's why — "

He stopped.

He stopped exactly where he needed to stop, on the edge of a thing he was
not going to be able to carry, and the one with the register said,
"Your brother," and wrote something, and Iwao said, "The little one,"
and that was that, and the thing that had nearly happened did not happen.

*He is going to be all right,* thought Mikage. *God forgive me. He is
going to be all right.*

---

"Name," said the one with the pen.

"What?"

"The father. Your father is Ruri Tadaharu of the household. If you are
not of the household you have a name, boy, and it is not Ruri. What is
it."

Iwao stood in the middle of the floor.

She watched it go through him. She watched it arrive at him that a
person who is not of a household has to be of something, that the whole
of it fell over in the next four seconds if he did not have a name, and
that he did not have one — and she watched his eyes go somewhere over the
clerk's shoulder, at a wall, at nothing, the way they went when he was
about to be funny at a dinner table.

"Kenji," said Iwao.

The pen went.

---

*Kenji.*

*Kanaya's man. The counter three doors up from the bridge at Ōura. He is
about forty-five and he has a glass on a chain that he does not use and
he made a face at the eleventh drop of the fourth dozen in the eighth
month.*

*And my son did the face at my table, and every one of us laughed at it,
and Tadaharu did it back, and Koishi fell off the bench.*

*He has reached into a room in our house and taken the first name off the
top and it is a man who sells beads.*

She sat with her hands on the elm.

*I am the only person alive who knows that.*

*Tadaharu was there and Tadaharu will not put it together, because
Tadaharu has been in that shop four hundred times and to him it is just a
name. Meno was asleep. Koishi is six.*

*It is me. It is only me. And in about four minutes it will not be
anybody.*

---

"Ruri Mikage," said the one at the end, who had a chair and did not use
it.

She looked up.

He was not looking at her particularly. He had one hand on the back of
the chair and he was looking at the register, and he said it the way you
say a thing you have said several hundred times and expect to say several
hundred more:

"Do you say otherwise?"

---

Four seconds.

She had them afterwards for the rest of her life, and she had them
separately, and she could not put them together into anything.

*One.*

*If I say yes, he is a Ruri, and there are eight names on this list
instead of seven and one of them is thirteen.*

*Two.*

*If I say nothing, they write it down. That is all that happens. A man
with a pen writes a word and it becomes a fact and there is no undoing a
thing that is written down in a room like this, and I will never in my
life be able to say I did not know that.*

*Three.*

*It does not save Hisui. It does not save Koishi. It does not save Meno
or Akagane or my husband, who is standing four feet from me and has not
said one word for ninety seconds. There is no version of this in which
saying it helps anybody at all.*

*Four.*

*And I want him to live.*

*That is the fourth one and it is the only one that is really in me, and
the other three are the ones I am going to use afterwards, and I would
like that written down somewhere too, and it will not be.*

---

She did not say anything.

The one at the end waited about the length of a breath — not impatiently;
he was reading something — and then he made a small movement with two
fingers, and the pen went.

"Recorded," said the one with the pen.

*That is my son,* thought Mikage, *and I have just put him down.*

*And I would do it again in four seconds.*

She got her thumb onto the torn moulding at the near left corner and
followed it round, and came back, and did it again.

---

It took eleven more minutes.

The one with the register read the determination off a form and did not
put any weight on any part of it, because there was no weight in it to
put.

"Ruri Mikage. Retained. Productive."

"Ruri Tadaharu. Ruri Meno. Ruri Hisui. Ruri Akagane. Ruri Koishi. No
requirement."

"Amagiri Kenji, minor. Released. Struck from the household."

Nobody in that room said what *no requirement* meant.

She looked for it. She listened for it through the whole eleven minutes
and it was not said, not once, in any form, by anybody — and the man with
the cold got his cloth out of his sleeve and used it and put it back, and
Hisui made a small noise and stopped it, and Koishi said, into Hisui's
coat, "Is it finished?" and Hisui said, "Nearly, love."

Her husband turned his head and looked at her.

That was all he did. He had been standing four feet away for an hour and
three quarters with his hands behind him and he had not spoken since they
gave his year of birth, and he turned his head and looked at her for
about two seconds and there was nothing in it that she could use and
nothing in it that was accusing her of anything, and then he looked
front again.

She never found out what it was.

---

They took her out first.

That was the part she had not costed and could not have. She had assumed
— she had had a night and a day and another night and she had assumed —
that whatever happened would happen to all of them in some order, and
that she would be there for it, and that there would be a corridor or a
yard or some number of minutes.

Two of them came round the end of the table and said, "This one," and
she was up and through the door before she had done anything at all with
her hands.

She got the corner of Hisui's coat, going past. That was it. That was the
whole of it: about four inches of wet wool between two fingers for less
than a second, and Hisui did not have a hand free because Hisui had
Koishi.

---

The room they put her in had a bench in it.

Not a cell. A room with a bench along one wall and a window with bars
that were not new, and three stools, and a shelf, and a tray on the
shelf.

She stood in the middle of it for a long time.

Then a man came in with a lamp and put it down and said, "Right, love,"
in the voice of somebody starting a shift, and took the tray off the
shelf and put it on the bench.

It was grit.

It was sieved and it was dry and it was not as good as the third bar down
at Shioiri, and she stood and looked at it, and the man said something
about how they would see what she could do and whether the eyes were up
to it, and went out, and left the lamp.

Mikage went and sat down at the bench.

She put both hands flat on it. It was pine, and it was better than the
elm.

Then she looked at her right hand for a while, at the nail of the
forefinger, where there was still a black line of grit under it from the
Tuesday, from the last thing she had made in her own house, which had
been four drops and a mount and had gone into the tin.

She got it out with her thumbnail.

Then she took three fingers of the new grit off the tray, and levelled it
off with the side of her thumb, and put her left hand under.
