# Chapter 5, Scene 3 — Nunome's Hand

*Act 1, month 4, the eleventh to the twenty-sixth. The harbour office,
Sunaba. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,480 words.*

---

They gave him the stove, the messages, and nine a week.

"And your dinner," said the counter man, whose name was Ide and who had
looked at him for about four seconds before saying yes, which Tadayoshi
did not understand at the time and understood entirely about a year
later, when he had seen enough ten-year-olds sleeping under things.

"Your dinner, and you'll sleep in the back with the coal, and if I find
you've had so much as a nail out of this building you'll be over that
rail on your head. Can you count?"

"Yes."

"Can you write?"

"Yes."

"Everybody says that," said Ide. "Nunome. Give him a pen."

---

Nunome was seventy-something and he had the end desk and he had been
behind for four years.

That was not an accident and it was not laziness; Tadayoshi worked it out
in about two days. The office took the day books from six stations up and
down that coast — the small posts, some of them manned and some of them
not, that sent their books in by boat every month — and one old man with a
rule was supposed to copy the whole of that coast into the bound annual
volumes, and had been supposed to for eleven years, and could do about
four lines a minute.

He gave Tadayoshi a pen and a sheet of scrap and said, "Copy that," and
went back to what he was doing.

Tadayoshi copied it.

He ruled it first, which took a moment, because the scrap was not ruled
and the thing he was copying had four columns in it; and he ruled the
fourth one narrow, because that was how it was done.

Then he did the eleven lines in about three minutes in the small exact
hand and squared it off and put it on the corner of the desk.

Nunome looked at it for a while.

"Where'd you learn to rule?"

"My great-aunt keeps the — " He stopped. "My great-aunt kept books."

"Did she."

"She had four columns and the fourth one was narrower because whoever
ruled the page first thought it would need less room."

Nunome made a noise in his nose.

"That's the only thing wrong with your hand," he said. "It's too good.
You'll slow yourself down being neat and by the eleventh year you'll have
a stoop. Sit there. Not that stool, that one's got a leg."

---

He copied for fifteen days.

He did the stove at six and the messages until about ten and then he sat
at the corner of Nunome's desk from ten until the light went, and he
copied the coast out of six day books into the bound volume for the
year, and by the fourth day he had stopped having to think about it and
by the eighth he was faster than Nunome and neither of them mentioned it.

*This is the best thing that has happened to me since the second month.*

*I should enter that somewhere. It is a finding.*

*I am sitting at a desk in a warm room with a pen doing four hundred
lines a day and my hands have closed up for the first time since the
tenth month, and I have not thought about the hill for*—

He looked at the clock, which was wrong, and then at the tide board,
which was not.

*—four hours and eleven minutes.*

*Enter it and do not enter what it feels like.*

---

The exemptions were on the twelfth day.

He had been waiting for them since the second and had made himself not
look, which was the hardest professional thing he had ever done, and the
reason he made himself not look was that he had worked out what happens
to a boy who opens a bound book at a particular month on his first
afternoon.

*Do it in order. Do it because it is the order. You are copying the
fourth month because the fourth month is what comes after the third
month, and Nunome will hand it to you and there will be nothing in the
room that has happened.*

So he did the first month, and the second month, and the third month, and
he did them at four hundred lines a day and he did not look ahead, and on
the twelfth day Nunome put the fourth month's bundle on the corner of the
desk and said, "That's Nezu and the two on the north shore in there as
well, they came in late," and went to the stove.

---

*Ex.*

There it was, on the second page, in a station clerk's hand that sloped
backwards: a craft in the day column, nothing in the master column,
nothing in the cargo column, and *Ex.* where the money went.

He copied it.

Then he did the rest of the month, all six stations, three hundred and
some lines, in order, without stopping, and he did not let his eye go
forward down a page once, and when he had finished he went back to the
top and counted.

*Three.*

*Three in the whole month. On the whole of this coast, six stations,
thirty days, three craft came in and did not pay.*

*The twelfth, at Sunaba. The nineteenth, at Sunaba.*

*And the third, at Nezu.*

---

He sat at the corner of an old man's desk in a warm room and put the pen
down and did not touch anything for about a minute.

*The third.*

*The boat went out past the bar at Shioiri at about twenty past five in
the afternoon of the third of the fourth month, and it went north, and
Nezu is north.*

*How far north.*

He got up and went to the wall where the tide boards were and the chart
was, and stood in front of the chart with his hands behind his back like
a man looking at a chart, and found it.

*Forty-one miles. About four days walking, more if the road goes round
the head of every inlet, and it does, because they all do.*

He went back and sat down.

*And that is the whole of it.*

*There is no master. There is no cargo. There is no destination column
and there never was one, because a harbour does not care where you are
going and has never been paid a penny to find out.*

*A craft came into Nezu on the third of the fourth month and did not pay
dues, and the entry is nine characters long, and I have been in this room
for fifteen days.*

---

He sat with it for the rest of the afternoon and did another hundred and
some lines, badly, and at about four he got hold of the other end of it.

*It is not nothing.*

*I have been treating this like a name. It is not a name. It is a
NEGATIVE and negatives are answers.*

*Before today I had: north, and a time, and forty miles of coast, and
nine boats with houses on them that were all the wrong size.*

*Now I have: it stopped at Nezu.*

*And a boat that stops has a reason. It stops for water, or for the tide,
or because somebody gets off, and it is forty-one miles from Shioiri
which is about right for the light going and about right for a small
craft that does not want to be in the open at night.*

*So it stopped at Nezu on the third and it did not stop at Sunaba on the
fourth or the fifth, because I have just done the fourth and the fifth
and there is nothing there.*

*Which means it went north again from Nezu, or it stayed at Nezu, or
whatever was on it stopped being on it at Nezu.*

*And every one of those is a thing you find out at Nezu.*

---

He copied until the light went.

That night he wrote it up in the back room by the coal, sitting on his
blanket with the ledger on his knees, and it came out in four lines and
took him about a minute, and it was the first entry since the fourth
month that had gone in without a fight.

*Nezu. Third of the fourth. One exempt entry, nine characters, no master,
no cargo.*

*Three exemptions on the whole coast in the month, so it is a rare thing
and it can be counted, and a thing that can be counted can be followed.*

*Forty-one miles. Four days.*

And then, in the narrow column, because that was where the true things
went:

*A dues book records what came in and never where anything went. Every
book records the thing somebody was paid to record and nothing else.*

*Find out what the book was FOR before you look in it.*
