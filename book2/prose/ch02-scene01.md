# Chapter 2, Scene 1 — The Ōura Road

*Act 1, month 3. The road from Shioiri to Ōura. Tadayoshi Ruri, age ten.
POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,790 words.*

---

They went up on the Tuesday with the mule and the tin and a bag of
Meno's flat cakes, which nobody wanted and everybody ate, and they were
past the head of the inlet before the light was properly up.

Tadayoshi had eleven of them in the tin.

Nineteen, if you counted the ones from the first four days, but he did
not count those, because the first four days had been him learning where
his own hand was and the work showed it. The eleven were the ones laid
after the fourth day, in the new frame, with the tapered fingers and the
dished palm, and he could pick every one of them out of four dozen by
eye at arm's length.

They had grain in them.

That was the thing he was carrying up the road like a stone in his boot.
They had grain in them because he had put grain in them, the way he
always did, laying the second course badly on purpose — and Akagane's
frame had spent nine weeks getting rid of exactly that, and the two
things had met in his hands and gone to war for four days, and the
eleven in the tin were what he had ended up with.

*They are not what he thinks they are.*

*He is going to ask me what the grain did. And what the grain did is that
I made it do the same thing it has always done, because I have a hand
that will not stop.*

---

"You're doing the face," said Iwao.

"I do not have a face."

"You've done it since the ferry stones. It's a small one and it goes on
for about a mile at a time." His brother walked backwards for a bit in
front of him, which he could do for a surprisingly long way. "Is it the
tin?"

"No."

"It's the tin."

"It is the road," said Tadayoshi. "I dislike the road."

"You dislike the road," Iwao agreed, and turned round, and let it go,
which was one of about four things about him that Tadayoshi had never
been able to account for. He always let it go. He never let anything go
because he had lost interest — he let it go the way you put a thing down
on a shelf you can reach.

---

Their father walked at the mule's head and said very little for the
first two hours, which was normal. Tadaharu on the road was a different
man from Tadaharu at the table: quieter, and he looked at things — at
fences, at what was in the fields, at how full the carts coming the other
way were. He had told Tadayoshi once that the road was the only place he
did any of his actual work, and that everything at the counter was just
the paying.

At about the ninth mile Iwao dropped back level with him and said,
"Da, I want to talk to you about the form."

*Here,* thought Tadayoshi.

He had been waiting for it since the ferry stones.

---

The form was a nomination.

Iwao was in his last year, and in his last year the Academy at Kiri put
about six names forward for a thing called early selection, which meant
you were seen by somebody from the corps at the winter trial instead of
waiting for the general graduation in the spring. Aoyagi did not teach
that year and did not have an opinion he was willing to give a
ten-year-old. What Tadayoshi knew was that Iwao had been in the top two
of that room since he was eleven, and that the top two did not always go
forward, and that the difference was a piece of paper with a parent's
name on it.

"No," said their father.

"You haven't heard it."

"I've heard it in my sleep. I heard it at breakfast on the ninth and I
heard it through a wall on the eleventh and I've heard it every day since
in one form or another, and the answer's your mother."

"The answer's my mother," Iwao agreed.

"So you know."

"I know."

And then he did not say anything for about a quarter of a mile, and
Tadayoshi, walking behind them with the bag of cakes, thought: *he has
just done something.*

---

*Record it.*

*He was told no. He agreed with the reason. Then he stopped talking, and
he stopped talking for four hundred yards, and he did it without going
sulky — he made a remark about the cart we passed and he laughed at Da's
answer and he was entirely pleasant, and the whole time he was not saying
the thing.*

*And now Da is the one carrying it.*

He watched the back of his father's head.

*He is going to bring it up himself.*

Four hundred yards later, his father said, "It's not that I think you'd
be bad at it."

"I know that."

"It's — " Tadaharu made a shape with one hand. "There's a difference
between a family that's got a boy at the Academy, which is nothing,
everybody's got a boy at the Academy, and a family that's got a *name*
at the winter trial. That's a list. You go on a list and then somebody
looks at where you're from."

"Yes," said Iwao.

"You agree with me."

"I agree with you completely. That's the whole point of it."

---

It took him two hours.

Tadayoshi walked eight feet behind and watched it happen and could not
afterwards have said how, and he tried — he tried the entire second half
of the day, and then again on the flat by the river, and then again in
bed at the inn.

Because he could do this. That was the thing. He could take a thing apart
and find the piece doing no work; he had done it to a five-seal sequence
in front of twenty-five people; he had done it to four hundred years of
copper. Watch a thing whole, then watch it again, then find the part you
can remove without the thing stopping.

He watched his brother for two hours and there was nothing to remove.

There was no sequence. That was what he kept arriving at and could not
make useful. Iwao did not have four moves he used in order. He said a
thing, and their father said a thing, and then Iwao said a *different*
thing — not the next one, a different one, built out of what their father
had just said — and it went on like that, thirty or forty exchanges, and
at no point did any of it look like a person trying to get something.

At one point Iwao argued the other side. He made their father's own case
better than their father had made it, and made it *warmly*, and their
father said, "Yes, exactly," and looked pleased, and Tadayoshi watched
his brother hand a man a stick and get handed it back.

At one point he made him laugh about Meno.

At one point — and this was the one Tadayoshi could not stop turning over
— he said, "You're not wrong about the list, though," and said nothing
after it, and their father filled the silence with the thing that beat
his own argument.

*He does not push. He arranges it so that the other person walks.*

*He has been doing that to me my entire life.*

---

They stopped for the flat cakes at the crossroads shrine and their father
signed it there on the step, with the pencil off the tin, on his knee.

"Your mother's going to skin me."

"I'll tell her."

"You will not tell her, you'll *manage* her, and I'll be standing behind
you nodding like an idiot, and we'll both know exactly what's going on
and she'll know as well." He held the paper out at arm's length and
looked at his own name on it with an expression Tadayoshi could not read
at all. "There. Winter trial."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. You didn't ask me for anything, that's the trick of
you." He folded it and gave it over. "You never do."

Iwao put it inside his shirt.

Then he sat down on the shrine step next to Tadayoshi and took a flat
cake off him without asking, and said, "Right, small. What's in the tin
that you're not saying?"

---

*Do not.*

"Eleven of mine are laid in the new frame."

"Akagane's thing."

"Akagane's thing." He watched a beetle on the step. "He says the grain
will change, and that Kanaya will notice about the third dozen and put
the grade up without saying why."

"And will it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

*Because I put the grain in by hand, and a frame cannot take out what a
hand puts in, and I have spent four days making sure of it.*

"Because it is a fine adjustment," said Tadayoshi, "and there are other
variables."

Iwao ate his flat cake.

"You know what I like about you," he said, "is you're a terrible liar
and you do it anyway. Most people who can't lie stop trying." He got up
and dusted the back of his trousers. "It's very brave. It's like watching
a man with no legs enter a race."

"I did not lie."

"No," his brother agreed. "You didn't, did you." And he went and took the
mule's head off their father, cheerfully, so the old man could walk with
his hands free for the last stretch.

---

They came over the top above Ōura at about four with the light going long
behind them.

It was the biggest place Tadayoshi had ever been, which he was aware said
more about him than about Ōura. A river, and a bridge with a customs post
nobody manned, and two hundred and some roofs going down to the water in
no order whatsoever, and smoke, and a road through the middle with
things on both sides of it.

He stopped for a second at the top, the way he had stopped at the top
every time since he was six.

*Six terraces at home. Eight people.*

*I can see about forty houses from here without moving my head, and I
know what is in one of them, and it is a counter with a man behind it who
will make a face at my brother's father over a bead.*

"Come on, small," said Iwao, from ten yards down. "You can count them
tomorrow."

Tadayoshi came on.

*I have already counted them,* he thought, and did not say, because there
was nothing in it for anybody. *There are forty-one.*
