# Chapter 7, Scene 7 — What Three People Paid

*Act 1, month 6, the thirteenth and fourteenth. Fukae. Tadayoshi Ruri,
age ten. POV: Tadayoshi. ~1,800 words.*

---

He stayed two days in Fukae because he had eighteen and Hanase was four,
and because a man had told him to buy a coat and he had six thousand ryō, and
because he had never in his life bought anything that was not food.

He made a list the night before. Of course he made a list.

*Coat. Boots — these are month 4 and the left one has gone at the seam
and I have been walking on it for six weeks. Blanket. Oil, for the boots
and the knife. Grit.*

*And a tin, because the tin has a dent in the lid and a dent in a lid is
a lid that will one day not come off.*

He wrote what he thought each would cost beside it, because that was what
you did with a list, and then he looked at the column of numbers he had
just invented out of nothing at all.

*I have made those up.*

*Every single one of those is a number I have put down because it looked
like the right size of number.*

He left them on the page and went to sleep.

---

The grit went perfectly.

There was a mason at the top of the town with a yard and a screen and
four grades in four bins, and Tadayoshi walked in and looked at the third
bin for about nine seconds and knew, and it was the first time since the
fifth of the fourth month that he had been completely certain of
anything.

"That one. How much for that one."

"How much do you want?"

"Fill this." He put the tin on the wall. "And I'll want to look at it wet
first."

The mason looked at him.

"You'll want to look at it wet."

"It's got a bit of feldspar in it. If it's the soft one it'll go to
nothing in a week and it'll go to nothing quicker wet, and if it's the
other one it won't." Tadayoshi picked up a pinch and looked at it in his
palm and put it back. "It's the other one. But I'll look at it wet."

There was a pause.

"Whose are you?" said the mason, and then, because Tadayoshi did not
answer, laughed and got a cup of water.

They did it wet. It was the other one. He paid a fortieth for a tin, and
the mason threw in a scoop of the fourth grade because he had enjoyed
himself, and told him the price of the third grade at Ōgi and at Hanase
in case he was going that way, unasked, in about four sentences.

*A fortieth.*

*And I knew before I asked that it was going to be about a fortieth,
because that is what my father paid for a tin of the third bar at Ōura
and that is what Meno paid before him, and it has been a fortieth for a
tin since before I was born, and I have known that all my life the way
you know your own name.*

He walked out of the mason's yard feeling extremely good about himself.

That lasted about two hours.

---

The coat was on a rail outside a place on the middle street with about
forty other things on it, and it was oiled, and it was small enough, and
it had a hood that was not just a bit of the collar pretending, and there
was nothing wrong with it that he could see.

He looked at it for a long time. He looked at the seams and he turned the
cuffs and he pulled at the shoulder to see whether the oil had gone hard
under the arm, which it had not, and he was thorough, and he was
thorough about all the right things.

He was thorough for about four minutes.

"How much is this one?"

"One and a half," said the woman.

"All right," said Tadayoshi, and paid her.

---

There was a moment, afterwards, that he did not understand until the
following day.

The woman took the money and she did not put it away immediately. She
stood with it in her hand and she opened her mouth and something happened
in her face, and Tadayoshi stood there with the coat over his arm
waiting for whatever she was going to say.

She did not say it.

She put the money in her apron and reached under the rail and got out a
pair of woollen mittens with a hole in one thumb and gave them to him.

"Here," she said. "Go on. They're no good to me."

"Thank you," said Tadayoshi.

He was pleased about the mittens for the whole of the rest of the day. He
put them in the list, under the coat, with a line beside them:

*Free. She gave me these for nothing.*

---

He bought the boots for one and the blanket for a quarter and the oil for
about nothing and a tin for a sixteenth, and it all went in the list with
the real prices written next to his invented ones, and he was down to
about three thousand ryō and the whole of it was the best-equipped he had been
since the fourth month.

He put the coat on that evening and it was warm and it was dry and it fit
and it was, without any question, the best thing he owned.

---

Ōtsuka saw it on the fourteenth.

He was going past the front of the Kiribata at about eight in the morning
and Ōtsuka was in the doorway doing something with a broom that he plainly
had no intention of finishing, and he looked at the coat.

"That's a good one."

"Yes."

"Where'd you get it?"

"Middle street. The rail outside the place with the shutters."

"Mm," said Ōtsuka. "What'd you give for it?"

"One and a half."

There was a pause of about a second and a half.

"Ah," said Ōtsuka.

And went back to the broom.

---

Tadayoshi stood on the street with his hands in his sleeves.

*Ah.*

*That's — *

*That is not a noise a man makes about a good price.*

He did not say anything for a bit. Ōtsuka moved about a pint of dust
approximately nine inches and then leaned the broom against the frame
and looked at the street.

"What should I have given?"

"Three-quarters. Maybe seven-eighths, it's a good one." Ōtsuka said it in
exactly the voice he had used about the step and the innkeeper's
husband — flat, unbothered, information rather than judgement. "She'll
have had it off a family up at the top end and she'll have given about a
half."

*Double.*

*I paid double.*

*I stood there for four minutes and I looked at the seams and I turned
the cuffs and I checked the oil under the arm and I did every single one
of those properly, and then I paid double, and the four minutes were the
part I was pleased with.*

And then, arriving underneath it, worse:

*The mittens.*

*She gave me the mittens because she felt bad.*

*She had it in her hand and she was going to say something and she didn't
say it, and then she gave me a pair of mittens with a hole in, and I
wrote down that they were free.*

He stood on the street outside the Kiribata at eight in the morning and
his ears went hot.

---

"Why didn't she say anything?"

"Because it's her stall," said Ōtsuka.

"But she—"

"She'd already said one and a half. If she says *no, give me
three-quarters*, she's a woman who asks double, out loud, in her own
street, in front of whoever's standing there." He picked the broom back
up. "So she gave you a pair of mittens instead and she'll feel about the
same as she'd have felt, and she's still a woman who says one and a
half."

*So she was not cheating me.*

*She asked a price and I paid it.*

*That is the whole of what happened. Nobody did anything to me at all. I
walked up to a rail and did the entire transaction correctly except for
one part, and the part I got wrong is a part I did not know was there.*

"I thought a price was a price," he said.

Ōtsuka stopped sweeping.

"Say that again."

"A price is a number," said Tadayoshi. "It's — it's like a weight. My
family sold by weight and grade to a shop and the shop had a book and the
book said what a grade was worth that month and there was nothing to
argue about, so I have never once in my life been in a room where a
number was a thing somebody had *decided*."

He heard himself say it and it was the longest thing he had said out loud
to anybody in about eight weeks.

Ōtsuka looked at him for a while.

"Right," he said. "Here it is, then, and it's the only one I'm giving you
for nothing, so learn it.

"Ask what three people paid before you pay anybody.

"Not what it's worth. Nobody knows what anything's worth. What did three
people give. Three, because two can both be wrong the same way." He
went back to the doorway. "And if you can't find three, walk off, come
back tomorrow, and see if the number's moved. If it moves it was never a
number."

---

He wrote it in the book that night, in a byre, four miles east of Fukae,
by the last of the light.

*14/6. A price is a decision and not a weight. Ask what three people
paid before you pay anybody. Three, because two can be wrong the same
way. If the number moves overnight it was never a number.*

He read it back and then sat there for a bit with the pen off the page.

*That is the first thing in this book that is not about her.*

He counted back, to be sure, because he was not going to say a thing like
that without checking it, and it took him about a minute and a half and
it came out the way he knew it would: forty-six entries and every single
one of them was a boat, or a book, or a hand, or a piece of crystal, or a
question about where she was.

*Forty-seven is about a coat.*

He looked at it.

Then he numbered it, and put the book inside his shirt, and lay down in
the straw in the coat he had paid double for, which was warm, and which
fit, and which was still the best thing he owned.
