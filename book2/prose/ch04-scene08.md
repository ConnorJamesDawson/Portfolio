# Chapter 4, Scene 8 — Ninety

*Act 1, month 4, the fourth and fifth — days nine and ten. The north
road. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki. ~1,710 words.*

---

She did ninety on the first evening and it took her two hours and it was
appalling.

She did it on her own left forearm, sitting against a milestone about
four miles out of Kayano with the cart beside her, and she got perhaps
twenty of them clean out of the ninety and the rest were bucket, or
nothing, or — worst — the trailing-off thing, where she went and did not
stop and found herself four seconds later still going at a quarter with
no idea when she had decided to.

Her arms were the arms of somebody who had been hit with a plank.

She wrote it on the back of the chit by feel, in the dark:

*90. Own left arm. 20 good. Arms bad.*

Then she went to sleep against the milestone under the sacking with the
cloth of food untouched except for the bread, because Chiyo had put four
days in it and she was going to do it in three.

---

Day ten she walked twenty-two miles.

She was not supposed to be able to do that and she knew it while she was
doing it and she did it anyway, and the reason was simple and she would
not have said it out loud to anybody: she had something in her hands now
and every mile between here and Nakadō was a mile it was not being used.

She did the ninety in the middle of the day instead of the evening,
sitting on a wall, because she had worked out by then that doing it at
the end of twenty-two miles was going to be the difference between ninety
and forty.

*22 good.*

Two more than yesterday.

She sat and looked at the two numbers on the chit for a while, which
looked like nothing at all, and then remembered a man saying *in four
months you'll be certain you've got worse, and you won't have,* and put
it away.

---

She met Ine at the Sanzu fork at about four in the afternoon.

It was not a coincidence and she worked that out afterwards: it was a
slate cart on a slate road, and slate goes one way and money comes back
the other, and a woman doing that route does the Sanzu fork about twice a
week for thirty years.

"You," said Ine.

"Hello."

"Did you get it?"

"Yes."

Ine stopped the horse.

---

"Go on then."

"He gave me three and a half hours," said Uzuki. "He said I've got about
a fifth of it and I'll have it by the autumn if I do ninety a day."

"Ninety what?"

"That's — it's hard to explain."

"Try."

So Uzuki tried, standing in the road at the Sanzu fork, and got about
four sentences in before she understood that she was describing something
that had a private language in it now, which she had had for one day, and
that this was going to happen to her for the rest of her life.

"Little," she said, in the end. "Stop. Wait. Ninety times."

"And that does something."

"It takes the edge off. It doesn't fix anything." She heard herself say
it in Anzai's exact cadence and did not like that she had. "It'd do the
nights."

Ine looked at her for a moment.

"Tarō," she said, "come and get in the road."

---

"It's not Tarō," said Uzuki. "It's you."

Ine's face did something.

"What?"

"It's the right one," said Uzuki. "You put the brake on with your right
and you flick the reins with your right and I watched you for eleven
miles in the wood and you kept doing this — " she showed her, a small
rolling shrug — "about every four minutes. Tarō's fine. Tarō's fifteen."

"Tarō is *sixteen*," said Tarō.

"Get out of it," said his mother, and got down off the cart.

---

She sat on the bank and put her sleeve up and Uzuki put both hands on and
shut her eyes, and did the first bit, which was the bit that was free.

"Left's warmer. Right's got a — it's not a cord, it's flatter than a
cord, it's like a — "

"It's a plate," said Ine. "That's what it feels like. It's been like that
since I was about thirty."

"Where does it hurt?"

"Neck."

"That's not where it is."

"I know it's not where it is," said Ine. "Nothing's where it is. Go on."

---

Little. Stop. Wait.

It was much harder on somebody else than on her own arm and she had not
expected that; there was a whole extra thing to do, which was keeping
track of a person who was not you, and about the twentieth one she lost
where she was and had to start the count again and Ine said, "You've gone
quiet," and Uzuki said, "Don't talk to me," and Ine, to her eternal
credit, did not.

At about forty her arms went.

She put her elbows on her knees, which was the thing Anzai had said
without looking up, and it worked, and she went on.

At about sixty she was certain it was not working.

*That's the one. He said that one. That's the sixtieth and it's the one
where you want to push.*

She did not push.

At seventy-one — which was where he had stopped her, and which she had
not chosen, and which she noticed with a distant sort of horror was now
simply the number — she took her hands off.

---

Ine sat on the bank.

Then she rolled the right shoulder, and stopped, and rolled it again, and
said, "Well now."

"It's not gone."

"No."

"It's not going to have gone. It'll come back in about four hours."

"Aye," said Ine, and got up, and put her hand on the small of her own
back and stretched, and stood there in the road looking at nothing for a
second.

"Thirty years that's been like that," she said.

Uzuki sat on the bank with her hands in her lap and her arms shaking and
found that she could not say anything at all.

---

Ine tried to give her money.

It was eleven thousand ryō and Uzuki looked at it in the woman's palm and had
one of the strangest moments of her life, because she wanted it — she wanted
it the way she had wanted the barley in Kanmuri lane — and what came out of
her mouth was:

"No."

"Don't be — "

"No. Not for that." She got up off the bank. "I'll take food. I'd take
food and be glad of it. Not money for that."

"Why not?"

*Because a man gave it to me for nothing four days ago and I couldn't buy
it and I still can't and if I sell it at a fork in the road four days
later I don't know what I've got.*

"Because I've only had it a day," said Uzuki, "and I don't know what it's
worth yet, and I'd rather not find out by getting it wrong."

Ine put the money away.

"That," she said, "is a much better answer than you know it is," and gave
her a end of ham and half a loaf and a thing wrapped in leaves, and made
her take all three.

---

She walked on from the fork at about five.

She got about a mile and a half and then she stopped in the middle of the
road.

---

*Little. Stop. Wait.*

*And stop is a thing you do. It's a separate action and it takes as much
as the going does.*

*You put it in late. Not at the set. At the going. You're paying before
you've decided.*

She stood in the road with the cart handle in her hands.

*It's the same.*

*It's the same thing. It is exactly and completely the same thing and it
is not like it, it IS it — hold it, don't spend it, put out a small one,
stop on purpose — that's the eighth count. That's the eighth count of the
third form and I have been doing it on a flat bit of ground behind our
house since I was seven.*

*Anzai said somebody's taught you to hold back, and I said my father, and
he said is he a medic, and I said he's a carrier, he does parcels, he
taught me sword forms.*

*And he said mm. And we went on to the next thing.*

*He didn't know. He said the words and neither of us —*

She stood in the road.

*Da taught me the palm work.*

*He taught me the whole of the hard part of it and he did it with a
wooden sword on a Sunday and he doesn't know he did and I've walked four
days and sat in a nettle bank and cried on a stranger's table to be given
the rest of it.*

*He's had it in him the entire time.*

*He's got about a hundred things in him like that and nobody's ever asked
him one single question about any of them, because he's a man with a
mule who mends chairs, and he taught me a medic's hands by accident,
next to a barrow, telling me not to go hard.*

---

She started to run.

She could not run properly because of the cart, so she ran with it, which
was a stupid and inefficient way to cover ground and which she did for
about two miles before her legs stopped agreeing.

Then she walked very fast in the dark for another six.

*Tell him.*

*That's the first thing. Not the palm — the first thing when I get in
that door is I'm telling him what he did, because he'll not believe it,
he'll do the thing with his eyebrows, and Ma will say we're both idiots.*

*Then I'll show him.*

*Then I'll do his night.*

She slept for four hours in a hedge because her legs made her, and was up
and moving before it was light, and she came over the last rise above
Nakadō at about eleven in the morning on the sixth of the fourth month,
which was the eleventh day.
