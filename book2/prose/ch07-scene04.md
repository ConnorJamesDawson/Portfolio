# Chapter 7, Scene 4 — No Stone By The Step

*Act 1, month 7, the second. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki.
~1,680 words.*

---

It took four journeys and Iwasa's cart for one of them, and the whole of
what they owned went up the lane in about three hours, and that was the
part nobody had warned her about.

*Three hours.*

*Fifteen years of two people and then me, and it's three hours and a cart
and a bit.*

She carried the box herself. Nobody asked her to and nobody stopped her,
and she went up the lane with it against her chest and put it on the
kitchen table in the new house before anything else went anywhere, which
she did not plan and which she thought about afterwards.

---

Shimoda carried the sword.

He took it down off the two pegs above the door in the back room with
both hands, the way you take down a thing you have decided in advance to
be careful with, and he looked at it for a second — at the red of it,
because it was very red, and it was the reddest thing in that house and
always had been — and then he tucked it under his arm and carried it up
the lane in the middle of the afternoon in front of the whole village
like a man carrying a rolled mat.

Uzuki watched him go out of the gate with it.

*Say something.*

She did not.

*Say something. That's Grandad's. Say: that's my grandad's, be careful
with it, don't put it in the shed.*

*Say anything.*

She stood in the emptying back room and did not say one single word
about it, and the reason arrived about an hour later while she was
sweeping a floor she was never going to see again:

*Because asking about it is saying it's mine.*

*And it isn't. It was never mine. It was Grandad's and then it was Dad's
and it was above a door for as long as there has been a door, and the one
thing anybody ever said to me about it was that it was not for me.*

*So if I ask, then I'm a girl asking for a sword.*

*And if I don't ask, it's just a thing that came up the lane with the
rest of it.*

She swept the floor.

*And I said nothing, which I never do, and I don't know why this one is
different.*

---

He hung it in the back room of the new house, above the door, on two
nails he put in himself, and he did it that evening without being asked
and without saying anything about it at all.

She saw it there when she went past.

*Right.*

*Same place. Different door.*

She did not go in and look at it.

---

The house was brick and it had four rooms and the roof did not do
anything when it rained, which she found out on the fourth night and had
to sit up for.

The kitchen was twice the old one. There was a proper range instead of
the thing her father had built out of somebody else's range in the year
she was born. There was a floor you could get clean.

The shed was brick as well, and the mule was in it, and the top of the
door was open, and at about six the next morning his head came over it
and looked at the kitchen window and did not find anybody, and at about
seven it came over again.

Uzuki was at the window the second time.

*You daft animal.*

She went out and stood with him for a bit before anybody was up.

---

And she had her own room.

She had never had a room. In the old house there had been the kitchen and
the back room and they had all three slept in the back room, her mother
and father on the one side and her on the mat under the window, and that
was the whole of the arrangement and had been since she was born and she
had never once thought about it.

Now there was a room with a door on it and a window and nothing in it but
her mat and the flour bag and the bokken, and the first night she lay in
it with the door shut and looked at the ceiling and was, without any
question at all, delighted.

*It's mine.*

*Nobody's in it. If I shut the door the whole of what's in here is mine
and there isn't anybody in it and I can do the ninety at the wall and not
be doing it four feet from my mother's head.*

Her mother said it twice.

She said it on the first evening — *she's got her own room, Ine* — and
then she said it again on the third day to Iwasa's wife at the water,
with Uzuki standing right there, in a voice Uzuki had not heard her use
about anything for a year: *she's got her own room now.*

And Uzuki stood at the water with the bucket and was pleased.

*She's pleased. She's telling people.*

*Let her tell people.*

---

The other thing about the room she noticed on the first night and thought
nothing of.

Brick does not do what wattle does. In the old house the wall between the
back room and the kitchen had been about an inch of daub over sticks and
you heard everything through it and it was the same as being in the room,
and so nobody had ever bothered.

Brick was different. Brick was solid and cold and you could put your hand
flat on it and there was nothing behind it.

And on the first night, lying on her mat with the door shut and the room
entirely hers, she could hear her mother and Shimoda talking through it.

Not the words. She could not get one word.

She could get the *shape* — the up and down of it, and which one of them
was going, and the gap where one stopped and the other started, and
somebody laughing once.

She lay and listened to the shape of it for about a minute out of
straightforward nosiness, and then felt slightly ashamed of herself, and
turned over, and went to sleep.

---

The flour bag was the actual problem and it took her two days.

There was no stone by the step. There was a step, and it was a proper cut
one, and it sat down onto the ground the way a proper cut step does, and
there was nothing under it and nothing beside it and nowhere in the whole
of that yard where a thing could go.

So she went round the house properly, in the way she went round things,
and she found four places inside about an hour.

*One. Under the mat. No — under the mat is where everything is. Under the
mat is the first place.*

*Two. In the thatch over the shed door, where the bundle's come loose.
That's good. Nobody would ever.*

*Three. Behind the beam in the back room. That's better than good. You
would have to know.*

*Four. The corner of my own room, bottom of the wall, where the render's
gone and the brick sits proud. It's loose. It comes out with two fingers
and there's about that much behind it.*

She sat on her heels in the corner and looked at the four of them.

*Two and three are the good ones.*

*Two is outside and three is a beam I can't reach without standing on
something.*

And then she found the thing she had actually been looking for, and it
came out of her sideways, and she went hot when it arrived.

*How long does it take to get it in the dark.*

She sat very still in the corner of her own room, in the middle of the
afternoon, with the sun coming in.

*Why is that the question.*

*It's the wrong question. The question is where would somebody look. And
I have not asked that once. I have been going round this house for an
hour asking how fast can I get to it and I did not notice I was doing
it.*

She looked at the loose brick.

*A hiding place you cannot get to in the dark in a hurry is a hiding
place for somebody who's going to be here.*

She got up and shut the door.

Then she pulled the brick out and put the flour bag behind it and put it
back, and stood up, and went to the door, and did it from the door — down
on one knee, two fingers, out, brick on the floor, bag in her hand — and
counted it.

Eleven times.

She got it down to about two and a half by the eighth, and stopped
bothering after eleven because it was not going to get faster, and put
the brick back, and stood in the middle of her own room with the flour
bag in her hand.

*Two and a half.*

*And out of that window is another four, because the catch is stiff and
I've done the catch.*

*So seven from the mat.*

She did not write that down anywhere. She had nowhere to write it that
was not the bag itself, and she was not going to put it on the bag.

*I'm not doing anything. I'm being sensible.*

*Everybody should know where their things are.*

She put the bag behind the brick and went to help with the water.

---

That night she lay in her own room with the door shut and looked at the
ceiling and did the ninety at the wall, at the brick, because it was the
coldest surface she could reach without getting up, and it was one
hundred per cent hers, and nobody was four feet away.

Twenty-nine good.

*Better.*

Through the wall, the shape of two people talking, going up and down.
Somebody laughed. It went quiet.

She turned over.
