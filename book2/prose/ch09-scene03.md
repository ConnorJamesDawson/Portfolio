# Chapter 9, Scene 3 — At The Bowl

*Act 1, month 9, the eleventh. Nakadō. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV:
Uzuki. ~1,730 words.*

---

Her mother said it on the tenth, at the water, in the ordinary voice she
used for the things that were on a list.

"That wants doing."

"I know."

"Tomorrow, then. It'll want the afternoon and there's nothing tomorrow
afternoon."

*Two months.*

*Last one was the ninth of the seventh month, and that was five days after
we came up here, and she did it in the new kitchen and neither of us said
anything about doing it in the new kitchen.*

---

Her father had done it about every two months since she was four and he
had never once said why.

She had asked him twice. She had asked him when she was small and he was
doing the bowl, and he had said *because* the first time, which is what
people say, and the second time he had put his hand on the top of her head
and looked at the water for a bit and said *because* again, and had gone on
with it, and she had never asked a third time, because she had worked out
at about six that he was not going to say and that it was costing him.

Her grandfather had done it before that. Her grandfather had done it to
her father, and Uzuki knew that in the way you know a thing that has been
said once in a kitchen and not explained.

And since the fourth month it had been her mother.

---

They did it at the bowl in the kitchen, in the afternoon, with the light
coming in from the yard side, because you cannot do it in the dark and you
cannot do it outside.

Her mother put the cloth over her shoulders and Uzuki sat on the floor in
front of the stool with her head at the right height, and her mother sat
on the stool behind her with the bowl on her knees.

That was how it was done. It had been how it was done for six years and
Uzuki had never once thought about it, and she thought about it that
afternoon, sitting on a kitchen floor with a cloth round her shoulders and
her mother's knees either side of her.

*She can't see my face.*

*And I can't see hers.*

*And it's forty minutes and neither of us can get up.*

---

The first part is the whole of it wet through, and that takes about nine
minutes and it is cold and it always has been.

Then the stuff out of the bowl, worked down from the crown, in handfuls,
and the smell of it, which was not a bad smell and was not a good one and
which Uzuki had smelled about thirty times in her life and would have
known blindfolded in a field.

Then the roots.

The roots were the part that took the time and the part her mother was
worse at than her father had been, and both of them knew it and neither of
them had ever said it. Her father had gone at the roots with the ends of
two fingers, a section at a time, working the parting about with his
thumb, and he had been quick and it had never gone patchy. Her mother went
at them the same way and slower and it went patchy anyway, about an inch
back from the front on the left, every single time.

*It'll be the left again.*

*It's always the left. It's because she's holding the bowl on her left
knee and she can't get her elbow round.*

*I could tell her that.*

She did not tell her that. She had not told her that in five months and
she could not have said why, and she thought about it for a while with her
head tipped forward and her mother's fingers on the back of her parting,
and got nowhere.

---

It came at about the twentieth minute.

Her mother's hands did not stop. That was the first thing about it and it
was the thing that made it possible: they went on doing the section they
were doing, at the same speed, and the voice came from about eight inches
behind Uzuki's right ear and it was completely level.

"I'd not blame you if—"

And stopped.

---

Uzuki sat on the kitchen floor with a cloth round her shoulders and did
not move.

*Don't.*

*Don't say anything. Don't turn round. Do not put a single word in that
gap.*

Her mother's hands went on. A section, and the thumb, and the next
section. About eleven seconds.

Then she said, "Tip forward a bit," and Uzuki tipped forward a bit.

---

She had three endings and she could not tell which.

She got all three of them inside about four seconds, sitting on the floor
with her eyes on the flags, and then she had them for the rest of that
afternoon and the whole of that night and for a very long time
afterwards.

*One. I'd not blame you if you went.*

*Which is her telling me to go. Which is her saying: I have looked at this
and there is nothing in it for you and you should get out.*

*And if it's that, then she has given up, and she has given up on her own
account and not on mine, and she is asking me to be the one who leaves so
that she does not have to be the one who threw me out.*

She sat very still.

*Two. I'd not blame you if you did something.*

*Which is worse. Which is a woman of forty asking a girl of ten to do a
thing she is not going to do herself, and doing it in a way that means she
never has to say it, so that afterwards she can say she never said it, and
she would be right, because she never said it.*

*And I have got a knife under my mat and she does not know that.*

*Or she does.*

*Three.*

She did not want the third one. She got it anyway, and it arrived last and
lay underneath the other two, and it was the one she could not put down.

*I'd not blame you if you hated me.*

*Which is not about doing anything at all. Which is just — a woman saying
sorry, in advance, for a thing that is going to go on happening, and
asking to be let off it.*

---

*Three.*

*And I cannot tell which.*

She sat on the floor with the smell of the bowl and her mother's fingers
going along the parting and worked at it and could not get it to come out.

*If I say "if what" then she has to finish it.*

*And whichever one it is, once it is said, it is said, and then it is a
thing in this house that has been said out loud, and then I have got to do
something about it.*

*And I do not know what I would do.*

*I do not know what I would do about any of the three. I have not got a
plan for going and I have not got a plan for the other one and I have not
got anything at all to say to the third one, because I do not hate her,
and I do not, and I have checked, and it is worse than hating her.*

**So I am not going to make her finish it, and I am telling myself that is
for her, and about half of it is for me.**

---

She said nothing.

She sat on a kitchen floor in the afternoon with a cloth round her
shoulders and let a sentence lie in the middle of a room, and her mother
did the roots, and the section, and the thumb, and the next section, and
it took another twenty minutes.

At the end of it her mother said, "Head back," and Uzuki put her head
back, and they did the rinse, which is four bowls and takes about six
minutes and which is the part where you have to hold still and shut your
eyes because it runs.

And she sat there with her eyes shut and her head back and her mother's
hand flat on her forehead to keep the water off her face — which is a
thing her mother had done thirty times and her father had done before
that, and which is the only time in a year that anybody's hand is on your
face — and she did not say anything then either.

---

Afterwards her mother wrung the cloth out and looked at the front on the
left.

"It's gone again."

"It always goes."

"It never went for your father."

"He had littler hands," said Uzuki.

Her mother looked at her for a second.

Then she said, "There's the end of that barley to do," and went and did
the end of the barley, and that was the whole of it, and neither of them
said one word about the other thing that afternoon or ever.

---

Her mother's hands were stained for three days.

It always did that. It went into the sides of the nails and the creases
across the knuckles and it took three washes and about seventy-two hours
and there was nothing you could do about it, and every two months for six
years there had been somebody in that house with brown hands for three
days.

Uzuki looked at them on the twelfth, at the table.

She looked at them on the thirteenth, at the water.

She looked at them on the fourteenth, when she came back down from
Kuriya's and her mother was doing something at the range, and they were
nearly gone by then, just the nail beds and one crease on the right
forefinger.

She did not know that was the last time she would see them, because there
was no way of knowing it, and there was nothing about the eleventh of the
ninth month that was any different from the ninth of the seventh or the
one before that.

It went in the bag that night with everything else, in the only place
there was, which was the back, because the four columns were full.

*11th. Done.*
