# Chapter 2, Scene 2 — Four Things

*Act 1, month 3. Nakadō, Tazu's shed. Nozaki Uzuki, age ten. POV: Uzuki.
~1,620 words.*

---

"I've got four things," said Uzuki.

Tazu did not look up. He was doing something to a tray of small dry
somethings with the back of a spoon, and the shed smelled the way it
always smelled, which was of about nine plants at once and one of them
gone off.

"Four."

"Four things I want to ask you and they're all about the same thing and
if you tell me to go home I'll come back tomorrow."

"You'll come back tomorrow anyway," said Tazu. "Shut the door, you're
letting the wet in."

She shut the door.

---

She had spent eleven days getting the four things into an order.

That was the part nobody would have believed if she had told them, and
she had not told them, because there was nobody in that house she could
have said it to without it turning into a conversation about not getting
her hopes up. Eleven days. She had had them in a different order four
times. She had had six things at one point and then worked out that two
of them were the same thing wearing a hat.

"One," said Uzuki. "When you said a medic could teach it. Did you mean
that, or was it a thing to say on the way out of a house."

Tazu put the spoon down.

He looked at her for a moment with an expression she had not seen on him
before — not surprise exactly; more the look of a man who has been handed
a tool he did not know was in the room.

"That's a very rude question."

"Yes."

"It's also a good one." He wiped his hands on the front of himself.
"Sit on the box. Not that box, the other one, that one's got something
in it."

She sat on the other box.

---

"I meant it," said Tazu. "And it was a thing to say on the way out of a
house. Both. That's how most true things get said, in my experience —
sideways, at the door, when the paying part's over."

"So it can be taught."

"The palm work can be taught. Some of it. To some people."

"Which people?"

"People with control." He sat down on the stool. "Now that's the bit
everybody gets wrong and I'm going to be careful about it, because I've
watched a lot of people be got wrong about it. It isn't strength, it
isn't a bloodline, and it isn't being clever. It's whether you can put a
small amount of something exactly where you meant to and stop when you
said you'd stop."

Uzuki thought about the eighth count of the third form.

She thought about *you're paying before you've decided*, and about a
year of going hard, and about the eighteenth try, which had felt like
nothing at all.

"Say the second thing," said Tazu.

---

"Two. How long."

"An afternoon for the bones of it. A year for it to be worth anything.
Ten to be a medic, and you won't be a medic, so put that one down."

"I'm not trying to be a medic."

"No," said Tazu. "You're not, are you."

"Three." She had her hands flat on her knees. "What would I be able to
do. After the afternoon. Exactly."

---

He took his time over that one.

He got up and went to the shelf and moved two jars that did not need
moving, and came back, and she let him do it, because she had learned
that from her father: when a person is going to give you the true
version, they usually walk about first.

"You'd be able to take the edge off," said Tazu. "That's the honest
answer and it's smaller than you want. A hand on a chest, and the tight
part goes slack for a bit, and the coughing that's got hold and won't let
go — that stops, sometimes, for an hour or two, because half of that is
the muscle panicking and you can talk muscle out of things.

"You'd be able to do the nights. That's not nothing, girl, don't let your
face do that. The nights are where it gets won and lost. A man who sleeps
four hours is a man with a chance and a man who sleeps none isn't."

"And the thing itself."

"No."

"Not at all?"

"Not at all," said Tazu. "Not in an afternoon and not in ten years and
not with the best of them standing over you. What's in your father is in
the deep part and it has a shape, and shifting a shape is a different
trade from taking the edge off, and I'd be lying to you if I said the
first one was a small version of the second."

Uzuki sat on the box with her hands flat on her knees.

"All right," she said.

"You're not crying about it."

"I did that on the ninth."

---

"Four," she said. "This is the actual one."

"I know. I've been waiting for it."

"Kayano's three days. A medic at Kayano is a person with a house and a
door and other people wanting them." She had gone through this at night
about forty times and it came out of her flat and fast. "Why would one
of them sit down for an afternoon with somebody who can't pay them?"

Tazu laughed.

It was not a kind laugh and it was not an unkind one; it was the noise a
man makes when a child asks the question the adults did not.

"Because it's quicker," he said.

---

"Quicker than what?"

"Than doing it themselves." He leaned forward with his elbows on his
knees. "Listen. A medic worth the name is the busiest person in whatever
town they're standing in. Everybody wants them and there's one of them.
And most of what comes through that door is not interesting — it's
nights, and edges, and old men's chests, and a hand on it for twenty
minutes at a time, three times a week, for years.

"Now. You come to that door and you say *my father's ill, come with me,
it's three days*, and what they hear is: six days of walking, a fee I
won't get, and a case I can't fix. And they say no, and they're right to.

"But you come to that door and you say *teach me the palm for an
afternoon and I'll take it three days away and you'll never see either of
us again* — " He sat back. "That's a good trade. That's a good trade for
*them*. They spend an afternoon and they get a chest off their list
permanently."

"That's — " Uzuki stopped.

"Go on."

"That's not asking for help. That's — "

"That's selling them something," said Tazu. "Yes. Which is why you'll get
it, and why your mother wouldn't, because your mother would go and ask
nicely and take no for an answer, and you're going to walk in there and
tell a busy person how to have a shorter week."

---

She sat on the box for a bit and did not say anything.

"Now say the fifth thing," said Tazu.

"There's only four."

"There's five. There's always five. Go on."

She looked at the floor of the shed.

"Would it have worked," said Uzuki. "If we'd done it last year."

Tazu was quiet.

"That's the one I'm not answering," he said. "Not because I'm being
delicate with you. Because I don't know, and a man who tells a child
*yes* to that question has just handed her something she'll carry
around for forty years, and I've seen it done and I won't."

"But you think — "

"I think you should ask me things I can answer," said Tazu, "and you're
extremely good at it, and that one isn't one."

---

He got up and went round the bench and got something out of the drawer.

It was a stub of pencil and a piece of paper about the size of his palm,
and he squared it up on the bench with two fingers, and wrote something
on it, and looked at it.

Uzuki watched him.

He looked at it for about as long as it had taken him to write it. Then
he folded it once, and once again, and put it in his shirt pocket, and
buttoned the pocket.

"Right," he said. "Go on, out. I've got this to finish before the light
goes."

She got up off the box.

At the door she stopped, with her hand on it.

"Was that a name?"

"Out," said Tazu.

---

She went home the long way, past the ditch and round by the field, which
took nine minutes longer and gave her nine minutes.

*He wrote it and he didn't give it to me.*

*Which means he's got a name. Which means he knows somebody, or knows
somebody who knows.*

*And he isn't going to, because a bit of paper off Tazu makes it a thing
Tazu sent a child to do, and he's got to live here afterwards, and he's
the one they send for.*

She kicked a stone along the path in front of her for a while.

*So I'll go without one.*

*You don't need a name for a door. You need a door.*

She got to the gate and stopped and looked at the house, which had a
light in the back room and no light in the front, which meant her mother
was at the table with the box out, at the eleventh of the month, again.

*Four things,* Uzuki thought.

*And now I know what I'm selling.*
