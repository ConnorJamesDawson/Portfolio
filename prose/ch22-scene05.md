# Chapter 22, Scene 5 — The Reading Room

*Act 4, part A. Konoha. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,550 words.*

---

The room they gave him was on the third floor of a block on the east side
that housed, as far as he could establish over the following weeks, a
retired weapons instructor, two families of refugees from a border
incident nine years old, an extremely deaf woman who did the stairs, and
about eleven people whose business he never learned.

It was twelve feet by nine. Wooden floor, one window facing east over a
tiled roof and a persimmon tree with four fruit still on it in the
eleventh month, a mat, a low table, a shelf, a hook, a paraffin stove and
a tap on the landing shared with two other doors.

It cost him four thousand two hundred ryō a month, deducted, which he only
found out about at the first audit.

It was the first room he had ever had in his life that was *his*, in the
sense that nobody was going to arrive at it, and he stood in the middle of
it on the first afternoon with his pack at his feet for a long time
without putting anything down, and then went round it — the door rebate,
the window sill, the skirting, the gable wall — on his hands and knees,
out of a habit four years old, finding the places where they would go.

Then he sat back on his heels.

He was under a permanent audit of everything he grew, at the direct
written instruction of the Hokage, in a village where a man would come and
ask him every single morning where he intended to be.

There was not one single thing he could put in those walls that would not
be found and entered in a book with a number.

Sazare Ruri knelt on the floor of a room in Konoha and laughed out loud —
once, quietly, entirely alone — and put the empty picture frame on the
shelf, and the medical roll under the window, and went to sleep on a mat
in a room with nothing whatever grown into it, for the first time since he
was fifteen years old.

---

The minder turned up at the sixth hour the next morning and was called
Ashida Kōhei, and he was a chūnin of twenty-six, and he was, from the
first four minutes, one of the most restful people Sazare had ever met.

He was a big round-faced amiable person with untidy brown hair and a
chūnin jacket that did not fasten over the middle of him, and he carried a
clipboard with a printed form on it and a canvas satchel with his lunch in
it, and he introduced himself by explaining the form.

"Right, so, this is a 41-C," he said, on the landing, at six in the
morning. "This is the one I have to do. It's got twelve boxes and eleven
of them are your movements and the twelfth is *observations*, and I have
to write something in the twelfth one every day or it comes back. Can I
come in? I'm not searching, I just have to have seen the room once, and
then never again unless something happens."

"Come in."

"Ta." He came in, looked round the twelve feet by nine for about four
seconds, wrote something, and said: "Right. That's that for a year."

"What did you write?"

Ashida turned the clipboard round.

*Room inspected 0603. Nothing.*

"I write *nothing* a lot," he said. "You'll get used to it. Now — the way
this actually works, as opposed to the way it's described, is that you
tell me where you're going before you go, and if it's somewhere new I'll
probably come with you the first time and be bored, and after that I
won't. I'm not going to follow you about the village. I've got four of
these and a wife."

"Four?"

"You, a Kusa woman who does accounts for the rice contractors, an old
fellow out of Taki who's about ninety and has never once left his street,
and a Suna lad who's been here three years and is honestly more Leaf than
I am at this point." He shouldered the satchel. "You're the interesting
one, which I want you to know is not a compliment. Interesting means my
report goes upstairs instead of into a drawer."

"Upstairs."

"Yeah," said Ashida Kōhei, with the cheerful obtuseness of a man who had
not thought about it for one second. "Straight up. Doesn't stop anywhere.
Never had one of those before."

---

The audit book came on the fourth day and it was, physically, a beautiful
object, which he did not enjoy at all.

Hard-bound, quarter-cloth, about three hundred pages, ruled in six columns
and printed at the head of every single one of them:

*No. | Date | Description | Mass (g) | Disposition | Auditor*

Kanzaki Uta put it on his table herself and stood over him while he opened
it.

"Every item," she said. "Not every session — every *item*. If you grow a
thing the size of a grain of rice to see whether an idea works, it gets a
number, a line and a weight, and then it goes in the failure tin and the
tin gets weighed too. If you drop one and it goes down a drain, you enter
it and you write *lost, drain* in disposition, and I will believe you,
because I have to, and because a man who lies about a drain lies about
other things."

"How often do you look at it?"

"Weekly, for the first year." She pushed her glasses up. "Now the part
nobody says out loud. This book is not to catch you. If you are what
three separate departments think you are, this book is the only thing that
will ever prove it — because in about four years somebody will find a
piece of blue-grey stone somewhere it should not be, and it will come back
to this village, and you will be able to open a ledger and show three
hundred numbered lines and a weight for every gram you have ever grown."

Sazare looked at the six ruled columns for a moment.

"It's an alibi," he said.

"It's *your* alibi," said Kanzaki Uta, "and you will keep it in your own
hand, and I would keep it very carefully indeed."

He wrote the first line that evening at the low table with the persimmon
tree going dark outside the window.

*0001 | 14th, 11th month | flat, test, 40 × 22 × 1.4 | 3 g | failed —
edge chipped on graver | S.R.*

Four ledgers. Men in an office counting his mother's output; a book with a
boxed price; a fat man with a list four deep; and this. He sat and looked
at his own handwriting in the first line of a three-hundred-page book that
somebody would be weighing on Thursdays for the rest of his working life.

Then he went and got the failure tin and put the chipped flat in it and
wrote down the weight of the tin.

---

He went to the reading room on the eighth day, and told Ashida the night
before, and Ashida said, "The *library*? Yeah, all right, I'll walk you
over, I want to look at the noticeboard anyway," and that was the whole of
the escort.

The Academy building was long and low and had been added to about four
times, and there were about two hundred children in the yard in front of
it at the third hour making the noise two hundred children make; and the
reading room was up a flight of stairs on the second floor at the end of a
corridor with a hand-painted sign on the door and a bucket under a leak.

It was one room, about sixty feet by thirty.

There were eleven long tables with benches. There was a librarian's desk
at the near end with a bored girl of about nineteen at it and a card index
and a rubber stamp. The windows were down the long side and the light came
in flat and grey across the tables, and there was a stove in the middle of
the floor with a guard round it and about nine children sitting as close
to it as the guard permitted.

And there were shelves on three walls from the floor to about eight feet.

Sazare stood in the doorway.

"Right, well, I'll be off, then," said Ashida. "Back by dark, and if you
go anywhere else after, tell me tomorrow." And went away down the corridor
whistling, and left him standing in the door of a room with nine hundred
books in it.

---

He did not get to a shelf for about four minutes because he could not make
himself walk.

What stopped him was not the quantity. He had worked out the quantity from
the door — nine hundred, near enough, he counted a shelf and multiplied
because he could not not — and nine hundred books is not a great many
books.

It was the *height*.

The shelves ran from the floor. The bottom three feet of every wall in
that room, the part a person has to crouch down to reach, the part you
put things on when you do not mind about them — that was where they had
put the introductory material. Chakra theory for beginners. Elemental
natures, an outline. Basic anatomy. *Control Exercises, Book One.*

At child height. On an open shelf. In a public room. With the corners
gone soft.

He got down on his knees in front of the near wall and took one out and
opened it, and it fell open by itself at a page it had been opened at
several hundred times, and somebody had written *THIS IS THE HARD ONE* in
pencil in the margin in a round eleven-year-old hand, and somebody else,
in different pencil, had written *no its not*.

He read the first four pages kneeling on the floor with his coat still
on.

It took him about ninety seconds to find, on page eleven, under a heading
in bold type, laid out in a numbered list with a diagram, the answer to a
question he had first asked himself at seven years old with his hand on a
copper lattice, and had asked a woman with a bad temper in a noodle shop
at sixteen, and had had nobody in the world to ask in between.

*The purpose of the frame is threefold: to define the boundary, to
regulate the rate of emission at the point of contact, and — most often
overlooked by beginners — to permit the practitioner's attention to be
divided, since a boundary that is physically present need not be
maintained mentally.*

The rate of emission. At the point of contact. *So the practitioner's
attention can be divided.*

Sazare Ruri knelt on the floor of a public reading room in the Academy
building at Konoha, at sixteen years and two months old, with about nine
hundred books above his head and a stove ticking twenty feet away, and put
his hand over his mouth.

Four hundred years. His grandmother had put a copper lattice on a sand
tray every working day of her life and had told him it was liturgy and had
been unable to say what it was for. His mother had held a piece under one
for six hours at a stretch. He had spent eight months on a bench above a
river with a hammer and thirty-four offcuts working out two thirds of
that paragraph by himself in the dark, and had missed the third part
entirely, and the third part was the one that explained his whole family's
working method —

— and it was on page eleven of a book with a soft corner on a low shelf,
in a room full of children, free, six days a week, from the second hour to
the tenth.

---

"Excuse me."

He got his face in order and turned round.

There was a girl of about eleven standing behind him with her arms full of
somebody else's homework, indicating a shelf above his head with her chin.

"Could you get the blue one down? The fat blue one, second from the end.
I'm not allowed on the stools since Kenta."

Sazare stood up and got the fat blue one down and handed it to her.

"Thanks," she said, and went back to her table, and sat down, and did not
give him one further thought in her life.

He stood in the middle of the room for a moment.

Then he took *Control Exercises, Book One* and two others off the low
shelf and went and sat at the end of the furthest table with his back to
the wall and the door in view, out of a habit he did not notice himself
obeying, and read until the girl at the desk came and told him twice that
they were closing.

---

He was there the next morning when they opened, and the morning after
that, and the morning after that.

He did not tell Ashida Kōhei, because he did not have to; the reading
room was not somewhere new after the first day. It went into box eleven of
a 41-C as *library* about a hundred and forty times over the following six
months, in a slightly deteriorating hand, and into box twelve as
*nothing*.

The exercises were numbered. That was the part he kept coming back to in
the first week, at night, on the mat, in the dark, and turning over the
way he turned over everything anybody ever gave him.

**One** was leaf-sticking. He had never heard of it. It took him about
four minutes and he sat on the floor of a room in Konoha at midnight with
a persimmon leaf on his forehead feeling like an absolute fool and could
not stop grinning.

**Two** was tree-walking, which a girl of twelve had named for him on a
hill in the Land of Fire for nothing.

**Three** was water-walking, and the book said it was harder and said why,
in two sentences, with a diagram.

There were nine.

He got to seven of them by the spring, alone, at night, in a room with a
persimmon tree outside the window, out of a book that had been on a low
shelf for eleven years being annotated by bored children — and every one
of them was control, and control was the only thing he had ever had, and
not one of them was combat training in any sense that anybody could write
on a form.

*Get somebody into the middle of you,* a man with very clean hands had
said, at a table, in a room with a barred window. *A person, a place, a
trade. I don't much care which and neither will it. Only don't leave it
empty.*

Sazare Ruri lay on a mat in a room he could not fortify, with a numbered
audit book on the table and a leaf on his forehead, and put a *trade*
there, because it was the third option on the list and because it was the
one he had reached for every single time anybody had ever offered him
three.
