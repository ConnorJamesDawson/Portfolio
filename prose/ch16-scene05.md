# Chapter 16, Scene 5 — The Second One

*Act 2, part D. Kanezaki. POV: Guren. ~3,750 words.*

---

She found him with her foot, which she would think about for years
afterwards, because of all the ways she had ever imagined it happening —
and she had imagined it, on and off, since she was nine — not one of them
had involved a public stair in a salt port at eleven in the morning.

She was coming up out of a lighter with her hood back and a bad temper,
in a town she had no interest in whatever, on the tail end of an errand
that had turned out to be nothing, and she went up the water stair two at
a time the way she went up everything, and the toe of her sandal caught a
ridge under the lip of the second step.

She got her balance and looked down.

There was a ridge on the second step of a public water stair, running the
width of it, under the overhang, about the thickness of a coin. In the
shade of the lip, where nothing had ever grown and nothing had ever been
built, in a town whose entire waterfront was granite and rendered brick.

Guren crouched down on a stair with forty people going past her and put
two fingers on it.

Crystal. Not stone that looked like it. Not glass. Not the pale rubbish
they mined out of the hills at Hida and sold to fools. It was cold under
her hand in the specific way, and the grain of it lay in the specific
way, and when she flicked it with a nail it made almost no sound at all,
and she was crouched on a wet stair in a country she had no business in
with her heart going like a bird.

Then she looked at what it *was*, and everything got worse.

It was old.

Not old-old; a season, perhaps two. But it had been through weather. It
had been walked on by every porter and fishwife in Kanezaki for months.
There was river silt in the grain of it and a shine on the crown where a
thousand feet had polished it, and where it met the mortar joint it had
been grown *into* the joint, following the line, packed in, to a
tolerance — the way a man beds a sill.

Somebody had made a thing out of crystal and left it there. On purpose.
For strangers. To stop them slipping.

Guren sat down on the step, in the wet, in front of forty people, and
discovered that she was having difficulty with her breathing, and it took
her a moment to identify why, and when she did identify it she liked it
even less.

She had been making crystal since she was nine years old. She could put a
wall across a road in the time it took to say so. She had raised a
labyrinth out of a hillside that had confounded a Byakugan, and grown a
blade down her own forearm mid-step, and taken the damp out of the air in
a valley and given it back as ten thousand needles, and there was not one
person alive who could do what she did.

And she had never once, in her entire life, made an object.

Not one. Nothing she had ever grown had been intended to exist ten
seconds after it was needed. Everything — every wall, every blade, every
spire and prison and mirror — had been made to be used and then left, and
most of it had been left in pieces, and she had never in fifteen years so
much as considered that the material would *hold still* if you asked it
to.

Somebody in this town had asked it to.

---

She had been looking for eleven years, in the way you look for a thing
you have entirely stopped expecting, which is to say by never once
walking past a jeweller's window without checking it.

Finding him took an afternoon and she was insulted by how easy it was.

She did not ask about crystal. She asked, in three shops, who mended
things, and every single person in Kanezaki gave her the same answer
without needing to think about it — *the lad on the salt side*, *the lad
in the lodging window*, *card up, MENDING, does brass, does anything,
doesn't talk* — and one woman added, unprompted, that he had given her
mother sixteen coins back off a job once, and did the lady know he was
leaving? Because he was leaving. Which was a shame.

Guren stood in a chandler's doorway with the fourth identical answer of
the afternoon in her ears and thought, with real anger, on behalf of
somebody she had never met:

*You absolute fool. Do you have the first idea how loud you are?*

---

He was in the window with his left leg out straight and a lock in
pieces, and he saw her about nine seconds before she reached the door,
and he did not run, and everything about the way he did not run told her
what she was dealing with.

He put the parts down in order. He shifted his weight off the bad side
and onto the foot that worked. His hands went still — not casually
still; *set* still, the way you make your hands still when you know they
shake — and his eyes went past her shoulder to the street and came back,
and Guren, who had been doing this since she was eleven, watched a
fifteen-year-old boy find both exits and choose the window without moving
his head.

He was small. He was very obviously not eating enough. There was a scar
line coming out from under a burn-cloth on the right forearm, and
something wrong with the knee, and something four weeks old in the way he
held his ribs, and under all of it he had the face of a person who had
been waiting a long time for somebody to walk through a door and was
faintly relieved that it had finally happened.

*Oh,* thought Guren. *You're not loud. You're* abandoned. *There's a
difference and nobody's ever told you.*

She stopped six feet short and put her hands where he could see them.

"I'm not here about money," she said. "I'm not here about anything. I'll
buy you a meal, you can pick the place, you can sit where you like and I
won't sit between you and the door. If you say no I'll go away and I
won't come back."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"Why?"

"Because there's a ridge under the second step of the water stair," she
said, "and I know exactly what it took to put it there, and there is
nobody on this earth I can say that sentence to."

---

He chose a noodle place on the corner of the fish market, which was full,
and loud, and had two doors and a serving hatch, and he took the seat
with the hatch behind him, and Guren sat with her back to the room and
let him have it, and was quietly and enormously impressed.

He ate like somebody who had learned to eat fast in a place where food
was not certain and had never got over it. She ordered him a second
before he finished the first, without comment, and did not watch him deal
with it.

Then she put two fingers into the wet ring her cup had left on the table.

It took less than a second. It always did. The tea went from a smear of
water to a crystal the size of a plum stone with a small internal click,
and she left it sitting there in the middle of the table between the
pickles and did not look at it, and drank her tea, and watched him over
the rim.

He had gone completely white.

"How—" he said. Then he stopped and started again in a different voice,
because he had heard himself. "That was tea."

"That was tea."

"There was no— " His hand had come halfway across the table and stopped.
"There's nothing in it. There's no *stock*. I need a medium. I need
something with the stuff already in it, I need forty minutes and a
surface and a—" He looked at the plum stone. "You did it out of water. In
a second. While talking."

"I can do it out of the air," said Guren, "if the air's damp, which on
this coast it always is. I can do it faster than that if I'm not being
polite about it." She turned her cup. "How long does a piece take you?"

"Depends what it's for." He was still looking at the thing on the table.
"That step took three nights."

"*Three nights.*"

"Twenty minutes at a time. You can't push it, it comes out—" He stopped
again, and she watched him decide, with an effort that was visible, to
finish the sentence anyway. "It comes out wrong if you push it."

"Wrong how?"

"May I?" he said, and nodded at the plum stone.

---

Guren pushed it across the table with one finger and got, in exchange,
the strangest three minutes of her adult life.

He picked it up in both hands the way a physician picks up a wrist. He
turned it against the window light and did not look at the light, he
looked at the *inside* of it. He put it against his cheekbone, which she
had never seen anybody do with anything. He set it on the boards and
rolled it, and listened. He put his thumbnail on it and drew the nail
across, twice, at two different angles, and stopped, and did it again in
one place.

Then he took a chopstick and tapped it, once, very lightly, about a third
of the way up.

It rang.

It rang like a struck glass, clear and long and absolutely pure, for
four full seconds, in a noodle shop, over the noise of forty people —
and half the room turned round.

Guren sat with her hand round a cup of tea and felt the whole back of her
neck go cold.

"There," he said quietly. "That's what's wrong with it."

"That's — " She had to clear her throat. "That's just the sound it
makes."

"No it isn't. That's its *note*." He put the chopstick down. "Everything
grown has one. Yours has got its sitting right on the outside where
anybody could find it in about ten seconds with a spoon." He looked up at
her, and there was no cruelty anywhere in it, and no triumph either; he
was a boy reporting a fault in an object he had been handed, in the tone
of a man saying *your pillar's a quarter-turn out.* "If somebody gives
that thing its note hard enough, it doesn't chip. It comes apart. All of
it, all at once, everywhere in the piece at the same time, because the
note isn't a place on it, the note is the *whole* of it agreeing to
something."

The room noise came back in around them.

"Say that again," said Guren.

---

She had been trapped in a valley two years ago, and she had put up a
wall, and a man she had trusted had sent bats at it, and the wall had
gone.

Not cracked. Not breached. *Gone* — the entire structure, four hundred
square yards of it, converted into a falling curtain of glitter in about
a second and a half, with a sound she had heard in her sleep every month
since. She had put up two more that day and both had gone the same way.
She had never in her life been so frightened, and the frightening part
had not been losing.

The frightening part was that she had not known *why*.

She had gone over it for two years. She had assumed it was the man; then
that it was the bats; then, for a long bad winter, that it was her —
that the walls had been thin because she had been thin, that the whole
kekkei genkai was conditional on some quality of concentration she had
failed to bring on the day. She had asked the only person she could ask
and he had been interested, genuinely and sincerely interested, and had
taken notes, and had said that they would look into it, and they had not
looked into it, because there had been eleven other things and there
always were.

Nobody had ever told her. In fifteen years, nobody had ever told her,
because there was nobody to tell her, because there was nobody.

And a fifteen-year-old jeweller in a noodle shop had picked up a bead of
crystallised tea and found it in three minutes with a chopstick.

---

"Everything grown has a note," he said again, patiently, the way you
repeat a price. "It's in the — " and he stopped.

She watched him stop. She watched him get about four words into the next
sentence, silently, behind his face, and then simply close, the way a
shop puts its shutters up: complete, fast, without apology, and without
one flicker of it reaching his expression.

"It's in the growth," he said instead. "That's all. It's in how it's
grown."

*Good,* thought Guren, with an admiration so sharp it was almost anger.
*And that's the fastest I have ever seen anybody do that.*

"You know how to move it," she said.

"I know it can be moved. I can't do it reliably. I'm — " He made a small
gesture that took in the leg, the ribs, the town, the whole of him.
"I'm working on it."

"How long can you hold a piece under load?"

"Four seconds."

"*Four.*"

"It cracks," he said. "It always cracks the same way and I know the sound
it makes and I know it's the note and I don't know how to stop it yet."

"Then that's it," he said. He had gone somewhere else; she could watch
him doing it, the way you watch a man count a stack. "That's the whole
of it. It's *time*. You make things fast, so they ring; I make things
slow, so they don't. The step took three nights and it's silent and it's
still there. Everything that's ever cracked on me cracked because I
hurried it." He looked up with something in his face that she disliked
on sight, because she had worn it herself at about his age. "That's the
same trade from both ends. That's one rule."

"No," said Guren.

He stopped.

"I want it to be that," she said. "I've wanted it to be something for two
years and I'd take that one gladly and it isn't true. I've got—" She put
her cup down. "In the valley I put up three walls. The first one I threw
up in about a second and a half and it went like a dropped window. The
second one took me maybe four seconds, which for me is *slow*, that's me
being careful, and it went the same way. The third one I built in about
the same time as the second and it *held*. It held through everything he
had and I walked out behind it, and I have gone over that third wall in
my head about four hundred times and I could not tell you one single
thing I did differently."

She watched him take that in, and she watched — with a kind of appalled
respect — his face do the thing where a good rule dies.

"Then it's not time," he said.

"It's not time."

"It's something time usually *gets* you." He had both hands flat on the
table now. "Something that happens while you're waiting, that you don't
have to do on purpose, so if you're slow you get it every time and never
notice, and if you're fast you get it sometimes and can't tell why—"

"Say the rest of it."

"I can't," said Sazare. "That's the end of what I've got."

And they sat there, the two of them, in the middle of a noodle shop, with
a bead of crystallised tea on the boards between them and half of an
answer each and no way at all to put them together — and Guren
experienced, for perhaps eleven seconds, the specific and unfamiliar
misery of wanting very badly to sit in a room with somebody until a
problem came apart, and knowing exactly what it would cost him if she
did.

---

She paid, and left him the second bowl, and gave him the thing she had
come to give him, and gave it flatly, because she had learned a long time
ago that the way to lose somebody like this is to reach for them.

"There's a person alive," she said, "who could tell you what you're doing
wrong. Not me — I'd teach you my hands and yours don't work like mine.
Somebody who'd want to know *why* it cracks at four, and would take it
apart, and would be interested, which is the part nobody ever believes."
She stood. "I'm not asking you for anything. I'm not telling you where he
is and you shouldn't ask, because you'd be asking a stranger in a noodle
shop and I'd think less of you."

"Why tell me at all, then?"

"Because I spent eleven years thinking I was the only one," said Guren,
"and it turns out that being the only one is a thing that can be done
*to* you, and it costs about as much as an arm, and I'm not leaving
somebody else in it if the fixing of it is one sentence."

She picked the plum stone up off the table and closed her hand on it and
it went back to a smear of water on the boards, and she wiped her hand on
her sleeve, and she was aware while doing it that she had just been shown
off to, at, by a boy who could not have done it, and that she had done it
anyway, and that she would be embarrassed about that on the lighter.

At the door she stopped.

"Two more," she said. "Free, and then I'm gone. One — whatever you're
doing that's put four weeks of hurt on you and made this town say
*doesn't talk*, I don't want to know, and I'm not judging it, and I'd
have done it younger than you did. But you're doing it as a *tradesman*.
I can see it from here. You go in with the same care you'd take over
somebody's kettle, and it's beautiful, and it'll get you killed inside
two years, because the trade you're actually in doesn't reward care. It
rewards leaving."

"And the second?"

Guren stood in the doorway of a noodle shop in a salt port and gave a boy
she had known for two hours the most frightening sentence anybody had
said to him in his life, and gave it kindly, because that was the only
way she knew how to give anything.

"Stop putting rungs in walls."

She watched it land.

It landed the way she had known it would — no start, no flinch, nothing a
room would have seen. What changed was underneath: a fast, total,
interior withdrawal, and then, about a second later, the shutters coming
back down again on purpose, because he had decided not to give her that
either.

"I've walked this town twice," she said, "and I found three. There's one
in the staple of a yard gate on the salt side that took me eleven minutes
and I only found it because the gate was standing proud. Nobody else
alive would look. But I looked, and I'm not the worst thing that could
come down that road, and every single one of those will still be there in
eighty years with your hand on it."

---

She walked down to the water in a bad temper she was not able to account
for until she was two miles out with the town going small behind her.

It was not the boy. She had liked the boy, which was inconvenient.

It was that she had gone to Kanezaki about nothing, and had come away
carrying something she was going to have to decide what to do with,
because she was not a woman who kept things back and had never wanted to
be — and the thing she was carrying was not *the last Ruri is on the
south coast and can be had*, which was worth a great deal and which she
had already resolved to sit on for reasons she had not examined.

It was smaller than that and very much worse.

It was: *the reason your walls came down in that valley is a fault in how
you grow, it has a name, it can be found in three minutes with a
chopstick by anybody who thinks to try it — and there is a boy who knows
where it lives.*

Anyone who was told that would want him. Not for the bloodline. For the
chopstick.

Guren stood at the stern rail with the salt quay going out of sight and
her hand round a wet smear of nothing in her sleeve, and told herself,
with great firmness, that you cannot carry a person who has not asked to
be carried, and that the one sentence was worth more than an offer, and
that he would come in his own time, the way everybody came.

Then she put her thumb along the inside of her wrist, twice, sideways,
the way you check the step on the shoulder of a drop — and had no idea at
all why she had done it, or where the gesture had come from, or why it
made her want to sit down.
