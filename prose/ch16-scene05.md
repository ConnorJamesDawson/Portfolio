# Chapter 16, Scene 5 — The Second One

*Act 2, part D. Kanezaki. POV: Guren. ~2,800 words.*

---

She found him with her foot, which she would think about for years
afterwards, because of all the ways she had ever imagined it happening —
and she had imagined it, on and off, since she was nine — not one of them
had involved a public stair in a salt port at eleven in the morning.

She was coming up out of a lighter with her hood back and a bad temper,
in a town she had no interest in whatever, on the tail end of an errand
that had turned out to be nothing, and she went up the water stair two at
a time the way she went up everything, and the toe of her sandal caught a
ridge under the lip of the second step.

She got her balance and looked down.

There was a ridge on the second step of a public water stair, running the
width of it, under the overhang, about the thickness of a coin. In the
shade of the lip, where nothing had ever grown and nothing had ever been
built, in a town whose entire waterfront was granite and rendered brick.

Guren crouched down on a stair with forty people going past her and put
two fingers on it.

Crystal. Not stone that looked like it. Not glass. Not the pale rubbish
they mined out of the hills at Hida and sold to fools. It was cold under
her hand in the specific way, and the grain of it lay in the specific
way, and when she flicked it with a nail it gave the specific short
sound, and she was crouched on a wet stair in a country she had no
business in with her heart going like a bird.

Then she looked at *how it was made*, and everything got worse.

It had been grown freehand.

No form. No former, no frame, no rail, nothing it had answered to — the
underside of it followed the mortar joint it had been laid into, which
means it had been *told* where to stop, by a person, with intent, at the
moment of growth. And the surface was smooth. Not polished after; nobody
polishes the underside of a stair lip. It had come out of a hand smooth,
which is a thing Guren had spent four years being taught to do with a
lattice and had never once managed without one.

She sat down on the step, in the wet, in front of forty strangers.

Somebody in this town could grow crystal freehand and smooth, and had
used the ability, at some unknown hour of some unremarkable night, to put
a *step-guard* under a public stair so that people would not slip.

---

She had been looking for eleven years, in the way you look for a thing
you have entirely stopped expecting, which is to say by never once
walking past a jeweller's window without checking it.

Nobody else in the world did what she did. She had been told so at nine
by the only person who had ever bothered to explain anything to her, and
he had been kind about it and completely accurate, and she had gone on
being told it by the world every day since: that there were people who
made fire and people who made water and thousands of each and schools for
them, and that there was Guren, and that this was a rarer and therefore
better and therefore lonelier thing to be.

She had made her peace with it years ago and the peace had held perfectly
well until eleven o'clock that morning.

Finding him took an afternoon and she was insulted by how easy it was.

She did not ask about crystal. She asked, in three shops, who mended
things, and every single person in the town of Kanezaki gave her the same
answer without needing to think about it — *the lad on the salt side*,
*the lad in the lodging window*, *card up, MENDING, does brass, does
anything, doesn't talk* — and one woman added, unprompted, that he had
done her mother's tally-frames for less than they were worth and had
given her sixteen coins back off a job once, and did the lady know he was
leaving? Because he was leaving. Which was a shame.

Guren stood in a chandler's doorway with the fourth identical answer of
the afternoon in her ears and thought, with real anger, on behalf of
somebody she had never met:

*You absolute fool. Do you have the first idea how loud you are?*

---

He was in the window with his left leg out straight and a lock in
pieces, and he saw her about nine seconds before she reached the door,
and he did not run, and everything about the way he did not run told her
what she was dealing with.

He put the parts down in order. He shifted his weight off the bad side
and onto the foot that worked. His hands went still — not casually
still; *set* still, the way you make your hands still when you know they
shake — and his eyes went past her shoulder to the street and came back,
and Guren, who had been doing this since she was eleven, watched a
fifteen-year-old boy find both exits and choose the window without
moving his head.

He was small. He was very obviously not eating enough. There was a scar
line coming out from under a burn-cloth on the right forearm and
something wrong with the knee and something four weeks old in the way he
held his ribs, and under all of it he had the face of a person who had
been waiting a long time for somebody to walk through a door and was
faintly relieved that it had finally happened.

*Oh,* thought Guren, *you're not loud. You're* abandoned. *There's a
difference and nobody's ever told you.*

She stopped six feet short and put her hands where he could see them.

"I'm not here about money," she said. "I'm not here about anything. I'll
buy you a meal, you can pick the place, you can sit where you like and I
won't sit between you and the door. If you say no I'll go away and I
won't come back."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"Why?"

"Because there was a ridge under the second step of the water stair," she
said, "and I know exactly what it took to put it there, and there is
nobody on this earth I can say that sentence to."

---

He chose a noodle place on the corner of the fish market, which was full,
and loud, and had two doors and a serving hatch, and he took the seat
with the hatch behind him, and Guren sat with her back to the room and
let him have it, and was quietly and enormously impressed.

He ate like somebody who had learned to eat fast in a place where food
was not certain and had never got over it. She ordered him a second
before he finished the first, without comment, and did not look at him
while he dealt with it.

They talked about nothing for a while. She was good at this and she made
herself be good at it slowly.

Then she took the little wrapped thing out of her sleeve and put it on
the table between them and unwrapped it, and it was a hair-pin — a plain
one, the sort a shop girl wears, with a small blue-grey drop on the end
of it.

"Tell me what's wrong with it," she said.

He picked it up. She watched his whole body change.

It took him about four seconds and he did not know she was watching,
because he had gone entirely into the object the way she had gone into
the stair, and Guren sat with her tea and had the peculiar experience of
watching a stranger do the thing she did.

"It's got a lattice line," he said.

"Yes."

"Round the shoulder of the drop, there — you can't see it, you can feel
it. There's a step where the growth met the wire and stopped and then
went round." He turned it in the window light. "And it's *even*. Too
even. That's not you doing that, is it, that's the frame doing the
smoothing for you." He looked up. "You can't do it without one."

"No," said Guren. "I can't."

"Why not?"

And he asked it — she would remember this, and she was not sure she would
ever be able to explain to anybody why it mattered so much — he asked it
with no cruelty in it anywhere. Not scoring. Not fencing. He asked it the
way he had asked about the shoulder of the drop: as a fault in a thing,
which faults can be found in, and found out, and possibly mended.

"Because I was taught," said Guren.

"That's backwards."

"It is not backwards. It is *exactly* how it works and you'll find out."
She put her cup down harder than she meant to. "I was taught by somebody
who is very good, and everything he gave me is the best there is, and
when you are given the best there is at nine years old you use it, and it
works, and you get quick, and by the time you're old enough to ask
whether the frame is doing something you could be doing yourself, you're
*fluent*, and fluent people don't go back to the beginning. Nobody does.
Nobody has ever done it in the history of anything."

She heard herself and stopped.

"You went back to the beginning," she said, "because there was nobody
standing behind you saying *not like that.*"

"There was nobody at all," he said.

"I know. It's in the work." Guren turned the hair-pin over. "That's what
I came to tell you, if you want it plain. Your work is the work of
somebody who has had to invent everything, and about a third of it is
better than mine, and about a third of it is worse than a first-year's,
and I could not have told you which third was which before I saw it,
because there is no *pattern* to what you have and haven't got. That
doesn't happen. That is not what a person looks like. That's what a
person looks like when they've been left in a room with a problem for
seven years."

He said nothing.

"How long can you hold a piece under load?" she asked.

"Four seconds."

"Four."

"It cracks," he said. "It always cracks in the same way and I don't know
why yet. I know what the sound is. I don't know what makes it happen."

"Six years," said Guren. "That's how long it took me to get past four
seconds, and I had somebody in the room, and he told me what I was doing
wrong in one sentence in the second week and it still took six years."
She let that stand for a moment, and then she gave him the only thing she
had come to give him, and she did it as flatly as she could manage,
because she had learned a long time ago that the way to lose somebody
like this is to reach for them.

"There is a person alive," she said, "who could tell you what you're
doing wrong. Not me. I'd get it wrong; I'd teach you my hands and yours
don't work like mine. I mean a person who has never in his life held a
lattice and could stand behind you for an hour and explain why it cracks
at four seconds, and would be *interested*, which is the part nobody ever
believes." She wrapped the hair-pin. "That's all. I'm not asking you for
anything. I'm not going to tell you where he is and you shouldn't ask,
because you'd be asking a stranger in a noodle shop and I'd think less of
you."

"Why tell me at all, then?"

"Because I spent eleven years thinking I was the only one," said Guren,
"and it turns out that thinking you're the only one is a thing that can
be done *to* you, and it costs about as much as an arm, and I'm not
leaving somebody else in it if the fixing of it is one sentence."

---

She paid, and left him the second bowl, and stood up.

At the door she stopped, because there was one more thing and she had not
decided until that second whether to say it.

The boy had given her a name and it had not been his. She knew that the
way she knew the grain in the ridge; it had come out a half-beat late and
a half-tone flat, and she had let it go by, because everybody she had
ever liked had lied to her about something in the first hour.

But there had been a moment at the table — when he turned the hair-pin
against the light and his thumb went along the shoulder of the drop, a
little sideways stroke, twice, checking the step of it — that had gone
through her like a draught under a door. She had seen a hand do that
before. She could not say where, or when, or whose, and it had not felt
like a memory so much as a room she had once stood in, and she had been
turning it over ever since and getting nothing.

She let that go too. It was not the useful thing.

"Two more," she said. "Free, and then I'm gone."

He looked up.

"One. Whatever you're doing that's put four weeks of hurt on you and made
this town say *doesn't talk* — I don't want to know, and I'm not judging
it, and I'd have done it younger than you did." She put her hood up.
"But you're doing it as a *tradesman*. I can see it from here. You go in
with the same care you'd take with somebody's kettle, and it's beautiful,
and it will get you killed inside two years, because the trade you're
actually in doesn't reward care, it rewards *leaving*."

"And the second?"

Guren stood in the doorway of a noodle shop in a salt port and gave a
boy she had known for two hours the most frightening sentence anyone had
said to him in his life, and gave it kindly, because that was the only
way she knew how to give anything.

"Stop putting rungs in walls."

She watched it land.

It landed the way she had known it would — no start, no flinch, nothing
that a room would have seen. His face did not change at all. What changed
was underneath it: a fast, total, interior withdrawal, like a shop putting
its shutters up, and then, about a second later, the shutters coming back
down again on purpose because he had decided not to give her that either.

*Good,* she thought. *That's the fastest I've ever seen anybody do that.
God help you.*

"I've walked this town twice," she said, "and I found three. There's one
in the staple of a yard gate on the salt side that took me eleven minutes
and I only found it because the gate was standing proud. Nobody else
alive would look. But I looked, and I'm not the worst thing that could
come down that road, and every single one of those is going to be there
in eighty years with your hand on it."

Then she left, and did not look back, and walked down to the water in a
bad temper that she was not able to account for until she was two miles
out on the lighter with the town going small behind her.

It was not the boy.

It was that she had spent eleven years being told that being the only one
was a distinction, and she had sat down that afternoon opposite the other
one, and the other one had four seconds and a bad knee and no teacher and
had used the first of those on a step-guard for strangers.

And she had gone away and left him there.

She turned her collar up and watched the salt quay slide out of sight and
told herself, with great firmness, that she had done the correct thing:
that you cannot carry a person who has not asked to be carried, and that
the one sentence was worth more than an offer, and that he would come in
his own time, the way everybody came.

Then she took the hair-pin out of her sleeve and turned it over, and put
her thumb along the shoulder of the drop, twice, sideways, checking the
step of it.
