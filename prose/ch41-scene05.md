# Chapter 41, Scene 5 — What He Owed Her

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the Hokage's office, after hours. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,951 words.*

---

The thaw turned back to frost overnight and stayed there, and by the
ninth the puddles in the tower courtyard had gone to that white
crazed-over ice that holds a bootprint and gives up nothing else. He
came up the outside stair at twenty past eight with a folder under his
coat, because the folder was paper and the air had teeth in it, and the
guard on the landing looked at him, looked at the hour, and did not ask.

Tsunade was at the desk with two lamps going and one stack left.

"You're not on this floor at this hour," she said.

"No."

"Is somebody dying."

"No."

"Then you can have twenty minutes and I'm going to be rude about the
end of it." She turned a sheet over. "Sit down."

He did not sit down.

"You made a trade," he said, "the night of the scroll."

The pen stopped.

He had rehearsed no part of it, which he had decided on the stair was
the correct decision and had begun to doubt about four steps from the
top, and so what came out came out in the order it had happened rather
than the order that would have made it land.

"You said you wouldn't ask me anything that night. And you said that in
exchange, when I wanted to talk about the technical content, I'd come
to you and not to a notebook." He put the folder on the corner of the
desk. "I've had it in a notebook for eleven days. I've written it up
four times. Yagi has half of it and Kanzaki has the other half and
neither of them has all of it, and both of them are the wrong person
for the whole of it, and I worked out about two nights ago that I was
writing it up over and over because writing it up is what I do instead
of the thing you said."

Tsunade looked at the folder and then at him.

Then she reached across the desk, took the whole remaining stack, and
put it on the floor.

"Shizune will kill me," she said. "Talk."

---

He talked for nine minutes without being interrupted once, which was
the longest anybody had let him run in his life, and he knew it was
nine minutes because he checked the water clock on the sill afterwards
and did the subtraction, and knew as he did it that checking was a
habit he was going to have to look at.

The four media. Yard aggregate, twenty-two per cent reject. Granite,
thirty-one. Site rubble off the invasion streets, forty-four, which was
the number that had made him stop. And his own prior work, re-seeded —
crystal he had grown himself and taken back down and grown again —
four.

"Four," Tsunade said.

"Four. Three hundred and eleven entries in Kanzaki's ledger over
fourteen months. I filled the source column in myself every single time
and I have never once read the column, because I entered it as
provenance. It's a quartermaster's field. It exists so nobody steals
gravel."

"And it was the independent variable."

"It was the only independent variable I had, and I had it for fourteen
months in my own handwriting."

"Kanzaki knew."

"Since the fourteenth month. She sat on it deliberately and she told me
why to my face and she was right, which I resented for about a day and
a half." He turned a page round. "The four per cent is the whole
finding. It isn't that I'm better at working my own crystal. The reject
rate is a measure of how fast the material takes up a course, and the
material that takes it up fastest is the material that has held one
before."

"It remembers."

"I don't want to say remembers. Remembers is a word I'd have to defend."

"Then defend the one you do want."

"A course is not a thing I apply." He heard his own voice do something
he could not correct in flight. "I've had it backwards for eleven
years. I thought time was for me — that a course took four seconds
because four seconds was how long the work took. It isn't. The time is
for the material. A course is the material taking up part of the
instruction, and the reason a master looks fast is not that his hands
are fast. It's that he can get the material to take it up quickly. The
skill isn't in the hands. It's in the handing over."

The office had gone very quiet.

Tsunade got up, went to the cabinet behind her without any particular
hurry, and came back with the bottle and two of the small cups and the
kind of expression he had learned to associate with Kanzaki looking at
a scale that had just told her something inconvenient.

"Sit down," she said. "You are not getting twenty minutes."

---

She came at it from the other end, and she was better at it than he
was, which he had expected and had come specifically to find out.

"You know what we call it," she said. "When two medics run the same
technique on the same wound and get two different results. We call it
constitution. Patient's constitution. It's in every textbook in that
hospital and it's in three I wrote, and I want to be honest with you
about what the word means, because I've had forty years to think about
it and the answer is that it means *we don't know*. It's a bin. You put
the variance in it and you close the lid and you go home."

"The stones read seventy per cent."

"On a second medic. Yes. Emiko's figure."

"Seventy, plus or minus six, and everyone in that room read it as a
loss." He had the second folder open now. "Six repetitions of Yagi's
hands, and then somebody else's hands, and it drops thirty per cent and
holds there. It's not a loss. It's the same number as my four per cent
wearing different clothes. The tissue took up the instruction faster
the sixth time than the first because the tissue had held that
instruction before. The receiver contributes. That's all seventy per
cent is."

"And when the receiver's had nothing before—"

"Site rubble. Forty-four."

Tsunade sat back and put her hand over her mouth, and did not say
anything for long enough that he began to construct four different
readings of it and got as far as the second before she moved.

"I have watched three good medics quit," she said, "because they could
not get past the plateau, and every one of them was taught what I was
taught, which is *push harder and control it finer.* And I have never
once, in forty years, taught anybody that the body has a job in it."

"You have. You just didn't put it in a book."

"Don't be generous, it slows this down."

"I'm not being generous. Yagi's rate is better than anybody's on that
ward and she takes longer per patient than anybody on that ward, and
she talks the entire time, and I logged it as bedside manner for
fourteen months because that is what it is called." He put a page
across. "It might also be the mechanism."

"It might also be that she's better at the technique."

"It might. That's a test, not an argument. Six practitioners, matched
injuries, half of them silent."

"Half of them silent," Tsunade repeated, "is a study I would have to
put in front of a review board who would ask me why I was withholding
comfort from a bleeding man, and they would be right to ask, and I'd
have to have a very good answer." She poured, and did not drink. "Get
me the answer and I'll sign it."

---

It got away from them somewhere after the eleventh hour.

She ate at some point — a cold dish out of a lacquer box with the
lacquer gone to a dull brown at the corners where a thumb had opened it
several thousand times, and she ate the way she did everything at that
desk, fast and without ceremony and entirely without looking at it,
which he filed as *object in use* and then, on the next rung up,
without meaning to, as *she has not sat down to a meal with another
person in some time,* and then stopped himself, because that was rung
four and he had no evidence for rung four and Kakashi had spent a year
telling him what happens to men who take the top of the ladder without
the middle of it.

Her hair came out of the ties at the twelfth hour. She did not appear
to notice.

He over-reached once.

"If the receiver contributes," he said, "then it follows that a patient
who trusts the medic heals better, and it follows from that that
outcome is partly the patient's—"

"Stop."

He stopped.

"Say the end of that sentence out loud," Tsunade said, "and listen to
it."

He said it out loud. He heard it.

"That's the sentence," she said, "that gets used on dying people. I
have heard it in four countries in two languages, out of the mouths of
people selling water, and out of the mouths of good doctors who were
tired, and the second one is worse. *He didn't want it enough. She'd
given up.*" She set the cup down hard enough to be heard. "Your finding
is about a rate. The instant it becomes about a *will* you have built a
machine for blaming the dead, and it will run without you, and it will
run for a hundred years."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know exactly what you meant. That's why I'm telling you now and not
in the review, because in the review I'd have to say it in front of
eleven people and you'd never write another line." She pushed the cup
back at him. "You're going to publish this. You have a materials brain
and you form hypotheses like a man throwing seed, and about one in nine
of them is going to be true and about one in nine is going to be that.
Write the true ones down. Bring me the other eight before you write
them anywhere at all."

"That's the trade again."

"That's the trade again," she agreed. "I've extended the terms. I do
that when I like the interest."

---

The guard changed on the stair at the fourth hour, boots and a mumbled
count, and Tsunade heard it and looked at the window, where the frost
had come up the inside of the glass in the small hours and made the
whole of the village into a white blur with four lights in it.

"Fourth hour," she said.

"Yes."

"I told you I'd be here until the fourth hour of the morning asking you
questions about it."

"You did."

"I was being rhetorical." She looked at the stack of paper on the floor
by her chair, and at the two lamps, one of which had gone out at some
point without either of them noticing. "Go home, Ruri. You're on at
six."

He was at the door when she said the other thing.

"For the record. That's the second-best hour I've had in this office
and I've had this office two years."

He stood in it a moment with his hand on the frame, and did not ask
what the first was, and worked out on the stair that not asking had
been the correct call and that he had made it without running anything
at all.
