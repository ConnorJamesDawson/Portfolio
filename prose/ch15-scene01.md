# Chapter 15, Scene 1 — Handholds

*Act 2, part D. Tomari, the thin coast. Sazare Ruri, age fifteen. POV: Sazare.
~1,450 words.*

---

Tomari was a ferry-stage and nothing else, and it had the particular
character of places that exist because of a gap in something: two hundred
people, four inns, a chandler, a customs shed nobody staffed after the
fourth hour, and a strait half a mile wide with a tide that ran through it
like a horse bolting.

He had been there six weeks, which was four weeks longer than he should
have been, and he knew it, and he had a reason, and the reason was money.

The runs paid in halves. Half at the peg going out, half at the peg coming
back, and the coming-back half was the half you had to be alive and in
Tomari to collect — Ryōsuke's system, and a good one, and it meant a
courier accumulated in a place rather than carrying. Sazare had four runs'
worth of coming-back sitting with a chandler's wife who held money for
half the strait, and a fifth run out and due to close, and *then* he would
move, and he had told himself that on the first of two consecutive months.

There was also the ferryman's boy, which he did not count as a reason
because he had trained himself not to.

His name was Shū and he was nine and he had a stammer that the whole town
had long ago agreed to wait out, and he had attached himself to Sazare in
the second week over the business of a broken oarlock, and had since
appointed himself to the position of assistant, apprentice, and principal
nuisance. He sat on the jetty bollard while Sazare mended things. He held
tools with enormous gravity. He asked, roughly, four hundred questions a
day, none of which Sazare answered with a lie, most of which he answered
with *I don't know, let's look* — and if that was a debt being paid
sideways to a boy who had once asked *why* at a woman with her sleeves
pinned and been told *because that's how it's done*, then it was paid
without either party being told about it.

That was the whole of Tomari. Four inns, a chandler, a tide, a boy, and
money in a wall.

---

The habit he had never told anybody about, and had been doing for two
years, was this.

Every place he slept, within about three days, he went round it.

Not searching — *reading*. Which wall would take a boot. Where the roof
space ran and whether it ran anywhere. Which door opened outward and could
therefore be wedged, and which opened inward and therefore could not.
Where the water was. Where the dark was. Where a man coming in fast would
have to slow down, and why, and whether the why could be made worse.

And then, over the following nights, quietly, on the scrap tray or with
his bare hands against stone and timber, he grew things.

Never weapons. He had thought about that once, in his first month out of
Yumoto, and had put it down and not picked it up: a grown blade in a room
is a room with a grown blade in it, and rooms get searched, and there is
no innocent explanation. What he grew were *fixtures*. A rung the size of
two fingers, blue-grey, mortared into the render of a wall six feet up
where no rung had been, in a colour that read as old repair to anybody who
did not put a hand on it. A spur in a doorframe's rebate, a quarter-inch
proud, exactly where a wedge would want a shoulder. A ridge on a slate,
under the eave, where a foot would otherwise slide. A cleat inside a
drain-mouth. Handholds, footholds, a stop, an anchor.

It took him twenty minutes a night for a week and then he never touched
them again. He had left them behind in eleven lodgings across two years —
in Yumoto, in Shiranui, in Ōura, in a loft at Sakihama, in three villages
whose names he had made a point of forgetting — and not once, in two
years, had a single one of them been the slightest use to him.

He knew perfectly well what it was. He had watched himself do it often
enough to have a diagnosis ready, in Tōan's flat dosage voice: *this is a
man who cannot sleep in a room he has not fortified, treating himself for
a condition he will not name.*

It embarrassed him. He did it anyway. In Tomari, over the first ten nights,
he did the loft above the chandler's where he slept, and the alley behind
it, and the customs shed's blind side, and the culvert that ran under the
ferry road to the flats — six weeks in a town, and a boy who had once lain
in a drainage cutting under a road for three hours, and the arithmetic of
that had never once needed explaining to him.

---

The warning came in on the fifth run, in the ordinary way, which was the
part that frightened him afterwards.

He picked the drop off the second peg of a rope-board at a hamlet up the
strait — a chip, wedged flush, in Ryōsuke's older tighter hand — and
underneath it, which was not usual, a second chip in a hand he had been
taught to know: Anzu's marker, the one that meant *this is from the
counter and it is not routine*.

There was a woman at the hamlet's chandler who fed him and looked at
nothing.

"Word's come down," she said, at the stove, with her back to him. "Three
men working the coast asking about a mender. They've been asking for
months and they're better at it than the last lot. They came through
Sakihama eleven days back."

Sakihama was four stages north. Eleven days.

"They're not asking as anybody," she said. "That's the thing the counter
wanted passed. No papers, no badge, no story you can check. They pay for
their drinks like honest men and they don't ask questions and they've
been doing it long enough to have got *good* at not asking, which takes
a year at least." She turned round. "You're to go inland. That's the
counter's word. Not south — south is where a boy on this coast goes, and
they'll have read that off a map before they left home. Inland, and
across, and sit down somewhere ugly for a season."

"I've money out."

"Then you've money out," said the woman.

"It's four runs. It's—" He heard the shape of it as he said it. "It's
most of what I've got."

The woman looked at him for a moment with an expression he had seen twice
before in his life and had learned to identify very precisely: the look
of somebody deciding how much of a true thing to say to somebody who is
going to do the wrong thing anyway.

"I lost a brother to a coming-back half," she said. "That's all I'll say
about it. There's soup."

He went back down the strait that night, and lay in the loft above the
chandler's in Tomari with the tide working under the boards, and did the
arithmetic four separate times, and it came out the same way each time,
which is how you know an arithmetic is a wish.

Eleven days at Sakihama. Four stages. If they came down the coast asking
at every stage, that was three or four days a stage — twelve to sixteen
days, minimum, and probably worse, because the strait towns were a
bottleneck and bottlenecks are slow.

The fifth run closed on the third day. The money — all five runs of it,
everything two years had made — was collectable on the fourth.

He gave himself the four days.

And on the second of them, because it had rained and because Shū had
been going on about it for a week, he spent an hour and a half on the
jetty in the wet re-seating the ferry's forward thwart, with a
nine-year-old holding the mallet in both hands like a religious object
and asking whether wood remembered being a tree.

"I don't know," said Sazare. "Let's look."

They looked. The wood, on inspection, appeared to remember something;
the grain ran two ways round a knot where a branch had been, and Shū
found this so satisfying that he had to be physically prevented from
telling the entire ferry queue about it.

It was, Sazare thought later — with the total and useless clarity of
hindsight, in a culvert, in the dark, bleeding — an extremely ordinary
afternoon.
