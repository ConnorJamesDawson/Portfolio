# Chapter 16, Scene 4 — The Deposit

*Act 2, part D. Kanezaki. Sazare Ruri, age fifteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,300 words.*

---

He did not sleep, and what he did instead was cost it out, because that
was the only thing he had ever been able to do with a problem.

**Go back now.** The body was on the gangway in the dark and would be
until the first hour at the earliest. He could be at the bend in eleven
minutes. And then he would have to open a dead man's side with his own
fingers, in the dark, blind, looking for a tapered thing four inches in
under a rib arch that had already broken it once — and if he found it he
would have made a wound that no physician on earth would call a mugging,
and if he did not find it he would have made that wound for nothing.

**Take the body.** Into the river, off the coping, at the cleat. Sixteen
stone of dead weight, on a knee that did not lock, and a man who goes
into a river comes out of a river, generally three days later and
generally somewhere embarrassing, and a body pulled out of the channel at
Kanezaki with two wounds in it is not a mugging either. A mugging leaves
its man where it dropped him. The whole value of the arrangement was that
nothing had been *tidied*.

**Burn it.** He considered this for perhaps four seconds and it turned
his stomach over so comprehensively that he had to sit down on the edge
of the bed, and he noticed — this was the part he minded — that what
turned it over was not the burning. It was that a fire on the salt side
would take the warehouse row, and the warehouse row was eleven buildings,
and there were people asleep over two of them.

He had checked which two. On the fourth day. Out of habit.

So he did the fourth thing, which was nothing, and he did it lying on his
back with his eyes open until the third hour.

---

At the third hour he got up and went out and walked to the top of the
gangway, which was, as choices go, the stupidest of his life to that
point.

There was a lamp at the bend and two men with it and a handcart, and a
third man in a physician's black coat crouched on his heels with his
sleeves back.

Sazare stood at the west end in the shadow of the yard gate — the gate
with a hair of blue-grey inside its staple, standing three inches proud,
fouled on its keeper exactly as designed — and watched them take him
away.

They were not careful with him and they were not rough with him. They
were two porters at the third hour of the morning moving a heavy object
they had moved before, and the physician's man had already stood up and
was talking to somebody, and it took the whole business four minutes from
where Sazare was standing.

He heard about eleven words of it and one of them was *purse*.

Then the handcart went up Kanmuri Street with the good brass lantern
riding on the dead man's chest because there was nowhere else to put it,
and the gangway was empty, and the tide turned, and Sazare stood in the
dark for a long time with his hand flat on the yard gate.

---

The question came to him there, and it was a strange question, and he
recognised it as the first genuinely new thought he had had in two
years.

*Is it still mine?*

The thing under Nakabori Sōhachi's ribs had come out of his own palm. It
had not been mined or bought or found. It had been *his*, four seconds
ago, in the sense that his hand was his — it had come out of him the way
the spike had come out of him in a square when he was thirteen, and he
had never in his life had a word for what that meant, because the only
people who had ever known anything at all about it were dead in a
district that no longer existed.

His mother had grown ten thousand pieces and sold them, and they had gone
out across the world in the ears and on the throats of people she never
met, and nobody in his family had ever once, in four hundred years,
asked whether the thing on the trestle was still connected to the woman
who made it.

Why would they. It was jewellery.

He put his hand on the coping at the bend, on the cleat he had grown
there three nights ago for a purpose it had duly served, and shut his
eyes, and tried — without any idea whatever what he was trying, with no
technique, no instruction, and no word for the verb — to *ask it
something*.

There was the cold of wet stone, and the river, and his own pulse in his
palm.

Whether anything else happened he could not have said, then or
afterwards, with any honesty at all. He knelt there for eleven minutes
in the dark like a man praying at a wall, and got up with his knee
locked, and went home.

---

The town took two days to decide what had happened and then decided it
comprehensively.

Nakabori Sōhachi, factor, killed on the salt gangway for eleven ryō,
which was — everybody said this, the phrase went round Kanezaki like a
song — *about right for him.* The harbourmaster came in for a great deal
of it, because of the lamps. There was a subscription raised on the
second day for the lamps, and it raised more in an afternoon than four
years of the guild asking politely, and Sazare put in five coppers
because a man who does not put in is a man who is remembered for not
putting in.

The physician's man had looked at him and had said stabbed, twice, from
behind and in front, by somebody who did not know his business, and that
was the whole of the medical opinion in a river port on a factor nobody
liked, and it was correct in every particular, and it went in a book, and
that was the end of it.

Nobody said *crystal*. Nobody said anything at all.

Sazare heard the account four separate times in two days and each time
the relief went through him like warm water, and each time, about a
second later, the other thing arrived behind it.

Because the tip was not gone. The tip had not been found, examined, and
dismissed. The tip had never been looked for, by a tired man with a lamp
at the third hour, in a wound full of blood, on a body nobody was going
to spend money on.

It had simply gone into the ground with the rest of him, on the fourth
day, in a plot on the hill above Kanmuri Street with a stone that would
have his name on it by the spring — which meant that for the rest of
Sazare Ruri's life there would be a specific marked location, in a
specific town, on a specific hill, that anybody who ever wanted to could
walk to with a spade.

He worked out, lying awake, that it was worse than leaving it on the
stones would have been. On the stones it was rubbish in a gutter and gone
in a week. In a grave it was *filed*.

---

He stayed four more days, because leaving the morning after a killing is
a description and he had learned that from a man who kept a door.

He mended thirty-one things.

He did it properly, every one, because doing it badly would also have
been a description, and because the queue did not stop coming, and
because on the second of the four days the lodging-house woman's
neighbour brought in a tally-frame from the counting-house on Kanmuri
Street — eleven of them there, she said, all in a disgrace, and the new
man had said to get them seen to, and did he do brass in quantity?

He said he did.

He mended all eleven and he took the money and he did not permit himself
one single thought about any of it, and that was the day he understood
that the discipline he had invented on a bollard nine days earlier — the
not-assembling, the loose parts kept deliberately unsorted in the tray —
was not a thing he was going to be able to put down when the job ended.

It was going to have to be permanent. He was going to have to live the
rest of his life with a tray of unsorted parts in him and never once lay
them out.

---

The word went back the way Umeya had said it would, through the boards,
and the boards paid.

He picked the second half off a peg at a rope-walk two stages down the
river in a twist of waxed paper with no hand on it at all, which was
itself a kind of message: routine work carries a mark; this did not
carry anything, because the men who handled it did not want their hands
on it either.

A hundred and twenty ryō. Two hundred and forty in total. More than
eight years of carrying, in his coat, in about a month.

He sent the tenth.

He did it at a counter, in his own presence, in a ledger, under a name
that was not true, and the woman behind the counter entered
twenty-four ryō against the war in the Land of Water without looking up,
because it was a remittance and remittances are the most boring thing in
the world; and Sazare stood there while the pen moved and thought about
Kohaku on a step in the dark saying *no* and *no* and *yeah*, and about
his mother's earrings going out across the world in the ears of people
she never met, and about a fifty-one-year-old man asking him to count a
salt barge personally.

He did not make an exception and he never would. That had been settled in
a room over a stable in Tsuzura and it was not going to be reopened
every month for the rest of his life.

---

The teacher was the part that did not work, and it did not work in a way
he had not seen coming at all.

He had two hundred and sixteen ryō and a clear specification and he spent
eleven days on it, through the boards and through Umeya's kind of people
in three towns, and what he learned was this:

Men who can teach the trade do not take students. They take
*apprentices*, which is a different word for a different thing — a
missing-nin who agrees to spend a season on a boy is a missing-nin who
intends to have that boy afterwards, on a rope, doing his work in his
towns for his money, and the money changing hands at the start is not a
fee. It is a purchase price. Two of the three people who got back to him
made this reasonably clear and the third made it very clear indeed.

And the ones who did teach for money, honestly, by the season — the
ronin, the retired, the drunks with a yard — taught what Sakai had
taught. Bodies and edges and ground. The best of them, at four times the
price, was a man Sakai would have called a decent third-rate and would
have refused to drink with.

Nobody in the whole border country would sell a fifteen-year-old
unaffiliated boy with no clan and no village and no jutsu an education in
chakra.

Of course they wouldn't. That was what a village *was*. Four years in the
Academy had taught him nothing whatsoever about the world and one
sentence about the thing he actually needed to know, and Mizuho had said
it in the second month of the year he was nine, standing at the front of
a room, in the flat voice: *a village is the only place that will teach
you this, and that is not an accident, and it is not generosity.*

He sat with two hundred and sixteen ryō and the exact knowledge of what
he needed, in a country where it was not for sale.

---

The scroll came over the boards in the same fortnight and he nearly
missed it, because it came the way the true ones always came: sideways,
as garnish, in a notice about something else.

A run going north to a broker at Nagihama. Contents listed for the
carrier's information in the way of consignments that are not quite
legal: *lot, mixed, ex-Water, dom. effects, temple and clan work, sold
per catalogue at the third hour on the—*

*clan work.*

Sacked compounds get looted. Looted things get sold. Looted things from a
district that no longer exists get sold *by catalogue*, in a port
two countries away, to collectors, at the third hour, by men in good
coats with a little hammer.

He read it four times.

Then he folded the notice back into the board's pocket exactly as he had
found it, and stood in the rope-walk with his hands flat on the counter,
and did not go north, and did not ask anybody anything, and went back to
his lodging and lay on the bed in his clothes with his eyes open.

He had a tray of parts in him that he was never going to be allowed to
lay out.

He put one more in it.
