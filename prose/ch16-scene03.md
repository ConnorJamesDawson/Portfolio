# Chapter 16, Scene 3 — The Doorway

*Act 2, part D. Kanezaki, the salt-side gangway. Sazare Ruri, age
fifteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,600 words.*

---

He was in position an hour and a half early, in the recess of a loading
door at the bend, because a man who arrives on time arrives breathing.

Then he went still.

It was the first time in his life he had ever used it for this and he
knew that while he was doing it, which did not stop him doing it, which
was the beginning of everything else.

*You are a bowl of water. Nothing is being poured in and nothing is going
out.* Nine years old, in a room full of eight-year-olds, in the fog, with
a woman at the front of the class who had marked him down deliberately
and never told him why. He had held it seven minutes for an examiner. He
had held it three hours in a drainage cutting under a road while
prisoners went past above him. He had held it eleven hours in six inches
of run-off with a professional four yards away in the dark.

Every single one of those had been a boy trying not to be found.

He held it now for eighty-one minutes in a doorway with a fish-knife in
his sleeve, and the water was as still as it had ever been, and nothing
came into it and nothing went out of it, and somewhere under the surface
of that stillness a thing he had no name for turned over once and went
back to sleep.

---

The lighter went down at the sixth hour with a lamp at its stern and a
man singing on it, the way it had for nine evenings.

Four minutes later the stick came along the cobbles from the west end.

He had learned the sound of it on the second day: the stick, then two
steps, then the drag of the right foot, then the stick — a three-beat
limp, absolutely regular, audible from ninety yards on a still night, and
he had thought when he first identified it that it was the most useful
thing he had found all week.

He listened to it come down two hundred feet of stone in the dark.

The man stopped at the bend, as he did, and turned to the water, as he
did, and stood there with something in his left hand that caught the
lighter's stern lamp for a moment — brass, and a shape Sazare's hands
knew better than his eyes did, because his hands had been inside it for
three days.

Sazare came out of the recess.

---

It went wrong at the second second and it never came back.

He had it right up to then. Eleven feet, closing on the outside, on the
soft blind quarter behind the man's right shoulder where the stick and
the bad hip meant a body could not turn quickly, over stone he had walked
a hundred times, on the ball of a foot that made no sound. That part was
nine days of work and it was excellent and it delivered him, exactly on
plan, to arm's length of a stranger's back.

And then a fifteen-year-old with a knee that did not lock had to put four
and a half inches of fish-knife through a wool coat into a fifty-one-year
old man, and the entire remainder of the design collapsed into what he
actually was, which was a boy who had never done this and weighed eight
and a half stone.

He struck low, because low was what he could reach with the leg he had.

The knife went in and he knew instantly it had gone in wrong. It went in
too far back and too flat, into the meat above the hip and along the
bone, and the coat took most of the depth, and the wool bound on the
blade, and it did not do the thing he had been told all his life that a
knife does.

The man made a sound like a door and turned round.

---

Everything after that happened in a space of about six seconds and
Sazare could have described any given quarter-second of it for the rest
of his life.

The man was not a fighter and did not fight. He did something much worse,
which was that he *grabbed*, the way a drowning man grabs, and he had
four stone and eleven inches on Sazare and two arms that worked and no
technique whatever, and Sazare went back into the warehouse wall with a
grown man's whole weight arriving on his chest and his own two broken
ribs, four weeks old and still knitting, going off like a struck bell.

He lost the knife. It came out of the coat with the turn and it was
simply gone, on the stones somewhere, in the dark.

He got a forearm up under the man's jaw and could not move it. He tried
for the knee and had no base to do it from, because the base was a joint
full of old fluid that had not straightened since Tomari.

*Seven seconds,* said Sakai, from a fence, in the steam. *Careful, a
better man needs about seven. Careless, three.*

This man was neither. This man was a salt factor with a bad hip and a
double handful of Sazare's coat and no idea in the world what to do
next, and he was going to win, because he was a grown man and Sazare was
not, and there was no ground left to fight because Sazare had used all
of it getting here, and the only thing on that entire gangway that
belonged to Sazare Ruri was inside his own hands.

He opened them.

---

It came out of his right palm, off the lattice, freehand, in a rented
second, and it came out *wrong* — he knew it the way you know a note is
flat while you are still singing it, the still water gone out of him
completely, no smoothing, no shape, nothing of the eight months on a
bench above a river — and it went into the man's side under the ribs to
the depth of about four inches and it was not a blade, it was a shape
that had wanted to be a blade and had been made by something screaming.

There was a small hard sound, transmitted up his arm, and everything went
loose.

He knew what the sound was. He had spent eight months finding out what
that sound was, on a bench, with a hammer and a spoon and thirty-four
worthless offcuts. It was a wrong-grown piece failing at its note, and
the note this time had been a rib.

The man let go of him and went back three steps and sat down in the
loading-door recess with his legs out in front of him like a child.

---

Sazare stood against the wall with his ribs howling and his right hand
open and blue-grey grit on his palm, and every instruction in his life
said the same word, and the word was *leave*.

The plan said leave. Sakai said leave — *every plan you ever make ends
with leaving.* The trade said leave. Eleven feet away, on the coping, was
a cleat he had grown for exactly this, and beneath it was black water
that went to the sea.

"Ah," said the man in the doorway, conversationally. "Ah. Oh."

And then, in a completely different voice, thin and up in the top of the
throat, to the dark in general:

"Somebody—"

Sazare walked over and knelt down in front of him.

He did not decide to. He would spend a considerable part of the rest of
his life being sure about that, and being sure that it did not matter in
the slightest, because *not deciding to* is what a habit is.

The man's face came round. The lighter's lamp was gone downriver and
there was nothing to see by except the sky, and Sazare was a small shape
kneeling, and the man looked at him for a second and a half.

"Oh," he said. "It's *you*."

---

He was relieved.

That was the thing. There was no mistaking it and there was no
misreading it: the shoulders came down, the grip on the coat let go, the
whole animal panic of a man alone in the dark with something wrong
inside him simply drained out of Nakabori Sōhachi's body, because the
mender had turned up.

"Bloody — *bloody* fool of a—" He was breathing in the wrong shape.
"There's someone about. Down there. I've been—" He looked down at
himself and his hand came away black. "Oh," he said again, differently.

"Let me look."

"Yes."

Sazare put his hands on him.

---

They knew what they were doing. They had been taught for two years by a
man who did not lie, and they had done this eleven times on strangers
for nothing and once on a bargeman's split knuckles *proper, like a
barracks medic does it*, and they went to work now with the flat
unhurried competence of a trade.

He got the coat open. He found the low wound at the back first, the
knife wound, the one that was messy and slow and not going to kill
anybody by itself for six hours. He found the other one under the ribs
with two fingers, in the dark, by feel, and his hands told him the whole
of it in about four seconds, the way hands do: the depth, the angle,
what was under it, and the specific slack, wet, subterranean quality of
a wound that is filling a place that should not be filling.

He knew exactly what it was. He knew exactly where the pressure went. He
knew that the answer was a fist here, packed hard, high, under the arch
of the ribs and *held* — held for as long as it took, an hour if
necessary, while somebody ran — and that the man would very probably
survive it, because most dying is done waiting, and this was a river
port with a physician on Kanmuri Street and eleven thousand people
within a quarter-mile of the sound of a shout.

He put his fist in low and to the left, on the soft of the belly, and
pressed there, firmly, with great care, and held it.

It was the wrong place. It was three inches and one lifetime from the
right place. It looked, from any distance at all, exactly like a boy
doing everything he could.

"That's — " said Nakabori.

"I know."

"That's better."

"Don't talk."

---

It took about eleven minutes.

That was the other thing he had known before he did it, because he had
been taught it flatly at a bench: *there's no wound so urgent you'll
dress it better fainting. A body mends from the middle out. Most dying
is done waiting.* He had been taught how long a man has, in order to
know how fast to run.

Nakabori talked for eight of the eleven minutes, in the way of a man who
has decided a situation is under control and is therefore free to be
irritated about it.

He was extremely angry about his coat. He was angry, at some length,
about the harbourmaster, who did not put lamps on the gangway, and whose
predecessor had not either, and whose lamp oil was a scandal. He said
that whoever had done it had been after the purse, and that the purse
had twenty-four hundred ryō in it and no more, and that there was a joke in that
somewhere which he could not at present find.

At about the sixth minute he asked Sazare to make sure the tally on the
salt barge was counted personally in the morning, and Sazare said he
would, and Nakabori said *no, personally*, and Sazare said *personally*,
and Nakabori was satisfied.

At about the eighth minute he stopped being able to finish sentences and
started one about his daughter, and got as far as her name.

At about the ninth he got cold, all at once, the way they do, and said
so, and asked whether there was somebody coming, and Sazare said yes.

At about the tenth he took hold of Sazare's wrist — not the sleeve, the
wrist, over the burn-cloth, with the enormous grip of a man who counts
things for a living — and did not say anything else at all.

At the eleventh he stopped.

---

Sazare knelt on the stones in the dark for a while after that with his
fist still in the wrong place.

Then he took it away, and cleaned his hands on the inside of the dead
man's coat, which was correct, and found the purse and took it, which
was correct, and took the twenty-four hundred out and put the purse in the river,
which was correct, because a mugging has a purse missing and a killing
does not.

He found the fish-knife by touch after four minutes on his hands and
knees, six feet away, against the wall. He dropped it off the coping
into the deep channel by the cleat he had grown for the purpose and it
went down without any sound worth the name.

The lantern was lying on the stones.

Brass, ten years old, the pillar true, the collar packed and turned back
to the year it was made, and a hinge-plate that had been made on a
Tuesday by a boy with a bad knee and aged badly on purpose so that it
would read as a repair from six years ago.

He looked at it for a long moment.

Then he left it exactly where it was, because it was the dead man's
lantern and a mugger does not take a lantern, and because there was
nothing on it that said anything about anybody, and because it was
good work.

---

He was four streets away, walking normally, breathing normally, with
twenty-four hundred ryō in his coat and his ribs going like a bell, when the thing he
had been not-thinking about since the sixth second came up out of the
floor of him and stood in the road.

He stopped walking.

He held his right hand up in the dark and looked at the palm of it, and
there was grit on it, and the grit meant what grit had always meant on a
bench above a river for eight months.

A piece had failed at its note.

Which meant a piece had *broken*.

Which meant that the part of it that was still in Nakabori Sōhachi —
four inches under the ribs, hard, tapered, unmounted, unfaceted, with no
tool marks on it anywhere and a snapped base where it had left a hand —
was, right now, lying in a body in a doorway on the salt-side gangway of
a river port with eleven thousand people in it.

And that the body would be found at about the first hour.

And that a body found dead in a port is looked at by somebody.

Sazare Ruri stood alone in a street in Kanezaki with his hand up in front
of his face, and did the arithmetic he was incapable of not doing, and it
came out in less than a second, and it came out the same way it was
going to come out every time he did it for the rest of his life.
