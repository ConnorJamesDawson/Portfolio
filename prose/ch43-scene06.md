# Chapter 43, Scene 6 — What She Ruled

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the Hokage's office. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen.
POV: Sazare. ~1,986 words.*

---

The window was open this time.

He noticed it because the last time he had stood in that room at that
hour the frost had come up the inside of the glass in the small hours
and made the village into a white blur with four lights in it, and now
the sash was up eleven inches and the noise of the whole village came
in with the warm — carts on the east road, a dog, somebody's shutters,
the enormous layered sound of four thousand people ending a working
day. It smelled of dust off hot roof tiles and, faintly, of the lime
trees on the tower approach, which had four days left in them and
everybody in the village complained about and nobody would have cut
down.

Tsunade read the sheet.

She got about nine lines in and stopped.

"Whose words are these."

"Kanzaki's."

"They're at the top."

"Yes."

"Above yours."

"Yes."

She went on reading and did not say anything else about it, and he
stood, because she had not said sit and because he had learned that in
this room the invitation to sit was information.

---

She read the whole of it twice, the second time with a finger going
down the margin, and then she put it flat.

"Right," she said. "The scheme works."

"It works."

"It's clever, it's cheap, it costs nobody anything they'd notice, and
it would very probably give you a door inside nine months." She pushed
her chair back. "Kanzaki's objection is correct and I'm sustaining it,
and I want to be clear that I'm not sustaining it because it's tidier.
It's not tidier. Sustaining it costs me a name I would quite like to
have."

"I understand."

"You don't yet, but you will in about two minutes." She got up and went
to the window and stood in it with her back to him, arms folded, the
green haori over the chair as usual and the whole line of her shoulders
saying she was working rather than looking at anything. "Tell me the
objective."

"Identify the door the schedule went out of."

"That's the *method*." She turned round. "I asked for the objective."

He opened his mouth and found nothing in it.

"I'll do it, then. The objective is that hospital paper stops leaving
this village." Tsunade came back and put one hand flat on the desk.
"That's the whole of it. That's what I actually want, that's what
Ibiki actually wants, and it has been what everybody wanted since the
second month. And you have written me eleven hundred words about
identifying a door, because identifying a door is the interesting
problem and stopping the paper is the boring one."

The room went on being warm.

"You did the same thing to eleven people in a committee room nine days
ago," she said. "I heard about that within the hour, because Kaburagi
files, and I have been waiting for you to walk in here so I could put
the two of them next to each other. You led with the finding because
the finding was the interesting part. You led with the door because the
door is the interesting part. That is not a fault of judgement, it is a
*preference*, it is extremely consistent, and it is going to cost you
something significant about once a year for the rest of your life
unless you learn to see it firing."

"How do I see it firing."

"You've already built the answer to that and you built it in stone
first, which I find enormously funny and I'm not going to explain why."
She sat down. "Here's the ruling. Mark the paper. All eleven versions,
exactly as designed, tare weight in the third column, four grams
apart."

He waited.

"And announce it," said Tsunade. "Notice on every department board,
signed by me, next Monday. *The schedules issued from this building
carry a departmental provenance mark. Any document recovered outside
Konoha can be traced to the department it was issued from.* Word it so
a clerk understands it in one reading."

---

He stood with that for four or five seconds and then went at it,
because she paid him to.

"That destroys it. If they know the paper's marked, the paper stops
moving, and if the paper stops moving I never—"

"You never get a name. Correct."

"Then we've lost the only—"

"Say the sentence you were about to say," said Tsunade, "and hear it."

He heard it on the way out and closed his mouth about two words in.

*Then we've lost the only chance of finding out who it is.*

"The paper stops moving," he said, instead. "Which is the objective."

"Which is the objective, and you have just this second discovered that
you were about to argue against the thing you actually wanted because
it arrived by a route that bored you." She let him have that without
any particular mercy and then moved on, which he was grateful for.
"Now. The rest of it, which is the part you'd have got to yourself in
about a week."

"There's a rest of it."

"There is always a rest of it." She turned the sheet round. "Your
scheme assumes the leak is a member of staff and that telling them will
stop them. Suppose it doesn't. Suppose a schedule surfaces anyway, six
months after a notice signed by me went up on every board in that
building. What have you learned?"

He worked it.

"That whoever it is either can't stop," he said slowly, "or isn't
staff, or knows the marking doesn't matter because the material's
already gone. And I'd know it from the tare weight anyway, because the
mark's still in it."

"So you have not lost the trap."

"No. I've—" He stopped. "I've put a floor under it. It stops the ninety
per cent case, which is somebody careless or somebody being paid a
little, and anything that survives the notice is by definition the
serious version, and that's a much smaller thing to look for."

"And you announced it, so nobody in that hospital learns nine months
from now that you were quietly testing them. Which was Kanzaki's whole
objection, and which you'll notice I have not had to trade anything
away to satisfy." Tsunade signed the bottom of the sheet and blotted
it. "The constraint made it better. That's not a moral. That's just
what happened. Take it downstairs."

---

"There's a second thing," she said, when he was most of the way to the
door. "Sit down for it."

He sat down.

"You withdrew your study."

"It was a bad document."

"I know it was a bad document, I've read it, Kaburagi is right about
the six practitioners and she's right in a way that nobody who has not
stood at a bedside for twenty years could be." She put the pen in the
rest. "Do you know how many people have withdrawn a proposal in front
of that board in the eleven years it has existed?"

"No."

"Neither do I, but Kaburagi does, and she told me, and it's two." She
held up two fingers. "In eleven years. Everybody else defends it,
loses, redrafts the same document with the objections papered over, and
brings it back in the spring, and the board rejects it again and
everybody's honour is intact and nothing is learned by anybody."

"I didn't have an answer."

"That's what withdrawing *is*." She sat back. "Ōkubo came to see me. Do
you know what he said?"

"He asked to be one of the six. He told me not to repeat it."

"Then I won't tell you what else he said, because he asked me the same
thing, and you can have the fact that there was an *else*." She looked
at him for a moment. "Build it again with the six of them in the room
and I'll take it to the board myself. Not for you. With you. There's a
difference and it's worth about four votes."

---

He got up, and the shoulder went.

It had been going for two days — the outside of the left, from the
sixth committed fall on the Tuesday, stiffened in the way Kakashi had
told him it would stiffen and had described with what Sazare now
recognised as insufficient enthusiasm — and it caught somewhere at the
top of the movement and he made a small noise before he could stop
himself.

"Sit down," said Tsunade.

"It's nothing. It's a—"

"Sit *down*."

He sat down.

She came round the desk and put her hand on it without asking him
anything at all, and he understood in the abstract that this was
entirely ordinary, that she had done it perhaps eleven thousand times
in forty years, that she was the greatest medic alive and a stiff
shoulder in front of her was less than nothing, and that the correct
response was to hold still.

He held still.

Her hand was warm and there was the particular green nothing of a
diagnostic going through the joint, four seconds of it, and she said
"hm," and moved her thumb about an inch, and said, "Hatake's making you
fall."

"Yes."

"On the left."

"He says pick the place."

"He's right and he's also a sadist and I'll be telling him so." The
green went off. She did not take her hand away immediately; she pressed
twice with the flat of the thumb, hard, into something above the joint
that lit up the whole arm and then let go of it, and the stiffness
went with it. "There. It's a strained infraspinatus and it's from
landing right, which is a better complaint than the one you'd have if
you were landing wrong. Ice it and don't let Yagi see you favouring
it, she'll take him apart."

"Thank you."

"Get out." She was already going back round the desk. "And Ruri —
Monday's notice. Draft it yourself, bring it to Shizune by Thursday,
and put it in words a clerk understands in one reading and not in
yours."

---

He was four streets down the hill before it caught up with him.

He stopped by the wall of a shuttered dye-works with the sheet under
his arm and stood there for what he later established was rather more
than a minute, doing nothing whatsoever, which was not a thing he did.

*Chapter five.*

*Of the Hand and the Shoulder. Attempted never, in seven months, on
eleven people, with a state in the column that has read the same word
since the ninth month.*

*And it arrived from the other direction, in about four seconds,
without being asked for and without anybody deciding anything, from a
woman who did it because a joint was wrong and because she has been
putting her hands on people since before my mother was born.*

*It was a diagnostic. That is what it was. I want that written first
because it is the true thing and everything after it is the part I
cannot account for.*

He made himself walk again.

*I have been trying for seven months to learn a language of about nine
words from a book, on the grounds that I could not acquire it any other
way.*

*It did not occur to me once, in seven months, that I have been
speaking it fluently and professionally since I was fifteen, and that
the only place I cannot speak it is the one place where the person is
not a patient.*

*Two complete systems, I wrote, in the ninth month. Neither of them is
the one everybody else has.*

*That was correct and it was not the finding. The finding is that they
are the same system and there is a governor on one of them, and I did
not put it there, and I do not know who did or when.*
