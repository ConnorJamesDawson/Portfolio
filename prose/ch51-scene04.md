# Chapter 51, Scene 4 — Six Seconds

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,763 words.*

---

She came through the gate at four minutes past nine on the fourteenth,
on foot, in the green haori, with the protocol in her hand.

The tenth month had turned properly in the second week and the yard was
cold for the first time since the spring. Clear, no wind, the sacking
over the far bench stiff with it, and their breath showing under the
lamp. Kanzaki had gone home at six and had left, without comment, the
stool at the end of bench three with a lamp on it, which she had done
every evening since the twenty-second of the eighth month and had
denied doing on two separate occasions.

Tsunade stopped four feet inside the gate and looked at the yard.

"You've got the sacking up."

"You told me to."

"I did." She came over. "Katsuyu."

"Tsunade-sama."

"Are you well?"

There was a pause of about a second and a half.

"...Yes," said Katsuyu. "Thank you. Nobody has asked me that in some
time and I did not expect it this evening and I am afraid I have taken
too long to answer."

Tsunade did not say anything to that at all.

---

She read the protocol standing up, under the lamp, all of it, and it
took her six minutes and nobody spoke.

Then she turned to the second sheet and read that again, which was the
one with the heading on it.

"This is new."

"Yes."

"You've never written one."

"I've written nine," said Sazare. "All of them hospital documents, all
of them for patients, and I built a doctrine on top of them that
doesn't say the word *patient* anywhere in it, and it took me until the
sixteenth of the eighth month sitting in a room in your building to
notice that I had never once put myself in a paper. The protocol on the
fifteenth had a risk line reading *none identified* and I underlined
it."

"I know. I read it." She turned the sheet. "*Petrification, from the
fingertips, painless, decelerating and not certainly self-limiting.*"

"Yes."

"*Arrested at the carpus after six hours of work by the Hokage, which
is not a resource that exists twice and which I am not entitled to
assume.*"

"That's the important line."

"It is the important line and I want to know whether you believe it or
whether you wrote it," said Tsunade, "because those are different and I
have watched a great many people write that sentence."

Sazare looked at her.

"I believe it," he said. "You went to the window five times. The last
one didn't empty properly and you came back and put your hands flat on
the table before you picked my wrist up, and I saw you do it and I did
not say anything at the time and I have thought about it approximately
every week since."

Tsunade folded the protocol.

"Right," she said. "Go on then."

---

He did what the protocol said.

Rose seven out of the box, dried, fourteen months, saturation
unchanged on every test to date. Left palm — the same hand, which was
in the protocol, with the reasoning, because the right had never done
it and this was not the evening to introduce a variable.

Katsuyu on the felt at his elbow, and then not on the felt.

*In it.*

He had done that eleven times in five days and it had not stopped being
extraordinary: the sensation of a second thing in the same place doing
the same job, at a tenth of the effort, with no hauling in it anywhere.

He shut his eyes.

He matched.

It came in about four seconds — faster than the eighth month, and he
noted that and put it away — and there was green in his palm, and he
did not let it grow, because the protocol said *the smallest thing I
can produce, a stem is sufficient and a flower is not required, and I
am specifying that in advance because I know what I did last time.*

About an inch and a half. One node. No leaves.

He held it for a count of two.

Then he closed it.

---

It shut.

That was all. It shut the way a door shuts, immediately and completely
and without any drama in it at all, and the draw stopped, and the green
in his palm stopped being connected to anything, and there was an inch
and a half of stem lying across his hand which was simply an object.

Six seconds, start to finish.

He opened his eyes.

"That's it," he said, and his voice came out wrong.

Nobody said anything.

He sat down on the yard stone, not because his legs had gone but
because there was suddenly nothing to stand up for, and turned his left
hand over under the lamp and looked at it: the patch on the heel the
size of a large coin, going deep, no report, skin whorls intact, and
above it four fingers and a thumb that were warm and pink and entirely
his.

*It's the same close.*

*It is not a door for each room. It is one door and I have had it since
I was seven and I have used it four hundred thousand times without
knowing, and it works on cellulose exactly as well as it works on
silicate, and I have been carrying it around inside my hands for eleven
years like a key I did not know was in my pocket.*

*Eight in ten. I said eight in ten and I wrote it down and I have never
in my life been more pleased to have underestimated something.*

"Ruri," said Tsunade.

"Sorry. Give me a second."

"Take four."

---

He took four.

Then he wrote it up, on the spot, at the bench, because that was the
protocol and the protocol had a line for it — *record before
discussion, in case discussion is interesting* — and it took him
ninety seconds and Tsunade stood and let him do it and did not say a
word.

*14/10, 21:19. Rose 7 as reference. Match at approx. 4 s, faster than
15/8. Product: approx. 38 mm stem, one node, no leaves, per
specification. Held 2 s. Close fired on the first attempt, deliberate,
complete, no hesitation.*

*Finding: the close is general to the operation and not specific to the
medium.*

*Operator: no petrification, no draw after close, no sensation of
anything continuing. Hand unchanged. Left forearm slightly tired, which
is ordinary.*

*Duration, contact to close: six seconds.*

He put the pencil down.

"Do it again," said Tsunade.

---

He did it again.

He knew why she was asking and approved of it and would have asked the
same thing, and he said so, and she said "I know you would, do it
anyway," and he did.

The second one was five seconds and the close fired and there was
another inch and a half of stem on the felt.

She did not watch his hand.

That was the thing he registered afterwards and could not do anything
with at the time. On the first one she had watched the palm, which was
where everything was happening. On the second one she stood at the end
of the bench with her arms folded and watched **all** of him — the
shoulders, the breathing, the way he sat, the four seconds before he
reached — and she did it the way he watched a casting.

When it was done she said, "Again, and this time in the right hand."

"That's a variable."

"I know it is. Do it."

He did it in the right hand.

It took six seconds and the close fired and nothing whatsoever
happened, and he sat there with a third piece of stem in a hand that
had never done it before, and he understood in about a second and a
half what she had just established and had not asked him about.

*It isn't practice in the left.*

*It isn't the patch. It isn't the route the fifteenth cut. It is not
localised anywhere and it never was.*

*It's me.*

---

Tsunade came and sat down on the low wall.

She did that slowly and she did it in the green haori in a cold yard at
half past nine at night, and she looked at the three pieces of stem on
the felt for a while.

"How long have you been able to do that," she said.

"That was the first time."

"That's not what I asked."

Sazare stopped.

"...No," he said. "It isn't."

"How long have you been able to hold?"

He did not answer.

Not out of concealment. He had the answer immediately and completely,
in a column at the bottom of a page in the ordinary place, and what
stopped him was that he had never once said it out loud to anybody and
had not, until that second, understood that he had been not saying it.

"Show me the sphere," said Tsunade.

---

He put his palms eight inches apart.

He did it the way he had done it every night for twenty-one weeks —
sitting on a floor with his back against a wall, ninety attempts a
week, a number at the bottom of a page under the interesting things —
and he had no idea in the world why she was asking.

It came up at once.

"Count it," she said.

"One."

"Out loud."

"...Two. Three. Four."

He got to eleven and stopped, because eleven was where it stopped
feeling like holding and started being the other thing, and he had
never gone past it in front of anybody and had never gone past it at
all.

He let it go and looked up.

Tsunade was sitting on the low wall in a cold yard with her hands on
her knees, and her face had done the thing it did perhaps twice a
season, where the entire working apparatus went still all at once so
that what was left was not an expression but the absence of one.

"Eleven," she said.

"Yes."

"On the twenty-fourth of the fifth month you told me you had a count of
two. In this yard. You said it was a schoolbook exercise for
eleven-year-olds and you had nine hundred and eighty attempts and a
count of two and you were not going to tell anybody the number."

"That's right."

"It is the fourteenth of the tenth month," said Tsunade.

She did not raise her voice at all.

"Who has been teaching you?"
