# Chapter 73, Scene 6 — Four Seconds

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, Tsunade's house. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen.
POV: Sazare. ~1860 words.*

---

He read the room in eleven seconds and it undid him faster than
anything that happened afterwards.

Four rooms, three of them empty of any evidence that a person
lived here. A bed made with the corners squared by somebody who
made a bed she did not expect to sleep in. One shelf, holding
nothing. No photographs — not one, in a whole house, belonging
to a woman with a hundred and eleven years of family behind
her. A lamp on the nightstand, new, still smelling faintly of
its own oil, standing on a ring of dustless wood four inches
across in a house where every other surface had a fortnight on
it.

*She bought a lamp. This week. For this room.*

*Three days ago she stood in this room and decided the light in
it was wrong, and went out and bought a lamp, and set it there,
and then went back to the tower and ran a country for two days
without saying one word about it to anybody, including me,
including — I would put money on this — including herself.*

And beside the lamp, a glass of water with three weeks of dust
on its surface, which was the loneliest object he had ever
been in a room with.

He did not say anything about any of it. That was the entire
gift he had to give in that first minute — the not-saying, the
choosing not to read her house out loud at her the way he had
read four hundred people off walls — and he stood in the door
of the bedroom of the fifth Hokage of the Land of Fire and
made the deliberate decision, one of the few genuinely
strategic acts of his life, to be, for one evening, a man with
no instruments at all.

"You've read the room," said Tsunade, behind him. Not a
question. Her voice had gone quiet in a way that had nothing
to do with volume.

"Yes."

"And?"

"And it is the first room I have ever been in that is worse
at being lived in than mine was," said Sazare, and turned
around, "and I have been in it once before, in the dark, on
the eleventh of the eleventh, carrying you, and I did not
look at anything then because I was frightened, and I have
thought about this room every week since." He made himself
hold her eyes, which cost what it always cost. "It is not a
sad room. It is a room somebody kept in case. And I would
like to be the case."

The sound she made was not a word.

And then she crossed the four feet of bare floor and kissed
him, and the whole careful architecture of the evening — the
terms, the papers, the eleven seconds of survey, the two of
them being witty at each other in a garden — came off its
bearings and went into the sea.

---

Everything after that arrived in the wrong order and stayed
that way.

Her hands in his hair and the wall against his back and no
memory of crossing the room. The lacquered sticks going onto
the nightstand — she took them out herself, one and then the
other, without looking, and her hair came down over her
shoulders and he made a sound he had never made in his life
and she smiled against his mouth with her eyes shut. His coat
somewhere. The buttons of her working shirt, which he could
not manage, which he had rendered in crystal-graded tolerance
on a fitting form eight weeks ago and could not manage now,
his hands shaking so hard that she had to put her own over
them and hold them still against her sternum for a second —
"breathe, love, *breathe*" — before they'd work.

*Love.* She had never once said that word to him as an
address. He filed it in the place he was going to keep
everything from this hour and had no more capacity for
filing after that, because his shirt went over his head and
her mouth found the scar on his forearm — wrist to elbow,
three exits, the only record of the square he carried — and
she kissed the length of it, all of it, without hurry and
without comment, and something in his chest broke open that
had been sealed for eleven years.

Skin. The extraordinary heat of another person's whole
skin. The small ordinary catastrophe of a lamp-lit shoulder
under his palm; the weight of her; the sound she made when
his mouth found her throat, which rearranged his priorities
permanently. He was aware, distantly, of his own body doing
things it had no procedure for — the shaking that would not
stop, the heart going like a hammer on hot iron, the blood
in his ears — and aware that none of it was fear, and
aware, above all, at close range in the lamplight, that
she was frightened.

That was the thing that steadied him. That was the finding
of the entire evening.

*She is fifty-three and she has not done this in decades and
she is frightened of being looked at, and she is doing it
anyway, in a house she keeps in case, with a lamp she bought
on Thursday.*

*So look at her. Properly. It is the one thing you are
actually good at.*

"Hime," he said, and took her face in both hands, and made
himself say it in the level voice, with the copper going up
his throat and the whole of him coming apart at every joint,
because a woman who is frightened of being looked at should
be told exactly what the looking finds. "You are the most
beautiful thing I have ever been permitted to be in a room
with. Every part. Every year. I have looked at nine hundred
articles and I have never once seen anything I wanted to
keep looking at for the rest of my life, and I am looking
at it, and I am not going to stop."

Tsunade made a sound like something giving way, and pulled
him down onto a made bed in a house she had kept in case
for eleven years.

---

It lasted about four seconds.

There was — afterwards he could reconstruct it, and did, for
the rest of his life, in the one file nobody would ever read
— a period of perhaps ninety seconds in which the world was
her, entirely, closer than any survey, warm and shaking and
saying his name in a register he had never heard from any
human being; and then the four seconds; and then the whole
of him went off like a struck bell and the room went white
and he was somewhere with no instruments in it at all.

When he came back she was underneath him with her arms
around his ribs, and his face was in her hair, and he was
saying something that was not words, and his entire body was
shaking in long waves he could not stop and did not want
to.

Then his brain came back online, in the specific manner of a
department reopening after a fire, and delivered its
assessment.

*Four seconds.*

"...Four seconds," said Sazare, out loud, into her hair, in
the flat clinical voice, because that was the only voice he
had ever had for a catastrophe. "Approximately. I had no
instrumentation. I am sorry. I have never — that has never
happened to me before, at all, in any form, and I did not
know it would be — that was—"

He could not finish any of the sentences. He raised his head
and looked down at her in the lamplight with his heart still
going and mortification arriving behind it like a second
wave.

Tsunade was looking up at him with her hair everywhere and
her face flushed and something enormous and gentle happening
around her eyes.

And then she laughed.

Not at him. He knew the difference by now — three months of
the finest laughter data in the Land of Fire and he could
have graded it in his sleep — this was the low one, the one
from under the bathwater, the one with her whole
fifty-three years in it and none of their weight, and it
went up through both of them where they were joined and
undid the last stitch of the mortification.

"*Four seconds*," she said. "You *counted*."

"I count everything—"

"You *counted*." She was shaking with it now, both arms
still around him, entirely unbothered, gloriously unbothered
— and then she got hold of his face with both hands and made
him look at her, and the flat administrative voice arrived,
warm as the lamp, and delivered the most merciful sentence
anybody had ever said to him: "Fabricator. Listen to me
very carefully, because I am the ranking medical authority
in this room and this is a clinical finding. Attempt one is
not charted as a failure."

He stared at her.

"It's charted as a *baseline*."

*Oh.*

"Day one," said Tsunade, quoting a white-haired man in a
frozen field with immense satisfaction, "first exercise,
complete."

"That is—" He had to stop. His voice had gone entirely.
"That is Jiraiya-sama's."

"That is his, and it was correct in the frost and it is
correct in my bed, and I'd like it minuted that I have
finally found a use for something that man said." She
pulled his forehead down against hers. "Now. Second finding,
and this one is mine, and it is the single greatest
clinical advantage in this room and you are going to
appreciate it in about eleven minutes." Her thumb moved
once along his jaw. "You are *eighteen*. Do you have any
idea what the interval is at eighteen? I have decades on
you, love. I know exactly what your body is going to do
next, and so, if you think about it for four seconds
instead of apologising, do you."

He thought about it.

He went, comprehensively, scarlet.

"There it is," said Tsunade, delighted. "There's my
copper."

"...Graduated exposure," said Sazare, faintly. "I wrote a
protocol. In January. About a *book*. Increments, and the
first dose is not the measure of the—"

"You wrote a protocol about a book," she agreed,
"and every single word of it was true, and it was never
about the book, and here we are." She kissed him, slowly,
and he felt the whole architecture of the night stop being
a disaster and start being a *method*, which was the only
country he had ever known how to live in. "So. The night's
young, the door's locked, nobody in this village knows
where either of us is, and I have eleven years of dust in
this house and eleven minutes on my hands."

"Hime—"

"*Try again*, fabricator," said Tsunade, against his
mouth. "That's the whole of the science. You know this
part. You've built your entire life out of it."

And the lamp burned on the nightstand beside a glass of
water with three weeks of dust on it, and in a house that
had been kept in case for eleven years, they tried again.
