# Chapter 25, Scene 2 — Eleven

*Act 4, part B. Konoha, the hospital. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen.
POV: Sazare. ~2,039 words.*

---

The hospital's disaster procedure occupied one side of a yellow card behind
the admissions desk and had been written by somebody who assumed the building
would be full of the people named on the staffing sheet.

Nurse Emiko read it once, turned it over as if the missing half might be on
the back, and began crossing out names.

"Matsuda is at the arena. Yagi's at the arena. Kaburagi's at the arena. Fujita
has the fever ward and does not leave it. Kōda—"

"Arena," said the porter.

"I know where Kōda is. I put him there." She drew another line. "Ashida-san,
can you carry?"

"Yes."

"Can you carry without being told what the object is worth?"

Ashida took off his flak jacket and laid it over the back of the chair.
"Probably."

"Good. Ruri, clear the west treatment room. Everything that isn't fixed down
goes against the north wall. Leave the oxygen rack. Then bring every empty
stretcher to the entrance."

"There are six."

"Bring six."

"One has a split rail."

"Bring five and the straps from the sixth."

She did not ask whether he could do it. He liked her for that and had no time
to think about why.

The first casualty reached the doors while he was carrying the fourth
stretcher down the stairs. A genin in a green festival marshal's band had a
senbon through the meat below her collarbone and blood down to her belt. She
walked in under her own power, said, "The stadium's under attack," and fainted
with sufficient precision to miss the edge of the admissions desk.

After that the building stopped being quiet.

The change did not happen all at once. It happened one object at a time.

The umbrella stand was put outside. The two waiting-room benches were moved
against the walls. The admissions clerk took the vase off her counter, poured
the water into the sink and filled it with used scissors. Somebody opened the
emergency linen cupboard and left the key in it. The smell of floor soap went
under blood, burnt cloth and the sweet medicinal paste used on open burns.

Sazare carried the split stretcher to the sluice, cut away its canvas and
removed both straps. The timber had failed at a knot halfway down the right
rail. In a workshop he would have scarfed in a new length, clamped it
overnight and refused to put a person on it until morning. He had three
minutes and a cupboard of cleaning handles. He selected an ash handle nearly
the same diameter as the rail, split it lengthways against the edge of the
sink and bound half beneath the break with wet bandage, two buckles and the
wire from a hanging label.

The porter watched him lean his full weight into the middle.

"You said five," he said.

"There are six now."

"Is it safe?"

"No. Put light people on it."

They wrote LIGHT across the canvas in iodine because the stationery had
already gone downstairs.

The first six casualties used every treatment surface. The seventh went on a
blanket on the floor. By the tenth, the west room had become red-tag
treatment, the waiting room had become yellow, and the covered passage beside
the kitchen had become the place for people who were breathing and could wait.
Green tags were sent to the laundry yard with one academy student, a bucket
and instructions not to leave unless the roof caught fire.

There was nowhere assigned to black.

Nurse Emiko made one by drawing the screen across the alcove beside the
stairs.

Sazare carried three people behind it. The first had no pulse when he arrived.
The second had a pulse that stopped while Sazare's fingers were on it. The
third was the man from the shutter. His abdomen had become hard beneath the
blood and the duty medic looked once, pressed twice, and shook his head.

"Not with one pair of hands," he said.

The strangers who had carried the shutter heard him. One began arguing. The
one whose fingers had locked round the compress did not. He remained bent over
the wound until Sazare placed a hand against each of his knuckles and opened
them one at a time.

"You kept him alive to the door," Sazare said.

"And then?"

There was no answer to the question asked that was also an answer to the
question meant.

"Then there was one medic," Sazare said.

The man looked at him as if he had been struck. Nurse Emiko sent Sazare for
blankets before he could make it worse.

Upstairs, the ordinary ward continued. Bed three still needed breathing
exercises. Bed twelve still needed a bedpan. The patients heard the feet below
and the alarm bells outside and asked questions the staff could not answer.
Sazare told them what he knew: the village was under attack, the hospital
remained open, nobody had ordered an evacuation, breakfast would be late.

"How late?" Handa asked.

"I don't know."

"Bad, then."

"Yes."

Handa looked past him at the other beds. "If we do have to move?"

"You go on a carry sheet."

"Undignified."

"You can have dignity or a pelvis."

"Pelvis, probably."

Sazare checked the repaired window latch before he went back downstairs. The
frame had not shifted in the first tremor. The gauze packing remained
compressed evenly along the runner.

It was holding.

They came in ones and twos at first. A civilian with burns along one arm. A
chūnin whose left sandal left a complete red print on every third tile. Two
children separated from their parents and neither injured, which cost a nurse
for eight minutes because there was nowhere else to put them. A man carried on
a shutter by three strangers, one of whom had kept pressure on an abdominal
wound with both hands and could not make his fingers release when told.

The procedure card divided casualties into four colours. They had no coloured
tags. Sazare tore four pages from the stationery pad, cut them into strips
with bandage scissors and wrote the colour names on them in block capitals,
because the paper itself was all white.

"That defeats the point," Ashida said, passing with the oxygen rack.

"They can read."

"Some of them are unconscious."

"The staff can read."

"Most of them are at the arena."

"Then it doesn't matter what colour the paper is."

Ashida opened his mouth, shut it, and carried the rack where he had been told.

At nine minutes past nine a masked shinobi landed in the forecourt, opened the
front doors and shouted that the village was under coordinated attack by Sand
and Sound. He had a cut across his mask and another across the hand holding
the door.

"How many coming?" Nurse Emiko asked.

"All of them."

"That isn't a number."

"I don't have one."

"Then bring me the ones you do have."

He left again.

Sazare worked in the treatment room. He did not count the time. He counted
breaths, dressings, compresses, hands available and hands not available. He
closed a four-inch cut on the outside of a man's thigh badly enough to leave a
raised ridge and well enough to stop it bleeding. He held a child's shoulder
still while Emiko found and tied an artery. He carried the shutter into the
west room and the man on it out again under a sheet.

Somewhere during the next half-hour, Ashida put his flak jacket back on.

"East road," he said when Sazare saw it. "Three reports. Armed movement coming
uphill."

"Konoha?"

"Not all of it."

The front doors stood open. Beyond them the forecourt fell by five shallow
steps to an iron gate and a strip of road wide enough for two carts. The first
organised casualty party had appeared at the bottom: four shinobi with two
stretchers between them, moving backwards while another pair covered the
street.

A roof tile broke on the paving beside the gate.

The second projectile went through the shoulder of the shinobi at the rear
stretcher.

He dropped his end. The stretcher struck the road. The person on it made no
sound.

"Shut the doors," said the porter.

"No," said Emiko.

"They're shooting at the entrance."

"Yes."

"Then—"

"If we shut it, they die outside more tidily. Ruri, how many upstairs?"

"Eleven who cannot walk."

"How long to move them?"

He had already worked it out. "With everybody here, fourteen minutes to the
rear yard. With current staff, thirty-six. Longer once the stairs fill."

Another tile burst. Not a tile: stone, thrown hard enough to chip the gate
pier.

The covering shinobi in the road looked back at the dropped stretcher, then
uphill towards the shapes moving over the roofs. He had to choose between the
living person at his feet and the hospital behind him.

Sazare took the shallow tin out of his pocket.

Ashida saw it. "No."

"The stretcher is twenty-two yards away."

"Your terms say—"

"I know what they say."

"You go through that gate and I have to stop you."

"Then come with me."

Sazare handed him the foot of an empty stretcher.

Ashida looked at it, swore once, and took hold.

They went down the steps together.

The first five yards were covered by the hospital wall. After that there was
the gate, the road and nothing with the decency to stand still. The Konoha
pair in the road threw wire between two gutter posts. A Sound shinobi came
over it rather than through it, turning in the air with a blade held backwards
along his forearm. One of the defenders met him on the wall. Their feet struck
plaster and did not fall.

Sazare had seen shinobi fight before. He had seen them from under floors,
through rain, from the wrong end of an alley. This was the first time one had
landed six feet away while he was holding hospital equipment.

The blade went into the Konoha defender below the ribs.

Ashida let go of the stretcher with one hand and drew a kunai. He was late.
Sazare was later. The Sound shinobi pulled his blade free and turned towards
them.

Sazare put his left hand on the iron gatepost.

The crystal came off the mineral in the mortar because the iron had nothing
useful in it. It grew blue-grey and cloudy, a sloped course from knee height
to shoulder height, too thick at the bottom and ragged where his concentration
broke. The Sound shinobi's kunai struck it and drove a white star into the
surface.

The first course held.

Sazare opened the tin with his thumb. The grains were too small to distinguish
through the cloth. He pinched one, pressed it into the wet edge and pushed.

The structure took the order as if it had been waiting.

Second course.

The tick came at four seconds, sharp enough to feel through the heel of his
hand. Ordinarily the whole face would have crazed after it. This time the
crack reached the seed and went around it. The wall bowed, recovered, and
held.

"Move," Sazare said.

Ashida had not stopped. He dragged the empty stretcher into the road, kicked
its legs flat and dropped beside the casualty. The wounded carrier was trying
to rise with one arm hanging loose. Ashida caught him by the harness and
pulled him behind the crystal.

Five seconds.

Something struck the other side with the flat boom of a paper bomb going off
too close.

Six.

The wall failed.

It did not become grit.

It divided along three pale lines and fell in pieces large enough to break
feet. One section remained wedged against the post. Another went flat into the
road. The third spun once and stopped by Sazare's sandal.

The Sound shinobi stepped through the opening, astonishment lasting exactly as
long as it took him to see that his target was still there.

Sazare bent because the next strike was already coming. His hand closed around
the fallen piece.

The ordered part of it touched him before the surface did: a small, familiar
resistance inside the larger cold weight, intact where there should have been
nothing but failure.

He had fifty-one minutes of work in his hand.

He also had less than one second.
