# Chapter 22, Scene 2 — Consistent

*Act 4, part A. Konoha, the intake block. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

It took nine days and it was nothing whatever like anything he had
imagined.

Ibiki's method, as far as Sazare could reconstruct it — and he spent a
great deal of the nine days reconstructing it, because he could not stop
and because it was the most interesting thing that had ever been done to
him — was repetition and shuffling.

Six hours a day. The same ground, over and over, but never in the same
order. On the first day it went chronologically from the compound
forward. On the second it went backward from Kasado. On the fourth
morning Ibiki spent two hours entirely on money — every sum Sazare had
ever held, where it came from, where it went, what a room cost at Yumoto,
what a tenth of four hundred thousand is and who counted it — and then
came back after the meal and asked him to describe the drying frames at
his mother's workshop, in detail, for forty minutes, and then said,
"Right. The eight hundred your friend's mother gave you. Was that before
or after you went to Kuromizu?"

"I never went to Kuromizu."

"You said you did on Tuesday."

"I said I *didn't*, because it was four miles and I had eleven hundred
ryō. You've written it down wrong or you're checking."

"I'm checking," said Ibiki, without any inflection at all, and wrote
something down.

He never raised his voice once. He never threatened Sazare with anything.
Twice he said something so exactly calculated to open him up that Sazare
felt the shape of it going in — once about his mother's hands and once
about what he had said to Inari — and both times he saw it coming and it
worked anyway, precisely as advertised, and both times Ibiki simply waited
out the four or five minutes it took him to get his face back and then
carried on from the same place.

On the sixth day Sazare said, "You're not trying to catch me lying."

Ibiki put the pen down.

"No," he said. "Go on."

"You'd have done it differently. If you thought I was making it up you'd
push on the parts I'm least sure of, and you don't, you go round them.
You've spent nine hours on money and about four minutes on the killings,
and the killings are the only part anybody would care about." Sazare
turned it over as he said it, which was how he did everything. "You're
checking whether it's *learned*."

"And?"

"A story somebody's been given comes out in the order they were given it,"
said Sazare. "So you keep changing the order and seeing whether the joins
move."

"Mm," said Ibiki. "And how am I getting on?"

"You've had nine days and you're still here."

"That's right," said Ibiki. "Which means I've got a boy whose account
doesn't move whichever way I come at it, and who is either telling me the
truth or is the best-prepared asset anybody has ever sent into this
building, and there is no question I can ask that separates those two, and
there never has been, and this is why I hate my job."

---

The Yamanaka came on the seventh day and Sazare asked for him.

That was the part he never regretted and never entirely got over.

He had worked it out on the fifth night, on the bench, in the dark: that
Konoha had, somewhere in it, people who went into heads, because everybody
had heard of that even in a lower district in the fog; and that if the
whole difficulty was that nothing he said could be checked, then there was
exactly one thing in that building that could check it, and it was
attached to him.

So he asked. And Ibiki looked at him for a while and said, "You understand
what you're volunteering for," and Sazare said no, obviously, and Ibiki
said, "No. Well," and sent for him anyway.

His name was Yamanaka Inoichi and he was about forty-five, and after Ibiki
he was almost absurdly unfrightening: tall, straight-backed, with long
pale blond hair pulled back off a high forehead into a tail down his back,
and blue eyes, and the sort of face that is used to being looked at. He
had a green jacket like everybody else and very clean hands, and he came
in and sat down and said good morning as though they had met at a counter.

"Has anybody explained it to you?"

"No."

"Right. I'd rather do that properly, because most of what people are told
is wrong." He put his hands flat on the table. "I do not read your
thoughts. There is no such thing. What I do is go into your memory and
look at things — and I have to know roughly what I'm looking for, and I
have to go where the memory actually is, and memory is not a shelf, it is
a heap that somebody has been rummaging in for sixteen years."

"So you'd see—"

"Whatever is nearest to what I'm reaching for," said Inoichi. "Which is
frequently not what either of us wanted. That is the first thing. The
second thing is that you will be there. It is not like sleeping. You will
go where I go and you will be *in* it, at the intensity it was originally
laid down at, and if I have to go somewhere bad you will have that day
again, in full, and I cannot make it less than it was."

Sazare sat with that.

"And afterwards?"

"Afterwards, in my experience, people are not right for about a week."

"All right," said Sazare.

"You can withdraw consent up to the moment I start and I will not think a
single thing about it, and neither will Morino, whatever he says."

"I know. I'm not going to."

Inoichi looked at him for a moment with an expression Sazare could not
place, and put a hand out over the table, palm up.

"Then put your hands there," he said, "and I'm sorry."

---

It took twenty-two minutes and Sazare was not right for eleven days.

He never described it to anybody in his life. The nearest he ever got,
long afterwards, to a man in a bed with nothing to do and a talent for
good questions, was:

*You know how you can be walking along thinking about something else and
you go past a doorway and a smell comes out of it and you're nine years
old? It was that. But it was somebody else choosing the doorways, and they
had a list, and they went down it.*

He had the square. All of it, at the intensity it was laid down at, with
the fog and the boards and the sound the crowd made; and there was
somebody in there with him, standing slightly behind and to the left,
being extremely careful, the way a man with clean hands is careful lifting
a dressing off, and it made not the slightest difference.

He had his mother's hands on a tray.

He had Kohaku on a step in the dark saying *no*, and *no*, and *yeah*.

He had a woman standing in eighteen inches of cold water at the top of the
ebb with a scull across her forearm saying *then they've had you off
somebody too*, and he had the three minutes after it, all of them, every
second, and he heard himself make a noise in an interview room in Konoha
and could not stop making it.

And at some point near the end he had a beach in the fog and a boy in a
grey cloak saying *would you have said yes*, and the answer coming out of
him before the question had finished.

Then it stopped, and he was sitting in a chair with his hands on a table
and somebody's palm under them, and the light through the barred window
had moved about four inches.

Inoichi Yamanaka had gone white to the lips.

He got up and went and stood at the window with his back to the room for
about a minute and a half, and Ibiki, in the corner, said nothing at all
and did not write anything down; and Sazare sat in the chair shaking and
understood, distantly, through the noise, that he had just watched a
professional need a moment, and that this was probably not a common event.

---

They gave him the rest of that day and the next one, and on the ninth
morning both of them came in together.

"Consistent," said Ibiki. "That's the word that goes on the paper. Every
verifiable thing you told me over six days is where you said it was, and
the unverifiable parts sit correctly in the middle of the verifiable ones,
which is harder to fake than people think and is about as far as it goes."

"That's all?"

"That's all it *can* be." He said it without any apology. "Now listen,
because you volunteered for something on the assumption that it would
settle the question, and it doesn't, and you deserve to know why rather
than think we're being obstinate.

"A placed asset believes his cover. That's the whole trade. If somebody
had grown you for eleven years and put you on that bridge, they would not
have handed you a script; they would have given you a *life* — a real
mother, a real workshop, a real dead sister, all of it true, all of it laid
down in a real child, and one instruction that nobody thinks about until
it's needed. And Inoichi would have walked through exactly what he has just
walked through, and come out, and said *consistent*.

"There is no technique in the world that reads intent. There is none that
reads a thing which hasn't happened yet. Everything we have tells us what a
man remembers, and every word of what you remember is either true or was
built by somebody a great deal cleverer than either of us, and I cannot
tell the difference and neither can he and neither can anybody, and that
is the actual state of the art."

"So it was for nothing."

"It was not for nothing," said Ibiki. "It went from *unverified* to
*consistent*, and that is one full grade, and it is the difference between
a hearing and no hearing. It just isn't proof, and you had better stop
expecting anybody to give you proof, because you will be waiting the rest
of your life."

---

Inoichi stayed behind when Ibiki went out, and stood by the table with his
hands in his sleeves.

"I owe you something," he said, "and I'd like to pay it now, because it
gets harder later."

"You don't owe me anything. I asked."

"You asked, and I took, and a man who takes without giving anything back
is a thief whatever the paperwork says." He said it flatly, in the manner
of somebody quoting a rule he had been given young. "So: one true thing
about what I saw. Not the file. Mine."

Sazare put his hands on the table.

"Go on."

"I went in looking for a handler," said Inoichi. "That is what I am for.
Twenty-one years, and it is nearly always there and it is nearly always in
the same place: there is a *shape* to a person who is working for
somebody. Not a memory — a sort of architecture. Somewhere in them there
is a direction that everything else is arranged around, and you find it
the way you find the fall of a floor by putting a marble down.

"You haven't got one."

"I know. I told Morino that on the first—"

"No," said Inoichi. "I don't think you do know, and I have spent two days
working out how to say it.

"I did not find a handler. I also did not find a village, or a clan, or an
order, or a cause, or a debt, or a god, or one single living person you
answer to. There is nobody in there. Not hiding — *absent*. I have been
into deserters and prisoners and half-dead men out of four countries and I
have never once been in a person with nothing in the middle of them, and I
came out onto that floor and had to stand at that window because I did not
know a person could be built that way and go on working."

He said it without pity, which was the only reason Sazare was able to sit
through it.

"You have got a habit instead," said Inoichi. "You keep everything in
pieces on purpose so it can't make a shape. I could see you doing it while
I was in there — I went past a thing about a factor at Kanezaki and you
put your hand on it, in front of me, and moved it. Which is remarkable,
and you invented it yourself, and it has kept you alive."

"But."

"But it is a splint, not a bone," said Inoichi Yamanaka. "It is holding
because you are sixteen. It will hold for a while. And one day you will be
somewhere quiet, with nothing to do and no work in front of you, and it
will all come out of that tray at once and lie down on the floor in order
— and there will be nobody in the middle of you to stand up and deal with
it."

He went to the door.

"That is not a diagnosis and I am not offering to fix it, and if I said I
could I would be lying. It is the true thing, and it is yours now, and I
would rather you had it early." A pause. "Get somebody into the middle of
you. Anything. A person, a place, a trade — I don't much care what it is
and neither will it. Only don't leave it empty for another four years."

He went out.

Sazare sat in an interview room in the largest military settlement in the
world with a receipt in his pocket for a coat, an empty picture frame and
three hundred and sixty thousand ryō, and a piece of paper somewhere with
the word *consistent* on it; and thought about a bench above a river, and
a tray with the parts kept loose in it, and did not sleep that night
either.
