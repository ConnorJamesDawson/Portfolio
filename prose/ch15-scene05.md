# Chapter 15, Scene 5 — The Price of Eleven Seconds

*Act 2, part D. Tsuzura, inland. Sazare Ruri, age fifteen. POV: Sazare.
~2,950 words.*

---

It took him nine days to get to Tsuzura, which a whole man does in four,
and he did it in barns and lime pits and one memorable culvert, and he
was not proud of any of it afterwards except the not-stopping.

Tsuzura was a river-crossing town two weeks inland by the road he had
not been able to use, ugly, and busy, and entirely indifferent, which
was the whole of its appeal. He took a half-room over a rope-walk and
did the only work available to a man who cannot stand up: clock-and-lock,
hinge plates, chain, scale-beams, the fiddly interior lives of things
that come to a bench and go away again. He was good at it. He was
cheaper than the smith. In three weeks he cleared his board and his
food and about nine hundred ryō.

He did not touch the crystal once in those three weeks.

He told himself it was discipline — a man with a price on him does not
grow blue-grey stone in a town where blue-grey stone is a description —
and that was true, and it was not the reason. The reason was that the
last thing he had grown had come out off-key in the mouth of a drain and
shattered in four seconds against a man's bare hand, and every time he
put his palm on a medium the sound of that shattering arrived first, the
dropped-cup sound going away down the pipe past his ear.

The knee did not mend, because knees do not mend in three weeks and
because he was neither resting it nor eating enough to mend anything.
Some of the fluid went. The rest of it stayed, and the joint made a
noise now on stairs, and he learned to go up sideways, and he made
himself write the honest note in the ledger nobody could read: *six
weeks minimum, on somebody else. Probably twelve, on me.*

He was fifteen years and eleven months old, and he did not have twelve
weeks, and he knew it in the first four days, and it took him another
seventeen to admit what that meant.

---

He did the pricing at the bench over four evenings, because he could not
do it all at once and keep his hands steady.

The question was: *what does the second visit cost?*

Not what would he like. What would it actually take, in coin, to change
the eleven seconds into a number that mattered — and he made himself
define the number first, because a man who prices a thing before he has
specified it is buying comfort.

Eleven seconds got him a hole in the ground on a morning when the men
after him were forbidden to break his arms.

He needed a number that survived men who were *not* forbidden. That was
the specification. Everything else was decoration.

And here the audit turned, and it turned on a thing Sakai had told him at
a door in the rain and Sazare had accepted at the time as the terms of
trade: *you will never be a swordsman; what you can be is hard to kill.*

That had been honest. It was still honest. But it had been an answer to a
question about *swords*, and the men at Tomari had not fought him with
swords; they had not fought him at all; they had walked him down like a
lame horse using a trade he had no name for, and against that trade a
stick of oak and four seconds of prepared ground was not a skill, it was
a hobby.

He owned nothing that belonged to the world those men worked in. Nine
years old at the Academy: still water, throwing, the substitution forms,
and no jutsu of any kind, because there had been fourteen months of
school left when the school stopped existing and every technique in the
place lived in those fourteen months. He had a bloodline and no
instruction in it beyond what his mother had taught a child about
jewellery, and two years of his own honest experiments at night, which
had produced a real discovery about notes and shape and had also
produced, under pressure, a bar that lasted four seconds.

He needed teaching. Not a doctrine. Not a curriculum of leavings from a
kind ruin. Actual instruction in the actual trade, from somebody who had
done it.

There was exactly one market where that was sold.

---

He knew what it cost, because he had spent a year and a half carrying
things past its counters and listening to men complain about prices, and
because he was, whatever else had happened to him, still a boy who had
been taught to read a hand at eight and had never in his life been able
to stop reading anything.

Missing-nin who took students took money by the season, and the going
rate in the border country that year was somewhere between forty and
sixty thousand ryō a season, cash, in advance, and that was for a man who would
teach you badly. The ones worth having asked for more and asked for it in a
lump, because a teacher who is himself hunted does not want a payment
schedule; he wants to be able to leave.

Two hundred thousand ryō would buy a year with somebody real.

Sazare sat at a bench in a rope-walk in Tsuzura with a scale-beam in
pieces in front of him and did the courier arithmetic he had done every
week for a year and a half, and it came out the way he had known it would
come out before he started, which is why it had taken him three weeks to
make himself sit down and do it.

Carrying paid, at the top of the market, in a good season, with routes he
had earned by being unusually good at it, about thirty thousand ryō a year clear
of living. It had taken him two years to accumulate something under fifty
thousand, and the fifty was in a hole behind a brick in a wall on the strait, and it
was going to stay there until the building fell down.

Seven years, then. Seven years of carrying to buy one year of teaching.

Except that was not the answer either, because the answer had a second
term in it, and the second term was the whole point.

They had found him in three years and had come within eleven seconds. They
had gone away with a description, a route, a face, and the knowledge that
he was on this coast and that he mended things. Tōan's flat voice: *prices
like that always find feet.* Feet come back. Say they came back in two
years, or three; and say that each time they came he lost, at a minimum,
every coin he had, because a courier's coin lives in one town and the
whole method of finding him was to find the town.

He would never accumulate anything. Not once, not ever. Every cycle would
take the pot back to nothing, and the pot had to reach two hundred thousand, and
the pot could not survive one cycle, let alone seven.

He checked it four times over four days, hunting for the arithmetic error,
because he wanted very badly for there to be one.

There was no error. The trap was closed and it was closed in a way that
was not even cruel — nobody had built it; it was simply the shape the two
numbers made when you laid them next to each other. Honest work could not
buy the thing that honest work needed to survive.

He put the scale-beam back together and cleaned the bench, and sat in the
rope-walk in the dark with his bad leg out straight, and let himself
finally look at the one line of work he had been not-looking at since the
lime kiln.

---

There was a pawnbroker at the top of the bridge street called Umeya, and
Umeya was licensed, and Umeya's shop smelt of oil and old cloth and had a
cat, and Sazare had put parcels into that shop's hands eleven times in
the previous year without ever once going up the inner stair.

Everyone in the trade knew about the inner stair. That was not a secret.
It was, if anything, the opposite of a secret: it was a *utility*, like
the holder at Tomari, a fixed unglamorous person in a fixed unglamorous
town, and the whole coast used it and the whole coast declined to
describe it, and the licence downstairs was not a disguise, it was a
convenience, because a man who lends against pledges has an entirely
legitimate reason to know who is desperate.

He went up on a wet afternoon twenty-three days after Tomari, with his
hair cut and his good jacket on and the knee strapped, and he took the
step at the door sideways because there was no other way he could take
it.

Umeya was about sixty and wore spectacles and was doing accounts, and
looked up at him over them with the mild irritation of a man interrupted
in the only part of his job he enjoyed.

"I'm not lending on tools," he said.

"I'm not pledging."

"Then what."

"The other ledger," said Sazare.

Umeya took the spectacles off, and looked at him properly, and did the
assessing pass — top to bottom, one sweep, the sixth or seventh of
Sazare's life, and Sazare stood in it the way he had learned to stand in
them and found that it no longer did anything to him whatsoever.

"You're the mender," said Umeya. "Off Ryōsuke's boards."

"Yes."

"Go home."

"No."

"Boy—"

"I've costed it," said Sazare. "I'm not here about a grievance and I'm not
drunk and I'm not going to change my mind in the street, and if you send
me away I'll go to Kakiuchi at the ferry and Kakiuchi is a thief who'd
sell my name back to the coast in a fortnight, and you know that, which
is why you're going to deal with me instead." He kept his hands still.
"You've got work that pays in one payment what carrying pays in eight
years. I want the list."

There was a silence with a cat in it.

Umeya put the spectacles back on, and got up, and turned the sign, and
went up the inner stair without another word.

There were fourteen steps in it. Sazare took them one at a time,
sideways, with his hand on the rail, and it took a while, and the old
man waited at the top and watched the whole of it and never mentioned it
then or afterwards.

---

The upper room was small and clean and had a stove and a second ledger,
and the second ledger was a ledger, ruled and tabbed, with a competent
hand in it. It looked exactly like the first one. That was the thing
Sazare had not been ready for and had no defence prepared against: he had
expected — he could not afterwards say what he had expected. Something
with a shape. Something that announced itself.

It was a book. It was a book kept by a careful man, and there were four
open entries in it, and Umeya read them out flatly the way Anzu had read
out categories, with the sums, and the terms, and the halves.

Three of them were nothing. Debt-grade, retrieval, standing-off work.

The fourth was two hundred and forty thousand ryō, and Umeya said the figure and
then stopped, and took his spectacles off again, and set them on the
ledger, and did the thing that Sazare understood, later, to have been a
kindness of the trade's own peculiar kind — the thing a broker does for a
man on his first, because a man who goes into it blind comes apart in
four months and a man who goes in knowing lasts years, and Umeya's
business was longevity.

"Now," said Umeya. "Before you say anything. The name's a factor at
Kanezaki and I'll tell you who wants it and I'll tell you why, and I'll
tell you what he did and what he's supposed to have done, because they're
not the same list and you'll want both. Most of them ask. Nearly all of
them ask, and I've never once refused, and I'd rather you—"

"Don't," said Sazare.

Umeya stopped.

"Don't tell me," said Sazare.

The stove ticked. Rain came across the window in a handful.

"You'll want it later," said Umeya, carefully, in a different voice. "They
all want it later. At three in the morning, about the sixth week. And by
then I might not have it."

"I know."

"Then why—"

"Because if you tell me who he is I'll take it apart," said Sazare.

He had not planned the sentence and it came out of him level, and it was
the truest thing he had said to another human being since a jetty at
Tomari.

"That's what I do. It's the only thing I've ever been able to do. Somebody
tells me a thing and I turn it over and I look at the grain in it and I
find out how it's put together, and then I know something, and then I have
to *carry* it." His hands, on his knees, were completely still. "I've got
one of those already. I've had it four years. I can't take a second one
and still do this, so I'm not going to be told, and I'm not going to ask,
and if it turns out later that he was a decent man who was owed money by
the wrong person then that will be a thing that happened to me and not a
thing I chose."

"That's a coward's arrangement," said Umeya, without any malice at all;
the way a man corrects a weight.

"Yes," said Sazare. "It is."

Umeya looked at him for a while.

"Fourteen years?" he said.

"Fifteen."

"Hm," said Umeya, and wrote, and did not say the other thing he had been
going to say. "Half now. Half on the word coming back, and the word comes
back through the boards, so don't get clever and don't come here after.
You'll want to be out of this town inside the month either way." He
counted it out of the box under the bench in fives, and pushed it across
the boards, and it was a hundred and twenty thousand ryō, which was more than two
years of a courier's life in one flat brick of paper and coin, and it took
about nine seconds to count.

Sazare put it inside his jacket.

"One more thing and then I'm done and we'll neither of us mention it,"
said Umeya, sitting back. "The ones who ask me everything — I sleep fine
about those. They're buying a story to live in and they'll build it and
they'll be all right for years." He put his spectacles on. "You're the
other sort. You're the sort that goes quiet. I've had four of those in
thirty-one years and I'd rather have had none, and it isn't because
you're dangerous, boy, it's because you're going to be *reliable*, and
this is not a trade that ought to have reliable people in it. Off you go."

---

He took a room that night at the far end of the bridge street, a cold one,
over a stable, because the money in his jacket meant he could not sleep in
a common room ever again.

It was a good room for it. He saw that at once and could not not see it:
the door opened inward and the rebate wanted a shoulder; the wall under
the gable window was six feet of render with a gutter he could not quite
reach; the stable roof ran three ways and one of them ran to the mill
lane; the drain under the yard went out to the river through a grating
that anybody could lift.

Four fixtures, about twenty minutes a night for a week, and the room would
be his the way eleven rooms in two years had been his — the way a room in
Tomari had been his, on a morning when a habit he had been ashamed of had
been worth four seconds and the rest of his life.

He sat on the edge of the bed with his hands hanging between his knees and
looked at the render under the window for a long time.

Then he got up, and went to the writing shelf, and did the only piece of
business he had left that night, which was the tenth.

He wrote out the chit for the boards in the small block hand he used for
the network — twelve thousand ryō, entered at the counter, name that wasn't true,
against a war four years and a country away — and he was three characters
into it when he stopped and looked at what he was doing, and made himself
finish it and seal it and put it in his boot, because the alternative was
to decide that this particular money was of a kind that could not be sent
home, and he was not going to start sorting his money into kinds. It went
where it had always gone. All of it was the same coin now, and the sorting
would have been for his own benefit, and he had had about as much benefit
as he could stand.

Then he lay down in his clothes with the rest of it inside his jacket and
did not grow anything at all.

He was still awake at the third hour, and he worked out why while the
mill-race went under the window, and it was not complicated and it did not
take him long. A man fortifies a room because he expects to be found in
it. He fortifies doorways and gables and drains and the six feet of render
under a window because his whole art, his entire life, the one honest
thing his teachers had between them managed to build out of him, was
being somewhere first and then leaving.

The work in his jacket did not involve being found.

It involved being the one who arrived.
