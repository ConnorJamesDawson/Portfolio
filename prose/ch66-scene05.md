# Chapter 66, Scene 5 — True Sentences

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the Hokage's tower. Shizune, age thirty-one.
POV: Shizune. ~1240 words.*

---

Shizune had been building the Wednesday for six days, and she had built
it the way she built everything that had to survive inspection: out of
true sentences, laid whole, with no joins.

She did not fabricate. It was not virtue; it was engineering. A false
line in a diary is a stone with a hollow in it — it holds until exactly
the wrong day, and then it holds nothing. Whereas a true line held
forever, regardless of who lifted it, regardless of what they were
hoping to find underneath. Three years of running the most watched desk
in the Land of Fire had made her, by her own private assessment, one of
the four or five best writers of true sentences alive, and nobody would
ever know, which was the point of the skill.

The Wednesday read:

*8th. Field review — external placements, medical crystal programme.
Tarugi. Fabricator attending. Hokage auditing in person. Overnight,
licensed premises, Corps rate. Return 9th.*

Every word of it load-bearing. She went down the line one more time,
the way Sazare went down a casting, listening for the ring.

The field review was real: nine stones had gone outside the walls in
the autumn, the programme's first external cohort, and four of them sat
in the therapeutic pool room of the Tarugi bath house, and the
twelve-week review had a due window, and the window was open now and
closed on the nineteenth. She had checked the window before she chose
the week, and then she had chosen the week.

The fabricator was attending because the protocol named the fabricator
as the reviewing officer. His own protocol. His own signature on the
protocol, thirty pages into the programme file, written fourteen months
ago by a man with no idea he was writing anybody a holiday.

The Hokage was auditing in person because the Hokage had signed the
public notice on the programme, and a programme under public notice
gets audited at the discretion of the signatory, and the signatory's
discretion was a thing Shizune kept in a drawer and exercised on her
principal's behalf, which was also true, and also in writing, and had
been since the second month of the administration.

The pass road had ice on it — she had the road report; she had
requested the road report; requesting it had taken one true sentence —
and so the route was the lake ferry, which ran all winter, and which
took forty minutes, and which had a bench at the stern where two
passengers could sit out of the wind.

She had not written the bench into the diary.

The diary did not need everything.

---

Tenzō came at four for the escort briefing, and stood at her desk with
the mild attention that she had decided, inside one Monday of knowing
him, was the most restful thing to brief in the entire village, and she
gave him true sentences.

"Escort at distance. The principal is auditing an external placement of
the medical programme — you have the file. Threat assessment is low and
generic: nobody knows the itinerary, and the itinerary is dull. Your
distance is your own judgement; the principal dislikes visible
security, and the fabricator will read you off any skyline you stand
on, so consider that a training constraint." She turned a page. "The
principal's rest is a medical matter and it is being attended to by her
physician's assistant, which is me, in writing, as of three years ago.
Questions about the itinerary come to me and nobody else."

"One question," said Tenzō. "Who else knows it?"

"You, me, and the innkeeper's ledger."

"Then that's the whole briefing," he said, in the tone of a man
professionally satisfied by a small clean thing, and took his copy, and
paused at the door — one degree of pause, barely a rest in the music —
and said: "One note from the route survey. The stern bench takes the
sun after the half-way mark and the wheelhouse kills the wind, and it
is the only seat aboard with no sightline from the shore road. As an
escort I would want the principal on it — and principals never sit
where the escort wants without a reason, so give her one. *Warm* is a
reason that works."

Shizune looked at him.

"Noted," she said, in her flattest administrative voice, and wrote
nothing at all, and he left, and she sat for a moment reassessing the
week's second-best decision in the light of it having apparently been
the week's best.

*He surveyed the ferry,* she thought. *For a low-generic dull-itinerary
escort, he surveyed the ferry, found the one seat that is safest, and
made the safest seat the warmest, and delivered the whole of it as
tradecraft so nobody has to be grateful. Where has this man been all
my career.*

Tonton, from the basket by the stove, made the noise she made when the
room's ethics had settled, and went back to sleep.

---

The sake question was resolved at six, in the Hokage's office, with the
door shut.

"The minutes," said Shizune, laying them on the desk, "of Monday. Item
four. The objection."

"The bench clause," said Tsunade. "As predicted. The sake stays in the
north cellar."

"Item four, sub-item one," said Shizune, not moving. "The *first*
objection. Raised before the bench clause. Regarding the lamp being
hung on the escort. Which was not the bench clause, and which was
raised first, and the wager as I minuted it—" she produced her notepad,
because she had known this hour was coming since Monday— "was on *which
thing he says he doesn't like*, singular, and the record shows the
first thing he said was not the bench."

"The record shows," said Tsunade, "that he *withdrew* the first one.
A withdrawn objection is not an objection. It's a courtesy with
paperwork."

"The wager," said Shizune, "did not specify surviving objections."

They looked at each other across three years and a desk.

"Split it," said Tsunade at last, magnanimous as a landslide. "You get
the sake. I get to be right. Both of us drink it."

"On the ninth," said Shizune, writing *personnel matter, resolved* in
the margin, "when you're back. Because on the eighth—" and she squared
the minutes, and let one true sentence out of the drawer where she kept
the ones that were not for spending, because it had been three years,
and because the diary block had held, and because somebody in this
tower ought to say it out loud once before the woman left— "on the
eighth you are not the principal, and I have arranged the paperwork so
thoroughly that there is nothing on that day with your title on it
except one signature in a bath-house ledger. I have been waiting three
years to write you a day like that. Go and spend it properly, and if
you come back early I will resign."

There was a silence of a specific and unrepeatable quality.

"Minute that," said Tsunade, which from her, on the seventh day of a
new year, with her coat already over her arm, was the
entire speech, both toasts, and the letter she would never write, and
they both knew it, and Shizune minuted it, in the margin, in her own
shorthand, where it would mean nothing to anybody who had not spent
three years learning to read the space between true sentences.
