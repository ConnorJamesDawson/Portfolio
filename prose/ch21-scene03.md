# Chapter 21, Scene 3 — Two Doors

*Act 3, part C. Nami. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,100 words.*

---

The town came back to life so fast that it was almost indecent.

He watched it happen over nine days from a window over a mudflat and it
was the single most instructive thing he saw in the Land of Waves. On the
Saturday the padlocks came off two of the five warehouses, because
somebody had worked out that a dead man's company does not have a
company. On the Monday four boats went out to the deep ground beyond the
point for the first time in three years and came back so loaded that one
of them was shipping water and nobody cared. On the Wednesday there was an
actual argument on the quay about the price of anything, which was a
sound Nami had not made since before the boy in the bucket hat could
remember, and which half the town came out to listen to with pure
pleasure.

And on the causeway, from first light to last light, every man in that
town who could hold a tool was on the bridge.

They finished the deck in eleven days. It should have taken four months.
They did it with green pine and borrowed nails and a hundred and forty
volunteers and no money whatsoever, and it was, as a piece of
construction, a disgrace from the ninth pier outward and would need a
carpenter on it every spring for the rest of its life, and it was
finished, and on the eleventh day a man could walk from the Land of Waves
to the mainland on a road.

Sazare worked eleven days on it and did not once go past the fourth pier.

He mended tools. That was all and it was enough and there was a queue: a
hundred and forty amateurs with a hundred and forty pieces of somebody's
grandfather's kit, and by the fourth day he had a bench set up at the
causeway head under a tarpaulin with a treadle he had made out of a boat
seat, and he re-handled forty-one hammers, re-set nine adze heads, welded
nothing because there was no forge and brazed a great deal, and sharpened,
by his own count, three hundred and eleven edges.

Nobody paid him one ryō. He ate every meal of those eleven days in
somebody else's house and never once in the same one twice.

---

He did the accounting on the ninth night, because the work was going to
end and he knew what happened to him when work ended.

**He had three hundred and sixty thousand ryō** sewn into the lining of a
coat, and it would not buy the only thing he wanted, and eleven days on
that bridge had made that worse rather than better, because he had spent
eleven days at the head of a causeway watching four people come and go
past his bench.

**He had a bad knee, a bag of tools, four seconds, and no jutsu at all.**

He wrote that down in his head in exactly those words and did not soften
any part of it.

**And he had been shown, in one morning, what the top of his trade
actually looked like**, and it was not a matter of degree, and he had been
lying under it on a twelve-by-twelve with his arms over his head.

That was the thing that would not go away. It had been going round him for
eleven days while his hands did edges. Everything he had built himself
into — the survey, the ground, the not-assembling, the tray, the five
lines in the ledger — the whole careful competent murderous apparatus he
had spent a year and a half constructing, at the cost of a woman on a
shingle spit and a piece of himself in a grave on a hill above Kanmuri
Street —

— had been, on that bridge, in that fog, of exactly no consequence
whatsoever. Not outmatched. *Irrelevant.* A different category of thing
altogether, like bringing a very good hammer to a fire.

---

*Two doors,* said a man in a bandage, in a dark room, with a cup in both
hands.

*First door: you get slow. It's never a better man; it's you at forty
doing the thing that worked at thirty. Second door: you get a village.*

Sazare sat at the window with the shutter open and the cold coming in off
the flats and turned it over, and the horrible thing about it — the thing
he could not get round from any of the four directions — was that the man
who had said it had gone through the first door about a week later, on a
bridge, four hundred yards from where Sazare was sitting.

He had not even got slow. He had got *unlucky*, and he had got hurt, and
then he had got a schedule and a client and a fortnight, and the door had
been standing open the whole time and he had walked at it with his eyes
open with a boy walking beside him.

And Haku had gone through it with him, at fifteen, holding it open.

Two of them. In a morning. And the second door — the village, the stamp,
the building behind you, the people who feed your people — had been the
entire object of the exercise, and neither of them had got within four
hundred yards of it, and the reason was not talent and was not courage and
was not want.

It was that there is no route to the second door from outside.

That was the whole of what Sazare had learned in the Land of Waves and it
had taken him eleven days and two graves to say it plainly. You cannot
buy a village. You cannot earn one from the outside. Zabuza Momochi had
been the best swordsman of his generation and had had a plan and had been
prepared to burn a country to get one, and the country had been the wrong
size, and there had never at any point been a version of it that worked.

*Nobody sells an unaffiliated boy an education in chakra. That is what a
village* is.

---

He put the other offer up beside it, honestly, because it was the only
other one there was and because refusing to look at a thing is how you end
up doing it.

*There is a person alive who could tell you what you're doing wrong.*

He had turned that over about four hundred times since a noodle shop in
Kanezaki and it had never once got less attractive. One sentence. One
afternoon. A man who had never held a lattice and would be *interested*,
which was the part nobody ever believed. And a woman with a bad temper who
had sat down opposite him and refused to say where, and had been right to,
and who had been — he was quite clear about this and had been from the
first minute — the only person in four years to hand him a thing he
actually needed.

He could find her. That was the honest part of it. He was a surveyor and
she had left him three towns' worth of thread and it would take him a
season at most.

And this is what he had, on the ninth night, at the window, and it was not
noble and it did not come out of any decency in him; it was a piece of
professional reading and he arrived at it the way he arrived at everything:

*She said, there is somebody who could teach you. She did not say, there
is somebody who would.*

*Everything about that offer was about being wanted. She came four
hundred miles because she found a step-guard. She bought me a meal because
she had been the only one for eleven years. She said the words* somebody
who'd be interested *and her whole face changed when she said them, and I
have thought about that face every week since.*

*And I have just spent an hour on a beach in the fog losing an argument to
a boy who was picked up out of the snow by somebody who said* you'd be
useful*, and who was happy — genuinely, entirely happy, happier than I have
been for one day since I was thirteen — and who is under four feet of turf
on a headland because being wanted by one person is a thing you cannot put
down when it asks.*

He sat with that for a long time in the cold.

*I don't want to be wanted. I want to be* taught. *And the difference
between those two is the whole of the last eleven days, and Haku could not
see it, and he was cleverer than me, and he was fifteen.*

---

So it was the village, and there was one village.

He did that arithmetic too and it was short.

Not Kiri, obviously. Not Suna, who had no reason and no history of taking
anybody. Not Kumo, who collected bloodlines with both hands and made no
secret of it. Not Iwa.

Konoha.

Where the Academy had taught him, for four years, in a room in the fog, by
a woman he had trusted more than anybody alive except his sister, that the
Land of Fire was soft and treacherous and had won its wars by being liked;
that Konoha's people were sentimental and could not be relied upon; that
they took in strays because a village that grows by taking in strays is a
village with no *spine*.

He had spent two years finding out that every single lesson in that room
had been an instruction rather than a fact.

And then he had spent two weeks in a poor town watching four of them.

A jōnin who could not walk, who had put himself thirty feet back with a
book he was not reading and had arranged an entire detail around the fact
that he was hurt, and who had gone out onto that bridge anyway on a client
job for a man who had lied about the rank.

A girl of twelve on point on a client detail, checking doorways in order,
frightened the whole way and doing it properly.

A boy of twelve who had been taught beautifully and knew it.

And one who had been given nothing whatever to work with that morning
except the fact that he had liked somebody, and had turned out to need
nothing else, and had gone after a child in a bucket hat and shouted at
him with no evidence at all and had made it *work*.

*Sentimental*, said a flat, careful, entirely trustworthy voice at the
front of a classroom in the fog. *They take in strays.*

"Yes," said Sazare, out loud, in an empty loft, to nobody. "That's what
I'm counting on."

---

The last part of it was the part that took him until the eleventh day,
because it was the practical part and he was better at practical parts
than at anything else and had been leaving it deliberately.

He could not walk to Konoha.

He worked it four ways and it held from all four. It was eleven days on
foot for a man with two good legs and considerably more for him. It went
through three countries' worth of road he did not know. And at the end of
it there was a gate with a permanent watch on it, in the largest military
settlement in the world, and a boy of sixteen with a Water accent and no
papers walking up to it out of a wood was not going to get as far as a
sentence.

And in his coat, if anybody looked, was three hundred and sixty thousand
ryō with no account of where it came from; and under his sleeve was a
scar; and in a fat sweat-swollen book with a tab for the Land of Water was
his name, his clan, his technique, his face, and a price with the word
*alive* boxed at the bottom of it.

There was exactly one way in and it had been standing at the head of that
causeway all week with a crutch it had stopped using.

They were leaving on the Thursday.

Sazare Ruri sat at a window above a mudflat on the Wednesday night and
made, at sixteen years and one month old, the single most frightening
decision of his life, which was to walk up to a Konoha jōnin in daylight,
with his hands out from his body, and tell him the truth.
