# Chapter 53, Scene 3 — Whatever You Pick

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the Hokage's office. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen.
POV: Sazare. ~2,385 words.*

---

He said it badly, which he had established on a landing was going to
happen and had decided to allow.

"I've lost something. I don't know how to get it back and I don't think
it can be got back, and I've been going round it for nine days and I
haven't got a second column, and there is one person I could bring it
to and I have not been up here in twenty-one days."

Tsunade did not say anything.

"That's the whole of it," he said. "That's what I came with. There
isn't a paper."

"All right." She had her hands flat on the desk. "What have you lost?"

And he gave her the wrong version, because he could not help it, and it
took four minutes.

---

He gave her Kanzaki at the reconcile — *it is not a series any more, it
is two series with a wall between them, and everything on the far side
of it describes a process nobody is going to perform again* — and the
take and the grip, and that every figure he had produced before the
fourth of the ninth month was a measurement of a fault and not a
capability.

He gave her Ame, and the grandmother in the room, and the finding that
his own history was not a baseline but the record of an absence.

He gave her the four steps in order and they were rigorous and every
one of them followed, and he heard himself get to the end of the fourth
and stop.

Tsunade let the silence go on for about six seconds.

"That's the paper," she said.

"...Yes."

"It's a good paper. It's correct and I can't fault a step of it and it
is not what you walked up a hill in the afternoon with nothing in your
hands to tell me." She did not move. "Where's the rest of it."

---

He sat in a chair in an office and found that his hands had gone flat
on his knees.

"When I write anything," he said, "I compare it to something. That's
the method. It's been the method since I was nine and it is not a
stylistic thing, it's *the instrument* — when I want to know whether a
result is unusual I put it against my own history, because there has
never been anything else in the box to put it against."

"Yes."

"And the phrase is always the same. *Since I was seven.*"

He stopped.

"Go on," said Tsunade.

"I was seven in a workshop in Kiri," said Sazare. "My mother had the
frames along the long wall. I had the stool at the end. There was a
graver I couldn't hold and a knee that had gone in the spring, and it's
the year I started, and I have used it as the origin of everything I
have ever counted.

"She was at those frames every morning of my life and she had six and a
half years left at them.

"Kohaku was twelve, or thirteen. Already going out in the mornings.
Still eating at that table."

The brazier ticked in the corner.

"They were alive until I was thirteen and a half," he said. "So every
single time I have written *since I was seven* I have drawn a line from
now back to a house with three people in it, and the line goes through
the whole of the time they were alive, and I have drawn it about four
thousand times and never once knew I was doing it."

He looked at his hands.

"It isn't a comparison set. It's the only continuous thing I've got.
The house went and the workshop went and the country went and the name
went into a register as *extinct*, and there is not one object in my
room that was in that room. Except the boy. He's in every entry,
underneath, holding the far end.

"And the instrument is broken and I have to stop using it, and if I
stop using it I stop putting him in the book, and there's no third
option, because the whole point of a bracket is that you don't get to
keep the reading and lose the evidence."

Tsunade did not say anything for a long time.

"There it is," she said.

---

She got up and went to the cabinet and got out two cups and the bottle
and put them on the desk and did not pour either of them, which he
recognised, because he had done exactly that with a lamp in the fifth
month.

Then she sat down again.

"Right," she said. "I'm going to tell you what I know about this, and I
want you to understand before I start that I am not going to be
comforting, and that everything I say is going to be short, and that
the shortness is not me being brisk with you."

"Go on."

"You don't replace it."

"...That's it?"

"That's the first thing and it's the one everybody gets wrong." She
turned one of the cups a quarter turn. "You will want a new one by the
end of the month. You are a man who cannot leave a gap in a document
and this is the largest gap you have ever had in one, and you will want
something to put there and you will want it badly and you will want it
soon.

"There isn't one. You don't have a comparison set for a while. And the
while is considerably longer than anybody tells you — I have watched
four people do this and done it myself once, and the ones who came out
of it in any state at all are the ones who spent longer with nothing
than they wanted to."

"How much longer?"

"Years," said Tsunade.

She said it without any particular weight and went straight on, which
he understood about four seconds later to have been deliberate.

"And here's the part that matters, and it's the only piece of advice I
have got that is worth the walk up that hill.

"The danger isn't the gap. Everybody thinks it's the gap. The gap is
uncomfortable and it is survivable and about nine-tenths of it is
boredom.

"The danger is what you reach for to fill it."

---

The room was very quiet.

"Because you will reach for something," she said. "That is not a
criticism, it is arithmetic — a man with a hole in his method will put
something in it inside two years, and he will not choose it carefully,
because he will not be choosing at the time, he will be *filling*.

"And whatever you pick, you'll have for thirty years."

She turned the cup back.

"That's the whole of it," she said. "That's forty years of watching
people and one of me. Take longer than you want to. Be careful what you
put there. It will still be there when you're my age and you will not
remember choosing it."

---

Sazare heard it as what it was, which was a specification.

He did not decide to. He had a criterion in front of him — stated
plainly, by somebody who knew, with the failure mode named — and eleven
years of hands that answered criteria the moment they arrived, and the
answer came up the way a course comes up off seeded stock.

"Then that's tractable," he said.

Tsunade looked up.

"...What?"

"You've given me a selection criterion." He was already leaning
forward, because this was the part he was good at and it was a relief
to be good at something. "That's the hard half. Most of the time the
problem is that there is no criterion at all and you have to build one
out of nothing and you get it wrong four times — I've spent eleven
weeks on a criterion for a *gift* and burned a design over it.

"You've just handed me one and it's a good one. Whatever I pick I'll
have for thirty years. So the constraint is: pick something I would
still want in thirty years."

"Ruri—"

"If that's the fix, then I choose you."

---

He said it in the flat voice.

That was the thing he was able to establish afterwards and never got
past: he said it in the register he used for camber and for reject
rates and for six short faces, the level one, the one he had learned at
thirteen because a fact delivered flat is a fact somebody can receive —
and he said it because he had solved the problem.

And he heard it land about half a second after it was out.

The office did not change. The brazier went on ticking. Somewhere below
them a cart went past on the tower approach and the sound came up
through a shut window and went away again.

Tsunade did not move at all.

Her hands stayed exactly where they were on the desk and her face did
the thing it did perhaps twice a season, where the whole working
apparatus went still at once so that what was left was not an
expression but the absence of one — and then, for about half a second,
it did not manage it.

He saw that.

He would not have seen it two years ago and he saw it now and he could
not read one part of it.

"Don't say it again," she said.

---

"I don't think you've—" He was already rebuilding it, which was
reflex, which was the whole of what he was. "I said it in the wrong
order. What I mean is that the criterion is thirty years and the thing
I would still want in thirty years is not a—"

"Ruri."

"—it's not an object, it's the—"

"*Don't say it again.*"

He stopped.

---

He sat in a chair in an office and did not move, and worked it out in
about four seconds, and every part of it was legible except the part
that mattered.

*I know what I said.*

*I want that recorded plainly, because there is a version of this in
which I did not understand and it would be a considerable comfort and
it is not true. I said it, I meant it, and I heard exactly what it was
about half a second after it left, and I would have got there before I
said it if I had been running anything at all.*

*And it is not a refusal.*

*I know what a refusal looks like from her. I have had four. She says
no, gently, in the voice she uses for a corridor at four in the
morning, and she does not move and there is no edge on it anywhere —
she did it to Katsuyu in a yard on the fourteenth of last month and I
was sitting on a stone nine feet away.*

*That was a door shutting. Shizune did the same thing to me on a
corridor in the ninth month over a rose and I wrote it down at the
time: not unkind, a door rather than a wall, regret and no hesitation.*

*This is not that. This is a woman putting a hand out to stop
something before it arrives, and the reason you stop a thing before it
arrives is that you cannot have it in the room, and the reason you
cannot have a thing in the room is not that you do not want it.*

*So: not a refusal. Something else.*

*And I do not know what.*

---

He reached for it.

That was the part he wrote up four days later and could not get right
for three attempts, because it happened in about a second and a half
and it was, on any measure he owned, the largest thing that occurred in
that room.

He reached for the instrument. He went for the comparison — the way his
hand went for a file, the way it had gone nine times a week since he
was nine years old — because there was a thing in front of him he could
not read and the method for a thing you cannot read is to put it
against everything you have seen before.

And there was nothing there.

Not the broken series. Not the invalid citation, not Kanzaki's wall in
the middle of a month, not the four steps he had recited in this room
eleven minutes earlier and been quietly pleased with.

*It is not that the set is bad.*

*It is that it does not apply.*

*I have four thousand entries and eleven years of me and it is
completely irrelevant, because the question is not about me. The
question is what is happening inside a woman four feet away who has
just put a hand out, and there is nothing in my history that touches
it, and there never was going to be.*

*I have never had one of these.*

*Every question I have ever had — the crystal, the six seconds, the
reject table, the close, the manual, the specification, the marking
scheme — every single one was available to me alone, at a bench, given
enough repetitions. Even the ones about people. I could observe, and
iterate, and be wrong four hundred times, and get there.*

*There is no bench for this.*

*It is inside somebody else and I cannot open it and she is not going
to tell me, and the only instrument that would work is her, and she has
just put a hand out.*

He sat with that.

*So it is not going in the second column tonight and it is not going in
next month.*

*And the boy is out of the book, and it did not happen because a
quartermaster proved my data unreliable at a reconcile. It happened
because I asked a question I cannot get at from my side, and my own
history had nothing whatsoever to say about it, and I felt it not be
there.*

Across the desk, Tsunade said, in a voice that was almost entirely
steady:

"Right. There's a reason I've been trying to get you up here for a
fortnight, and we're going to do that now."
