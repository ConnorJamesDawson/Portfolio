# Chapter 73, Scene 4 — Three to One

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the garden of Tsunade's house. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1890 words.*

---

She scored the first touch in four seconds, and he learned more
from those four seconds than from any four minutes of the eight
weeks.

She did not come in fast. That was the education. Everything he
had braced for — the crossing speed, the ground-splitting
overture, the thing that had put a hole in a training field on
the twentieth of December — did not happen. She walked two
steps, and then she was inside twelve feet, and the walk had
somehow eaten the distance the way a tide eats a beach, and her
open palm arrived flat on his sternum with an unhurried
precision that was worse than violence.

"One," said Tsunade. "You watched my feet."

"I watched your feet."

"Everybody watches my feet. My feet are a rumour." She stepped
back, unbothered, already resetting. "Watch the shoulders. Feet
tell you where I've been."

*Finding, immediate,* he thought, with his sternum ringing.
*Everything in the file about her is about power. Nothing in
the file is about how she CLOSES. She closed fourteen feet
without any of the three tells Jiraiya-sama has drilled into
me, because those tells belong to men who need to accelerate,
and she does not accelerate, she simply arrives.*

*Second finding: she is not going to teach me by beating me
badly. She is going to beat me and then TELL me. Two, and she
has been Hokage for three years and a teacher for thirty and
this is what she is when nobody is watching.*

*Right. Then let us find out what eight weeks bought.*

---

He got the second exchange to eleven seconds, and that was the
footwork, and the footwork was Jiraiya's and it held.

Soft deep ground, roots at the surface, grass to the shin —
ground he had never once trained on, which was the entire
point of a garden nobody had used in eleven years — and his
feet did not go looking for advice. They found the root before
he saw the root. They found the fall of the land toward the
north-west corner and used it. When she came through the
twelve-foot line the second time he was already not where the
line had been, and her palm went past his ribs by four inches
and he felt her *reassess* in the middle of the motion, a
whole recalculation happening in the space of a step.

"Better," she said, and hit him with the other hand.

"Two," said Sazare, from the grass.

"Two. Get up. That was good and it doesn't matter that it was
good, because I am still standing in the middle of your ground
telling you what you did wrong, and the assessment is at two,
and there is a piece of paper on my step."

He got up.

*Right.*

*She is correct, and she is deliberately being correct in the
most annoying possible register, and both of those facts are
instruments. She is trying to make me want it.*

*And here is the finding I am going to give Jiraiya-sama on
Monday, because it is the largest thing in this garden and it
arrived without any ceremony whatsoever:*

*I do want it.*

*Not the way I have failed to want things in a frozen field.
There is no hesitation anywhere in me. I have been looking for
the decision to hurt somebody for eight weeks and could not
find the room it lives in — and I have just discovered that
the room does not contain THAT decision at all. It contains a
different one. It contains: DECIDE TO TAKE.*

*And with her, on this ground, with the terms agreed and her
telling me to — I can. Instantly. Completely. Because there
is no version of this where I damage her, and the entire
brake in me is a brake against damage, and she has built an
afternoon with the brake removed and handed me the whole
question with nothing else attached to it.*

*Trust is where the yes lives. Write that down tomorrow.*

*Not now. Now: the ground.*

---

He gave up on winning exchanges and started building the
garden.

That was the third phase, and it lasted eleven minutes, and it
was — he would think later, on the far side of everything else
that happened that evening — the finest work he had ever done
in his life.

He did not make walls. Walls were for a man who wanted to be
somewhere else; he wanted her here. He made *ground*. A ridge
of graded stone through the grass on the low side, four inches
proud, so that the fall of the land she had been using stopped
being hers. A knuckle of crystal at the plum tree's root where
her left foot had gone twice. The gravel path, seeded end to
end with articles the size of a thumbnail, laid in one act
across nine yards, each one waiting.

She broke three of them. She broke them contemptuously and at
speed, and each break cost her a quarter-second she had not
budgeted, and he was not trying to hold her with them, he was
*pricing* her — and the price accumulated, quarter-second by
quarter-second, until the garden was a place where the
strongest woman alive had to think about where her feet went,
and she began, visibly, to enjoy herself.

"There he is," said Tsunade, from behind the lantern, out of
breath in a way that had nothing to do with exertion. "That's
the reef. Hello, reef."

"You gave me eleven minutes and four trees."

"I gave you a garden I've never used and I am about to find
out how much that cost me. Come on, then, fabricator." She
came out from behind the lantern at an angle that his ridge
made her take, which was the first time all afternoon that
anything he had built had chosen her direction for her, and
his whole chest went bright with it. "Show me the thing you
did to Naruto."

"That is what I am doing," said Sazare, and closed the
cannibal.

The ground came alive along nine yards of gravel path.

She was in the air before it finished — she read it in
something under a third of a second, which was the fastest
anyone had ever read it, including the man who wrote it —
and she came down on the ridge he had built and it took her
weight and she used it, which he had known she would, which
was why he had built the ridge in the first place, because
a woman who lands on your architecture is a woman whose next
step you have already chosen.

He put the wall up at four feet in front of the plum.

She hit it with her fist. Not a chakra strike — an ordinary
fist, at ordinary strength, contemptuous, testing — and the
wall held, and he saw her eyes change, and then she hit it
with a fraction and it went into grit and she came through
the grit and the third touch was going to land on his
shoulder and end the assessment at three to nothing.

And it did not, because the ridge was where her back foot
went, and the knuckle at the plum root was where her weight
transferred, and both of those were his, and for a
half-second — one half-second, in a garden, on the
fourteenth of the third month — the strongest woman alive
was standing entirely on ground that Sazare Ruri had made.

*Now.*

He did not reach for the sword.

He reached for the *hilt*, and she took it — of course she
took it; she was inside two feet and she was Tsunade and the
whole four inches of motion was an invitation written in a
language she had been reading for forty years — her hand
closed over his hand and the guard and she took the sword
off his body.

And clause eleven fired.

*Including if somebody takes it off my body.*

The article locked. It did not shatter, it did not fight,
it did not do anything dramatic whatsoever: it simply
became, in the space of a tenth of a second, a bar of
graded crystal with no give in it anywhere and thirty-one
per cent of its own mass devoted to refusing, and her grip
— a grip that could crack a foundation stone — arrived on
a thing that had already decided.

It cost her a quarter of a second.

His palm landed flat between her shoulder blades.

"Three," said Sazare, into the sudden enormous quiet of the
garden. "To one."

---

She did not move for a moment.

Then she turned around, still holding his sword, and looked
at him with an expression he had seen exactly twice before —
once across a desk with a bloodline thesis in her hands, and
once in a corridor at Tarugi with a lamp — and said,
quietly:

"Nobody has touched me in nine years."

"The governor bought it. Not me. You took the blade and the
blade said no and it cost you a quarter—"

"*Fabricator.*" She said it with enough force to stop him
dead. "Do not do that. Do not stand in my garden and file
the credit somewhere else." She looked down at the sword in
her hand, at the article that had refused her, and then she
laughed — one short astonished breath of it — and handed it
back to him hilt first, the way you return a thing to a
professional. "You built the clause. You built the ridge.
You built the article that made me put my foot exactly
where you wanted it and then you *offered me your own
sword* as bait, and I took it, because I have been taking
bait like that since before your grandmother was born and
nobody has ever once made me pay for it." She was breathing
hard now, and her ears were red, and she was grinning in a
way that made the whole grey afternoon look poorly lit by
comparison. "That's the assessment. That is the entire
assessment. Eight weeks ago you'd have been on the grass in
four seconds three times running. You got a clean touch on
me, on your own ground, out of your own architecture,
against a fraction I have not used on a living person since
the invasion."

"You won three to one."

"I *won three to one*," said Tsunade, "and I have not been
frightened in a fight in nine years, and for a half-second
just then, standing on your ridge with your ground coming
alive underneath me, I felt the floor of the world move —
and if you EVER tell Jiraiya I said that I will deny it
under seal."

"Minuted," said Sazare, hoarsely.

"Nothing's minuted." She looked at him across four feet of
wrecked garden, and the grin went, and what came in behind
it took the whole of the afternoon's air with it. "Nothing
today is minuted. That was the term."

The garden was very quiet. Somewhere over the wall a bird
was doing ordinary bird business in the last of the light.

"The victor," said Sazare, "opens both."

"Yes," said Tsunade. "She does."

And she walked past him, through the ruined grass, over the
ridge, to the step where two folded papers had been sitting
in the cold for twenty-six minutes.
