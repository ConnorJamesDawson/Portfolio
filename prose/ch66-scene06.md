# Chapter 66, Scene 6 — The Price of a Hat

*Act 5, part A. The lake ferry and the Tarugi market. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~2050 words.*

---

They went out the east gate at first light as two travellers and a
rumour of a third.

She wore a road cloak the colour of wet slate with the hood up, and no
robes, and no marker of office anywhere on her that he could find,
and he looked properly, twice, because finding such things was what he
was for. Nothing. A woman on a winter road. It was the most thorough
piece of fabrication he had seen her perform, and she had performed it
by taking things off.

The third traveller was somewhere. Sazare had him twice in the first
mile — a rod case on a far stile, a mild profile at the water stair —
and then gave up looking on purpose, as a courtesy, the way you do not
check a colleague's arithmetic twice.

The ferry left the north stage at eight. Forty minutes across, the
pass road being iced, which Shizune had established with a road report
that he suspected had enjoyed being requisitioned. There was ice at
the lake's margins too, pane ice, white where the reeds went through
it, and open pewter water beyond, and coots on the pewter, going about
in the offended way of coots everywhere.

"Stern bench," she said, from inside the hood. "I'm told it's warm."

The stern bench was warm. The wheelhouse took the wind off it, and
after the half-way mark the sun came over the shoulder of the hills
and lay along it like a paid bill, and Sazare sat in it beside her
with the sword-case across his knees and the whole day ahead of him
with nothing in it, and set about the work of having nothing in it,
and failed inside four minutes.

The ferry had a list. Barely — a degree, perhaps a degree and a half,
loaded — and he had it off the waterline before the stage was out of
sight, and then he had the cause off the cargo lashings, which were
newer on the port cleats than the starboard, and then he was doing the
caulking, seam by seam, forward from the stern, and he had got eleven
seams done when he caught himself, and stopped, and sat with his hands
deliberately open on the case.

*Today is not about a thing.*

He held that for a while. The hills went by at hill pace.

Then he noticed what her eyes were doing.

She was watching the ferryman at the sweep, and she was not watching
him the way a passenger watches a ferryman. She was watching the left
shoulder, which sat a half-inch proud and swung shy of the top of its
arc, and her thumb was moving on the fold of her cloak, very slightly,
the way a thumb moves when the hand it belongs to is palpating a joint
from thirty feet away on professional autopilot.

"Capsular," she said, feeling him watching her watch. "Old tear, badly
kept, worse every winter. He'll sweep with it another two years and
then he won't." And then she heard herself, all the way, and shut her
eyes, and started to laugh, low, inside the hood, at herself first
and then at both of them, and the laugh got into him as well, arriving
the way it had learned to arrive since the fifty-fourth chapter of
whatever his life had become, without asking his face for permission.

"Eleven seams," he said, by way of confession. "And the list is off
the port lashings, and I costed the lashings."

"Of course you did." She put the hood back far enough that the winter
got to look at her for a moment, and the winter came out of it
considerably improved. "Right. Say the terms, because we're both
carpenters about this and there's no use pretending otherwise. It
doesn't count as working if you don't keep it."

"Say that again slowly."

"You can *see* whatever you like," said Tsunade. "Seeing's free.
That's the concession, and I'm making it because forbidding either of
us to see would be a fortnight's failed policy and we'd both lie about
it by Thursday. But nothing gets kept. No notes, no fixes, no
prescriptions. It goes through and it goes out, the way everybody else
lets the world go by. We are not on duty, we are two people on a warm
bench, and the ferryman's shoulder—" she settled back into the sun—
"is the next ferryman's problem."

"The next ferryman is his son," said Sazare. "The lashings say so."

"You see," said Tsunade, comfortable as a cat, "that's exactly the
kind of thing that goes through and goes *out*."

---

The market at Tarugi was a winter market, which meant it was one lane
where the summer plan promised four, and half the stalls were the
same eight families who were always there, selling to each other out
of habit and to travellers out of principle.

He had a commission. He had been careful to establish with himself on
the road that it was a commission and therefore not work: Katsuyu
wanted the market — prices, what things cost when nobody needs them —
and a gift is not a job. The notebook stayed in his coat. That was the
discipline he had set himself at the gate: whatever the day put in
front of him went into memory or it went nowhere, because a man with a
notebook out is a man on duty, and he had it from the Hokage of the
Land of Fire herself, on a warm bench, that he was not.

So he walked the lane and took it all in through the front door of his
head, pricing in silence. Dried gourd strips, forty a bundle, down
from sixty by the state of the paper sign. Charcoal by the sack,
two-twenty, firm, the one price in the lane with no give in it because
cold is cold. Pickled plums in fours and nines — nines, not tens,
which wanted a full minute of standing still until the arithmetic
surfaced: nine keeps the jar under the weight where the carrier's levy
starts. Somebody in that family had been fined once, about a decade
ago, and the jars remembered.

"You're doing it again," said Tsunade, not looking at him, holding a
winter pear she had no intention of buying.

"Through and out," said Sazare. "Nothing is being kept."

"You've got the levy off the plum jars."

"The levy went through and out."

"Mm," she said, and put the pear down, and bought nothing either, and
they went on up the lane in step, two liars in a hood and a scarf,
enormously pleased with themselves.

---

The hat was her idea.

It was on a dowel at the clothier's stall, dark green felt with a low
crown and a brim you could actually work under, and she took one look
at his bare head, which had been his bare head in all weathers for as
long as she had known him, and said, "That one."

"I have got a hood on this coat."

"You've got a hood you don't put up because it costs you your ears,
and don't tell me it doesn't, I've watched you refuse it since
November. Try the hat."

He tried the hat. It fitted. That should have been the end of the
matter and would have been anywhere in the world except a market.

"Nine hundred," said the stallholder, a broad and cheerful man with
the voice of somebody who had sized the cloak, the posture and the
scarf and concluded, not unreasonably, that the morning had sent him
city people.

What followed was, by his own count, the best value he got all day,
and it cost him nothing but the hat's own price.

She went in at two hundred, with theatre — the disbelief, the
half-turn away, the appeal to the lane at large. The stallholder came
down to seven-fifty wounded. She came up to two-sixty as a personal
favour. He described his children, in winter, hungry, hatless. She
described the felt as *last year's*, which it was not quite, and he
countered with the workmanship, and that was the mistake, because the
word was a door, and the door was open, and Sazare walked through it
in the level voice of a man reading a casting sheet.

"The felt is good," he said. "Well fulled, even, three-and-a-half,
maybe four millimetres, and the crown's blocked properly — there's no
thin spot at the turn, and most of them have a thin spot at the turn.
The band is dyed jute, not silk. The stitching on the brim is
machine-run and it's run twice, the second pass over a wander in the
first, lower left, about eight o'clock as you wear it. The sweat
leather's the real cost in it and it's decent pigskin. Materials and
the blocking, you're at about a hundred and forty. Labour you're
entitled to, stall rent in winter, carriage over the pass before it
iced. It is a *good hat*," he said, with feeling, because it was, and
he had reached the age of eighteen without ever once being this
species of happy, "and a fair price is three-forty, and at three-forty
you are making an honest ninety and we both know it."

There was a silence in the lane of the kind that gathers spectators.

"Three-forty," said the stallholder, to Tsunade, "and I want it noted
I'm doing it for the pleasure of the speech. Eleven years on this
pitch. Nobody has ever haggled me with a *survey*." He took the coins,
and boxed the hat's dowel-mate for a lady four stalls down who had
been listening and wanted one now, and said to Sazare, wrapping
nothing because the hat was going out on the head it now belonged to:
"You want work, son, come back in cherry season. I'll put you on the
silk table and you can insult people at the prices where it pays."

"Its job," said Sazare, later, up the lane, because the thing had
been sitting in him and needed saying while it was true, "the hat's.
I have been looking for it since the stall. It keeps rain off, but so
does the hood, so that is not it."

"Its job," said Tsunade, straightening the brim a degree with one
finger, proprietary as anything, "is that I like it. Some things get
to have that job. You'll adjust."

---

The stall at the top of the lane was a numbers game, cups and a
token, five ryō a go, run by an old man with a brazier and a face
like a walnut that had seen everything, and the prizes were paper: 
pinwheels, cranes, a fish kite for the big board.

She stopped the way people stop at a smell from a kitchen they used
to live over.

"One," she said, and put down five ryō, and the old man spun the
cups, and Sazare watched her watch them with an expression he had no
column for anywhere — appetite and dread in the same glance, an old
argument resumed mid-sentence — and she tapped the left cup, and the
token was under it.

The old man looked mildly pleased, the way stall men do when a mark
wins early, and held out the pinwheel row for choosing.

She did not choose for four seconds.

He counted them. Four. Her face did nothing at all for the whole four,
which for a face that ran loud even into a hood was itself the event —
the lighthouse of her, out, for four seconds, over a paper pinwheel at
a winter stall.

Then she took the blue one, and thanked him, graceful as you like,
and they walked on, and she spun the wheel once against the pace of
her own walking and watched it go round, and said nothing, and Sazare
filed the four seconds where he filed the two bells and the gamble
kanji and every other thing that was hers and not offered — *seen,
kept, not asked* — and the lane let them out at the top, into the
street where the bath house stood with its lamps already lit against
the early dark, and steam standing up out of it like the flag of a
small warm country.
