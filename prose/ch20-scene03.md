# Chapter 20, Scene 3 — Underneath

*Act 3, part B. Under the bridge, low water. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen.
POV: Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

He went under it at twenty past six with fifty minutes of falling tide in
hand, along the shingle foreshore at the mainland end, in fog so thick
that he found the first pier by walking into it.

Under the deck the world went dark and the sound changed.

That was the first thing and it was extraordinary. Out on the open shore
the fog had swallowed everything and left him in a soft white nothing;
under forty feet of concrete road the fog was still there but the *ceiling*
came down on it, and every noise in the bay — the water on the stones,
his own boots, a gull somewhere — arrived flattened and doubled, with the
echo coming off the underside of the deck about a quarter-beat behind
itself.

It stank. Green weed and cold stone and the specific ammoniac reek of
forty feet of pier that a colony of gulls had been using as a lavatory for
two years.

He worked out along the piers on the wet shingle, in the dark, with one
hand on the concrete.

Pier one: sound. Pier two: sound. Pier three: a shrinkage crack running
up out of the shingle for about nine feet, hairline, harmless, the kind
every mass pour in the world has. He put his palm flat on it as he went
past out of pure habit.

At the fourth pier the shingle stopped and there was water, and beyond that
the deep piers went out into the channel with the fog lying on the surface
between them, and he could go no further on the ground.

So he went up.

The bracing between the piers was the thing that made it possible: massive
timbers, twelve by twelve, doubled and bolted, running pier-to-pier under
the deck at about half height, put there to stop the whole structure
racking in a gale. They had been placed by somebody who had thought about
access, because there was a way from one to the next if you did not mind
where you put your hands, and they were black with weed for the first
three feet and dry above.

Sazare got up onto the bracing between the fourth and fifth piers with his
knee complaining bitterly the whole way, and lay down along a
twelve-by-twelve in the dark under the road with the water eleven feet
below him and the concrete two feet above his back, and made himself into
an object.

He was, without any question, the safest person in the Land of Waves that
morning and he had put himself there on purpose and he was not proud of
it.

---

They came out onto the deck at a quarter to seven.

He knew that not from voices but from *feet* — four sets, and a fifth
dragging slightly, walking out along a hollow structure over his head, and
he could have told you which of them was where in the road within about
six feet.

The heavy uneven one with the pause in it was the old man. The light quick
one was the girl. Two boys, one of them going in a straight line and one
of them fidgeting the whole way out. And behind them all, thirty feet
back, a step and a crutch, a step and a crutch, on a road he could not
run on.

Sazare lay on a beam in the dark and listened to five people walk out onto
a bridge, and thought, with a completeness that frightened him:

*None of you are going to remember me at all.*

Then the fog got thicker.

He did not see it happen; he had no view of the deck. What he felt was the
temperature drop — three degrees, four, in about ninety seconds, on a
still morning on a falling tide, which does not happen — and the light
under the bridge, which had been going from black toward grey, stopped
going.

And every sound in the bay stopped with it.

The gulls went. The water on the stones went to nothing, or the fog took
it. He lay on the beam in a silence so complete that he could hear the
blood in his own ears and the small tick of a drop of condensation coming
off the deck above him and landing on the timber by his hand.

*That's him,* thought Sazare, in the dark, under a road, in the cold.
*That's not weather. That's a man putting a country over the top of five
people, and I have been in his house, and I mended his veranda.*

---

He read the whole of the next two hours through the structure and never
saw one clear thing.

That was not a limitation. He understood that at about the fifteenth
minute and it was one of the strangest realisations of his life: he was
getting *more* than a man standing on the deck in that fog would have got,
because a bridge is a machine for transmitting force, and every single
thing that happened up there arrived in the timber under his chest with a
signature on it.

He learned to read them inside four exchanges.

**Steel on steel** came through as a bright short crack with almost no
follow-through, because the shock went into two people's arms and stopped
there. He counted eleven of those in the first minute and then stopped
counting.

**A body hitting the deck** was a low double thump — the impact, and then
the deck's own answer about a fifth of a second behind, a wallow that ran
out along the beams and died.

**Something hitting the deck very hard indeed** was different again, and
he felt the first of those at about the eighth minute: a bang that came up
through the twelve-by-twelve into his sternum and put a fine grey dust of
concrete down through a gap in the boarding six feet away.

He put his palm flat on the timber under him and left it there, and after
that he had the whole thing.

**Water.** A great deal of water, being moved deliberately, more than
once — he could hear it coming off the deck edge in sheets and falling,
and the deck taking the weight of it and shedding it, and each time it
happened the fog under the bridge went momentarily wet enough to taste.

**And then, at about the twentieth minute, the cold.**

It arrived from the seaward end and it went through everything. Sazare had
been cold since three in the morning and this was not that; this was a
different substance, a dry immediate cold that made the inside of his nose
sting, and it came with a sound he could not place at all for several
seconds and then could: a very high, very quiet, continuous *ringing* —
not a bell; a hundred thin sheets of something all going at once.

Light came down through the deck gaps.

Not sunlight. There was no sun; it was ten past seven in a fog and the sky
was a lid. This was hard and white and it flickered, and it moved, and it
came through the boarded section over the fifth and sixth piers in
narrow bars that swung as whatever was making them swung, so that the
underside of that bridge for four or five minutes was full of turning
shafts of white light, cutting through fog and gull-lime and forty feet of
falling condensation.

Sazare lay under it with his face turned up.

He was, entirely against his will, at that moment, purely and simply a
sixteen-year-old boy watching something he had no name for.

---

The rest he got in pieces and he never afterwards got the pieces into a
sequence he trusted, and he was scrupulous about that for the rest of his
life: he had been under a road in fog and he had *heard* a fight and had
seen not one thing worth calling seeing, and anybody who told him
afterwards what had happened on that bridge knew more than he did.

What he had was this.

A dog. More than one dog — several, at least four, arriving out of nowhere
at about the fortieth minute, and their feet going on the deck in a way
that made no sense at all until it did, and then a great deal of shouting.

A boy screaming. He put it at the sixth pier and he could not tell which
boy and he never found out, and it was not a fighting sound, it was the
other kind, and it went on for a while.

A silence after it that was worse than the sound.

And then, at about the fiftieth minute, something happened above him that
Sazare Ruri had no framework for whatever and never got one for.

The *pressure* changed. That was the only word he ever found for it and he
knew it was inadequate. Something came out of the middle of that bridge in
a wave — not force, not wind, not heat; a sort of arriving — and it went
through the fog and the deck and the concrete and the twelve-by-twelve
timber and the boy lying on it, and every hair on his body stood up, and
the water under him went into rings, and the gulls that had been sitting
somewhere out in the fog all came off the water at once, and something
inside him that had nothing to do with his mind said *get down*, and he
was already down.

It lasted perhaps four seconds. There was a noise like the ice going, if
you could have all of it at once, and something came apart up there and
came off the seaward edge in pieces and hit the water.

Then it went quiet in a completely different register.

Sazare lay on a beam under a bridge with his face in the crook of his arm
and his whole body shaking and understood, with total clarity, several
things at the same time:

That whatever that was, it was on the Konoha side.

That there were four of them up there and three of them were children.

And that his entire professional assessment of everybody on that bridge —
the assessment he had built carefully, from evidence, over nine days, and
had been quietly satisfied with — was not slightly wrong. It was not a
matter of degree. He had been looking at the wrong *kind* of thing, from
the beginning, and had never once suspected it, and the four seconds of
his life he had just spent flat on a beam with his arms over his head were
the only honest reading he had taken all week.

---

He did not do anything.

He wanted that on the record, in his own private ledger, in the hand
nobody could read, and he put it there afterwards without any softening:
*I did nothing. I was under it for two hours and I did nothing at all.*

He had come with the four seconds and a bag of tools. He had, lying on
that beam, three or four ideas of the kind that get people killed — a
wall at the seaward pier; a bar across the gap where the boarding was out;
something, anything — and every one of them failed the same test, which
was that he did not know where anybody was.

You cannot fortify a fight you cannot see. Every position he could have
built would have been as likely to close a door for Tazuna as for
anybody else, and there was a twelve-year-old girl on that deck standing
over an old man with a knife she had probably never used, and the single
worst thing Sazare Ruri could have done that morning — the arrogant thing,
the thing a man does to feel that he was present — was to put an
unexpected wall into a fog fight between people who had spent eleven days
planning where each other would be.

*You won't fight the fight that's offered when you can fight the one that
isn't,* said a ruined ronin in the steam.

There was no fight here for him.

So he lay on a twelve-by-twelve under a road, in the cold, forty feet from
the only two people in the country he had eaten with, and did the thing he
was best at in all the world, which was to be extremely still and let
something enormous happen four feet above him without disturbing it.

---

At about the second hour the fog began to go.

He felt it before he saw it, in the temperature; and then the light under
the bridge went from that flat submarine grey to something with a
direction in it, and the underside of the deck acquired edges, and out
past the piers the water came back into the world in a long widening lane
of green.

And in that lane, at about the tenth minute past nine, going out from the
shore, he heard a sound he identified instantly and completely and with a
lurch that took the bottom out of him.

Boats.

Not one. Not two. He counted eleven before he stopped and there were more,
small craft, coming out from the town side and rounding under the piers
toward the seaward end, badly rowed, overloaded, with men in them.

And then the noise of them arriving at the far end and going up onto the
deck.

Not four sets of feet. Not five.

A crowd. A hundred and more, boots on concrete, all of it coming up
through the structure at once as a low continuous roar with no rhythm in
it whatsoever; and over the top of it a voice, amplified by nothing but
its own pleasure, saying something Sazare could not make out, at length, to
an audience.

Sazare lay under the bridge with his hand flat on the timber and felt a
hundred and something men walk out onto a road above him, and knew exactly
what it was, and knew who was standing at the front of it on a raised
thing with people arranged toward him, and found that his hands had gone
completely cold and that he was, for the first time in two hours, not
frightened at all.
