# Chapter 24, Scene 1 — The Other Library

*Act 4, part A. Konoha, the civilian quarter. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen.
POV: Sazare. ~2,350 words.*

---

He found it because he asked a question that annoyed a nineteen-year-old.

The girl on the desk at the Academy reading room was called Himeji and had
by then stopped pretending she did not know who he was, largely because he
had mended her chair.

"Where would I find something on how a crystal actually forms?"

She looked up from her card index.

"There isn't any—" She stopped, and started again, in the voice of
somebody quoting a shelf list. "There's nothing. There's an entry in
the kekkei genkai index and it's four lines and three of them are *not
attested in Fire Country*. I looked it up in your first week because I was
nosy."

"Not the technique. The material."

"The what?"

"The material," said Sazare. "Rock crystal. Quartz. Anything. How does a
crystal get to be a crystal? What decides which way it goes? Why do some
of them come out clear and some of them come out cloudy and some of them
have a crack in them that's dead flat, like somebody's cut it, when nobody
has?"

Himeji put down her pen and gave him the look that librarians have been
giving people since libraries were invented.

"That's not us," she said. "That's not *ninja*, is it. That's rocks."

"Yes."

"Then you want the other one." She pointed with the pen, roughly
south-west, through two walls. "Village library. Off the Nakadōri, past
the fish shop, next to the bathhouse. That's the one with the *books*
books — farming and law and mending an engine and how to raise a child and
four hundred novels. It's for everybody. Nobody from here ever goes."

"Why not?"

Himeji shrugged with the whole of her upper body.

"Because it's for civilians," she said, without one particle of malice,
which was the part that Sazare thought about for the rest of the week.

---

The village library was a two-storey building on a corner with a chestnut
tree outside it and a bicycle rack that had been mended about nine times,
and it did not look like anything at all.

It was open, on a Wednesday afternoon in the second month, and it had in
it: four old men reading newspapers on a rack; a woman of about thirty
with two small children in the corner where there were cushions; a boy of
fifteen asleep on his arms; and a stove.

It smelled completely different from the other one. The Academy reading
room smelled of paper, chalk and eleven-year-olds. This smelled of paper,
coal smoke, and — from somewhere at the back — glue.

The woman at the desk was about seventy-five and was called Ochiai, and
she wore a cardigan over a cardigan and had a stamp in her hand and a pot
of paste and a stack of eleven books with their spines off, and she was
mending them.

She looked up at him and then, immediately and without any embarrassment
whatsoever, at his hands.

"You'll be the crystal boy from the hospital," she said.

"Yes."

"Mm. My niece is on the third floor there. Nights." She went back to the
spine she was doing. "What do you want?"

"Rocks."

Ochiai stopped.

"Say that again."

"Rocks," said Sazare. "Minerals. How crystals form. I've — " and he had no
idea, afterwards, why he said the next part to a stranger in a cardigan
when he had not said it to a single person in that village, but he did.
"I've been growing them since I was seven and I have no idea what I'm
doing. Nobody in my family did. We had a way of doing it and we did it and
nobody ever asked why it worked and there's nobody left to ask, and I've
been trying to reason it out on a bench for three years and I keep
getting to the same wall."

The old woman looked at him for a moment over the top of a broken spine.

"Second room, left-hand side, third and fourth bays," she said. "Under
*natural history*, because whoever did the shelving in 1961 hadn't heard
of geology and I've never had the heart to change it. Take your coat off,
you'll be there a while, and don't put anything back — leave it on the
end of the table and I'll do it."

---

He was there four hours the first day and about two hundred hours over the
following four months, and it changed the whole of the rest of his life,
and the first thing that happened was that he sat down on the floor of the
second room between two bays and laughed at himself for about a minute.

Because there were forty books about it.

Forty. Not four hundred; it was a village library. But forty separate
volumes on a subject he had assumed, in some unexamined way, to be
*nobody's* subject — mineralogy, crystallography, a two-volume thing on the
gemstones of the eastern provinces with hand-coloured plates, three on
mining, one enormous and quite mad one from ninety years ago called *On
the Habits of Salts*, and a school textbook for fifteen-year-olds with a
soft cover and a diagram on page four that stopped him dead.

He had spent nine years inside a trade. He had spent three years since
reasoning about that trade from first principles, alone, at night, with a
hammer and thirty-four offcuts, in four countries.

And it had never once occurred to him — not once, in any hour of any of
it — that *crystal is a thing that happens by itself*, everywhere, all the
time, in rock and in salt pans and in the bottom of a kettle, and that
ordinary people with no chakra at all had been watching it happen and
writing it down for four hundred years, because they were interested.

Nobody in his family had ever read a book about stone.

They had been the best in the district at it. They had had four hundred
years of practice, an inherited method, a guild relationship and a
reputation on three coasts, and not one of them had ever gone to a shelf.

---

The school textbook did it in about nine pages, and he read those nine
pages perhaps forty times over the following month, and copied all of it
out twice, once straight and once in his own words.

**A crystal is a repeating arrangement.** That was page one and it was the
whole foundation and he had not had it. Not a lump of something hard — a
*pattern*, the same small arrangement of parts, laid down over and over in
three directions, and the shape of the outside comes from the shape of the
repeat and not the other way about.

**It grows by adding to what is already there.** Layer on layer, at the
faces, and the layer that goes down copies the one underneath it.

*Copies the one underneath it.*

Sazare sat in the second room of a village library with his finger on a
line of type and felt the floor move.

**And that is why growing it fast is different from growing it slowly** —
and here the book was completely matter-of-fact about a thing he had bled
for. If the material arrives faster than it can find its place, it does not
wait. It goes down wherever it lands. And where it lands wrong, you get a
*defect*: a row out of step, a plane where two halves of the same crystal
meet at an angle, a trapped pocket of the stuff it grew out of.

And a crystal with a plane of defect in it does not break where you hit
it.

**It breaks along the plane.**

There was a diagram. It was two inches across, on page eleven, and it
showed a hexagonal crystal with a dotted line running clean through it at
an angle, and under the diagram it said:

*Cleavage. Note that the fracture is not related to the point of impact.
Struck anywhere, a crystal of this habit will part along its own weakest
plane. The specimen decides where it breaks; the hammer only decides when.*

---

He did not go to work that afternoon.

That was the only shift he ever missed in four years and he squared it
with Sister Kaburagi afterwards by working two of somebody else's, and she
looked at him for a while and did not ask.

He sat on the floor of the second room from the second hour to the tenth
and read, and made notes on the backs of four requisition forms he had in
his coat, and by the time Ochiai came and told him they were closing he
had, laid out in front of him on the boards in his own handwriting, the
whole of the thing he had been walking round for three years.

*The note is the cleavage plane.*

*A piece grown fast has a plane in it, because the material went down
before it found its place, and it went down in the same wrong way all
across one layer, and that layer is now a fault running through the whole
piece.*

*It does not shatter where I hit it. It shatters along that plane,
wherever I hit it. That is why the sound is the same every single time and
why it comes apart all at once instead of chipping: I am not breaking it.
I am* giving it permission.*

*My mother's frames hummed because production work has two of these
planes in it, and a good piece has none, and the pieces the shop kept
back were silent, and I have known that since I was seven years old and
have never once known what I knew.*

He sat on the floor with his back against a bay of natural history and put
his hands over his face for a moment.

"You'll want to be out in about four minutes," said Ochiai, from the door.
"I lock the back one first."

"Yes."

"Found what you were after?"

Sazare took his hands down.

"There's a diagram," he said, "on page eleven of a book for
fifteen-year-olds, that answers a question my family has been unable to
answer for four hundred years."

"Mm," said Ochiai, entirely unimpressed, and put the light out at the far
end. "That's books, that is."

---

He came back three days a week for the rest of that spring and he read
everything in both bays, and about half of it was no use whatever and
about a fifth of it was wrong, because it was ninety years old and had
been written by a man with strong opinions about salts; and four or five
things in it went into him like nails.

**Seeding.** A page and a half in the gemstone volume, almost in passing,
about how the growers in the eastern provinces made large single crystals
for optical work: you do not start from nothing. You put in a *seed* — a
small piece, already properly formed — and the new material lays itself
down copying the seed, and the whole thing inherits the order of the thing
you started with. Start from nothing and the material has to organise
itself, and it will, and it will do it badly and in a hurry.

**Annealing.** Hold a flawed piece near its working temperature for a long
time and the arrangement relaxes and re-orders itself, and strain comes
out of it, and it does not become a different crystal — it becomes the
same crystal, tidier.

**Habit.** That the same material grows into different shapes depending
entirely on the conditions it grew in, and that a man who knows the
habits can read the conditions backward out of a specimen he did not
watch being made.

And on the last page of the school textbook, in the summary, in bold, for
fifteen-year-olds:

*Growth is a race between arrival and arrangement. Everything else in this
chapter follows from that sentence.*

Sazare Ruri copied that sentence onto the back of a requisition form in a
village library in the Land of Fire, and put it in his coat, and carried it
about for eleven years.

---

He asked Ochiai, on the way out, at the end of the third week, whether
there was a way of borrowing them.

"Course there is," she said. "It's a library. Two at a time, three weeks,
and there's a fine and I'll charge you it."

"I'm a probationary foreign national."

"You're a what?"

"I'm on conditions. Movement's reported. I didn't know whether—"

"Have you got an address?"

"Yes."

"Then you can have a card," said Ochiai, and got a blank one out of a box
under the counter and inked the stamp and looked up at him with the pen
ready. "Name."

He gave it.

She wrote *RURI, S.* on a piece of buff card about two inches by three, and
stamped it, and wrote the date, and pushed it across the counter.

Sazare stood in a village library at the end of a Wednesday and looked at
it.

It was the sixth time an institution in his life had written his name on
something, and it was the first one that had been done in about nine
seconds by somebody who had not looked up first, and had not asked him
one further question, and had not written down anything else about him at
all.

He put it in his coat, next to the requisition form with the sentence on
it.

"Second bay's got a gap in it now," said Ochiai, going back to her spines.
"You've had four out of it. Bring 'em back Wednesday and I'll see if I can
get the county to send the other volume of the gemstones, because we've
never had it and I've been meaning to ask for about nine years and you're
the first person who's ever wanted it."
