# Chapter 16, Scene 1 — Not Assembling

*Act 2, part D. Kanezaki. Sazare Ruri, age fifteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

Kanezaki was a river port with a salt quay and a lumber quay and eleven
thousand people, and he was in it four days before he understood that the
hardest part of the work was not going to be any of the parts he had
worried about on the road.

The hard part was going to be not listening.

He had thought, at Umeya's, that refusing the name settled it. He had
said *don't* and meant it and had been proud of it in a queasy way for
about nine days. Then he arrived in a town where a man lived, and
discovered what any adult in the trade could have told him for nothing:
you cannot watch a man for a week without a town telling you his life.
Towns cannot help it. A town is a machine for saying things about the
people in it, and it does not require a question to work — a question is
only a way of making it go faster.

By the end of the first afternoon, without asking anything of anybody, he
knew that the man in the blue coat off the fifth house on Kanmuri Street
had a bad hip, a daughter, a temper about the tally of things, a
reputation of some kind that made the salt porters go quiet in a
particular way, and a habit of walking home along the water instead of
through the lanes.

He had wanted one of those. He had been handed five.

So he invented a discipline for it, on the second day, sitting on a
mooring bollard eating cold rice, and the discipline was this: he would
not *assemble*.

He could not stop the facts arriving. He could refuse to sort them. He
kept them the way you keep parts you have not identified yet, loose in
the tray, not laid out in order, not grouped, not turned the same way up
— because the moment you lay a man's facts out in order you have made a
person, and a person has a shape, and a shape can be liked or disliked
or pitied, and any of the three would finish him.

It was the exact opposite of every instinct he had. It was harder than
the knee.

He got, in nine days, quite good at it, and the getting-good was worse
than the difficulty had been.

---

The survey itself he did the way he had done Tomari, and Ōura, and
Shiranui, and eleven lodgings across two countries, and the first time
he noticed the resemblance he had to stop and sit down on a stack of
lumber for a while.

Which wall would take a boot. Where the roof space ran and whether it
ran anywhere. Which door opened outward and could therefore be wedged.
Where the water was. Where the dark was. Where a man coming in fast
would have to slow down, and why, and whether the why could be made
worse.

The same list. The identical list, item for item, in the same order,
done by the same hands with the same twenty-minutes-a-night patience —
and the only thing in the whole world that had changed was that he was
no longer asking *how do I get out*.

He was asking *where does he have to be*.

*You fight ground the way other men fight people,* said a ruined ronin,
in the rain, in a bathhouse yard, meaning it kindly.

Sazare sat on a stack of green lumber in a foreign port with his bad leg
straight out in front of him and understood that Sakai had handed him an
assassin's education over two years and had gone to his bed every night
of it believing he was teaching a boy to run away.

He wondered whether Sakai had known. He thought about it properly, the
way he thought about everything, and concluded that the old man had
almost certainly known and had done it anyway, because the alternative
was a dead boy, and because a door-keeper who has buried students of all
three sorts learns to prefer the ones who are alive to be disappointed
in.

That did not make it better. But it was, he thought, probably true, and
he had made a rule about preferring true things some years ago and was
not going to start making exceptions for the ones that hurt.

---

He worked while he watched, because a man in a port who is not working
is the only interesting thing in it.

That part required no invention at all. He put a piece of card in the
window of a lodging on the salt side — *MENDING* — and by the third
morning there was a queue, because there was always a queue, because
eleven thousand people generate broken things at a rate no town's smith
has ever kept up with.

He took the work sitting down. Locks, hinges, a bosun's whistle, a
brass tally-frame, a child's spinning top, forty-one separate items in
nine days, and he was cheap and quick and he did not talk, and Kanezaki
formed the opinion of him that every place had formed of him since he
was thirteen years old: *quiet lad. Knows what he's about. Doesn't ask
questions.*

He knew, by then, precisely what that opinion was worth and precisely
what it cost. He had watched a woman at an exchange counter say it —
*in a port, a man who doesn't ask questions is the loudest thing in the
room* — and he had watched a white face come out of a drain because a
whole coast had spent a year mentioning a mender.

He put the card in the window anyway.

He told himself, at the time, that it was the delivery system: that a
mender goes everywhere, is spoken to by everyone, has a legitimate
reason to be found on any street at any hour with a roll of tools, and
that the trade would have to invent this cover if it did not already
exist in him. All of which was true.

The other reason he did not look at, and it stood in the window with
the card, and it was that he had been on the road nineteen days and had
not fixed one single thing in all of them, and he had found that he
could not do another.

---

On the sixth evening he walked the gangway.

The warehouse row on the salt side stood with its backs to the water,
and between the buildings and the wharf edge ran a stone gangway four
feet wide and two hundred long, unlit, used by nobody after the sixth
hour except men who lived at the east end and could not be bothered to
go round by Kanmuri Street.

It was the correct place and he knew it inside forty seconds, and he
spent an hour and a half finding out why he knew, because *correct* is
a feeling and feelings have workings.

The workings were: one way in, one way out, and a third way that was
water. No sightline from any window, because warehouse walls have no
windows on the water side, because nobody puts a window where a crane
can swing. No lamp, because the lamp trade in Kanezaki was the
harbourmaster's and the harbourmaster's writ stopped at the wharf edge.
And a bend three-quarters along, where the row jogged out to follow an
old dock, which meant that a man at the bend could not be seen from
either end and could not see either end, which cut both ways and was
therefore honest.

He stood at the bend in the dark with the river going by underneath and
his heart perfectly steady and thought, clearly and in his own voice:
*this is where I am going to kill a man.*

Then he went back to his lodging and was sick, and lay awake, and was
not sick again, and noticed that too.

---

The knife he bought on the eighth day.

He had worked out the principle on the road and had been quietly pleased
with it, in the awful way he was quietly pleased with things now, and
the principle was: **nothing of mine in the wound.**

It had come to him in a lime-kiln sort of way, at night, on his back,
and it had come as a question about evidence and had arrived as a
question about *himself*. A shard of blue-grey stone in a body is not a
clue. It is a signature. It is his mother's trade and his sister's clan
and four hundred years of a family, put inside a stranger, and left
there. Any hunter with a book knows a Shōton user by the thing he leaves
behind, because the thing he leaves behind is the reason there is a book.

So: nothing of his. The wound would be made by an object that ten
thousand people owned, bought for a few hundred ryō in a market, and dropped in
deep water within the hour.

He chose a fish-knife — a gutting knife, four and a half inches, thin,
wooden-scaled, the commonest working blade on any river in the world —
and he bought it at the tail of the fish market on a busy morning from a
stall that had sold, by the stallholder's own boasting, sixty of them
that week, and he paid in small coin and did not haggle and was one of
about nine people the man served in that minute.

He honed it that night on his own stone. He was very good at edges. It
was a jeweller's skill and it took him eleven minutes and it came off
the stone better than the man who made it had ever managed, and Sazare
looked at what his hands had done and put the knife down and sat with
his fists against his forehead for a while.

**Every single thing he was going to use, he had been taught by his
mother.**

The reading of a room. The patience. The hands that did not shake. The
edge. The knowledge of exactly how much force a material wants and
exactly where it fails. Mizuho had built all of it into him at a bench
before he was ten years old, teaching a boy a trade in a doomed district
because a trade was the only inheritance she could think of that the
state could not confiscate.

The state had not confiscated it. He was going to spend it.

---

The last two nights he did the ground, and the ground was the part he
understood best in all the world, and it went in like breathing.

He did not put one grain of crystal anywhere near the gangway.

That was the discipline and he held it, and holding it took an effort
that surprised him, because his hands wanted to. Nine days of surveying
a place and the hands simply expected to be allowed to improve it — a
rung here, a stop there, a spur in a rebate — and every time the want
came up he put it down, because a rung in a wall six feet from a body is
not a fixture. It is a confession, mortared in, and it will still be
there in eighty years.

So the crystal went where nobody would ever look at a body.

Forty yards back along the row, where the gangway met the yard gate, the
gate's staple had a hair of blue-grey inside it now, grown one night on
his palm, invisible unless you had the staple out and a glass — enough
that the gate would foul on its keeper and stand three inches proud
instead of swinging. A gate that will not swing is a gate men go round.
Going round put a man on the gangway who might otherwise have taken the
yard.

At the east end, the second step of the water stair had a ridge on it
the thickness of a coin, under the lip, where a foot going *down* in the
dark would not meet it and a foot coming *up* fast would.

At the bend, in the joint of the old dock's coping, a cleat the size of
a door hook. He had put one exactly like it under a road at Tomari and
had hung off it for eleven hours with the tide in his mouth. This one
was for going over the edge into the water with something in his other
hand.

Nothing else. Three pieces, none within forty yards of where it would
happen, none of them ever to be found by anybody, none of them touching
the man at all.

He lay in his lodging on the last night and went over it, and it was
good work, and he knew it was good work, and the knowing sat in him like
a swallowed stone.

He was fifteen years and eleven months old. He had no jutsu. He had a
bad knee, no reach, no mass, a stick of oak taken off him by a
professional on a beach, and a trade in ornamental stonework. And in
nine days, alone, from nothing, with no instruction whatever, he had
built a killing out of a mender's survey, a fish-knife, and three
fixtures nobody would ever see.

*You wanted to know what you were,* he thought, in the dark, in the flat
voice he was starting to think in. *There it is. You're a man who is
good at this.*

Downstairs the lodging-house woman was banking the stove for the night,
and somewhere off toward the salt quay a dog was going through its
repertoire, and out on the black water a lighter went down toward the
sea with one lamp at its stern and a man on it singing something in a
dialect Sazare did not know.

He did not sleep, and he did not fortify the room, and in the morning
there would be a queue at the door with broken things in its hands, and
he would mend eleven of them, carefully and well and for very little
money, and one of them would belong to a man in a blue coat.
