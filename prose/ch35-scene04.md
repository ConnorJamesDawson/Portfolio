# Chapter 35, Scene 4 — The Check Was Running

*Act 4, part C. Konoha General, the corridor. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,770 words.*

---

Yagi came up from surgical still in the apron and did not take it off,
which meant she had been got out of something.

She was thirty-eight and had been thirty-four when he met her and the
four years had gone into her the way they went into everybody in that
building. The voice was the same. She had cut her hair short at some
point in the winter and it suited her and he had not said so.

She stopped four feet away and looked at him in the relatives' chair
for about two seconds.

"Right," she said. "Up. Walk with me."

"Emiko said to stay—"

"Emiko said stay so you didn't go and hide in the sluice, and now I'm
here and we're going to walk, because you have never in your life
managed a conversation sitting still and I am not going to start
requiring it today."

They walked.

The second-floor corridor ran ninety-one feet from the desk to the
window at the men's end and they did it eleven times, which he counted,
because he counted everything, and because counting was one of the two
things still working.

---

"Emiko says five weeks."

"She asked when I last slept a full night. It's not the same question."

"It's exactly the same question and you know it is." Yagi put her hands
in the apron pocket. "Start where you like. I've got forty minutes and
Fujita's closing without me and he'll cope."

Sazare walked eleven feet.

"There's a form," he said.

"Yes."

"It's four lines and it has to be in my handwriting, and I've tried
eleven times and I can't finish the word *clan*, and I have written
down in my own notebook that the reason is a technical query about the
relationship clause, and that's not the reason, and I knew it wasn't
the reason when I wrote it down."

"Good. That's the hardest sentence and you've led with it." She did not
break stride. "What's the reason?"

And there it was.

The gap.

It ran the way it had run since he was seven — the quarter-second, the
check, *what does this tell them, what can be done with it, is there a
shorter version, is there a version that gives away less* — and it ran
on a sentence he had constructed at two in the morning four nights
earlier and had deliberately not written down, and it stopped it dead.

He walked nine feet in silence.

"I don't know," he said.

"That's not true either."

"No."

"Right." Yagi turned at the window and started back. "Then we'll do it
the other way, because I'm not going to stand here and win an argument
with you, you're better at arguments than I am and you'll enjoy it and
we'll both go home."

"What's the other way?"

"I'll tell you what I've observed and you correct the ones that are
wrong. You're very good at correcting things that are wrong. It's about
the only reliable way I've ever got anything out of you."

---

She did it in a flat clinical register, walking, without looking at
him, in the tone she used for handover.

"Five weeks of broken sleep, self-reported and understated. Eleven
unrostered attendances in a fortnight. Two dropped items in a man who
has never dropped anything. One repeated observation on Tuesday that
you didn't notice repeating. Weight — I'd say six pounds, maybe seven,
and don't argue with me because I've watched you lift and your belt's
moved a hole."

"That's—"

"I said correct the wrong ones."

He walked.

"None of them are wrong," he said.

"Two more. You have not been to the library since the delegation, and
Ochiai asked Ashida about you, and Ashida asked me, because neither of
them wanted to ask you." She turned at the desk end. "And you have not
been at your bench. That's Ōtaki Sue. She hears it through the floor
and she says it's stopped and she wrote it on a slate for the boy who
carries your tools and made him bring it to the gate."

Sazare stopped walking.

They were about halfway down, by the sluice door, and there was an
orderly at the far end with a linen trolley who took one look and went
the other way.

"That's four people," he said.

"That's four people who noticed and didn't want to say, and one of them
is seventy-four and deaf and worked it out through a *floor*." Yagi
stopped too. "You have been walking round this building for five weeks
running on the thing you always run on, which is that if you complete
every task correctly then nothing needs to be said. And the tasks have
been correct. That's not in dispute. I'd put you on my floor tomorrow."

"Then what's the problem?"

"That you've been doing them at the eleventh hour on a Sunday when
you're not rostered, because the alternative is being in a room with a
box in it."

---

He did not say anything for some time.

Yagi did not fill it, which was the single most useful thing she did
that afternoon.

"I can't do it," he said eventually.

"Do what?"

"Any of this." He heard his own voice do something and did not have the
resources to stop it. "Four nights ago I had it. I sat in a bar with
the Hokage and I said eleven things I'd never said to anybody, about
the bench, about my mother, about what I wanted to be, and it came out
of me like — like a tap, and it was *easy*, and I went home under a
lamp and worked out exactly why, which is that alcohol switches off the
part of me that checks. That's all it was. A chemical switched off a
process."

"And?"

"And it's running now." He put a hand flat on the sluice door frame.
"I've been standing in this corridor for nine minutes trying to say one
sentence to you and it will not come past. And I know what it is. I've
known what it is for four days. I could tell you the whole of it in
about forty seconds and I *cannot make it go*."

"Then don't say it."

He looked at her.

"Say something else," said Yagi. "Anything. It doesn't have to be the
sentence. You've decided there's one thing that has to be said and
that everything else is a failure, and that's you doing what you always
do, which is deciding the standard and then failing your own standard
and calling it evidence."

"That's not—"

"Ruri. You came off a bridge in the Land of Waves with a shattered knee
and you didn't tell anybody for two years." She said it without any
heat at all. "You do not have to produce the whole of it. Produce four
words."

---

It took him, by the corridor clock, another eleven minutes.

Yagi stood there for all eleven of them, in an apron, with a surgeon
closing without her, and did not speak once.

What he got out, in the end, was:

"If I file it and they refuse it, then somebody will have decided."

Yagi waited.

"At the moment nobody's decided," said Sazare. "The register says
extinct but that's not a decision, it's a clerk working off a list. If
I put my name on that form somebody looks at the question properly and
writes an answer on the bottom of it, and there's a good chance the
answer's no, and then the last thing that ever happens to my family is
a clerk in Kirigakure deciding they don't count and going to lunch."

He stopped.

"That's it," he said. "That's the whole of it. It took me four days and
eleven attempts and it's four sentences."

"It's four sentences," Yagi agreed.

"It's four sentences and I have been unable to say them for five weeks
and I dropped a kidney dish."

"Yes."

"That's — " He put both hands over his face, briefly, which he had
never done in front of another person in his life. "I don't know what
that is. There isn't a name for it. I've checked. I've checked the
whole way through and there's no unit on it and I can't measure it and
I don't know whether it's getting better or worse because I don't know
what it's *doing*."

And Nurse Yagi, who had taught him to close a wound on his own forearm
and had held a kneecap in place for forty-three minutes and had never
in four years told him a comfortable thing, said:

"It's grief, love. It's just grief. It's been coming for six years and
it's arrived in a corridor because you finally stood still long enough
for it to catch up."

---

He did not cry.

He wanted, afterwards, to be able to say that he had, because it would
have been tidier and because everybody he ever half-told this to
assumed it, and it was not what happened.

What happened was that he stood in a corridor with his hands over his
face for about ninety seconds while a nurse in a surgical apron stood
four feet away and did not touch him and did not speak, and then he
took his hands down, and his face was dry, and he said:

"I've got the four o'clock."

"No you haven't. I've given it to Tomoe."

"She's got the fever ward—"

"I've given it to Tomoe," said Yagi, "and you're going to sit in the
staff room for an hour and eat something, and then you're going to go
home, and tomorrow you're on a late so you can sleep, and I have
already told Kaburagi and she said, and I am quoting her exactly,
*about time.*"

"She knew?"

"Ruri." Yagi took the apron off at last and folded it over her arm.
"Everybody knew. That's what I've been telling you for forty minutes.
You have been surrounded for five weeks by people who noticed on the
first day and were waiting for you to come and say a word, any word,
and the only reason none of us came and got you sooner is that every
single one of us has watched you handle four hundred things by
yourself and we were all extremely worried about being the one who
insulted you."

She started back toward the stairs.

"For what it's worth," she said, over her shoulder, "Emiko lasted the
longest and she cracked at nine seconds. So none of us are as good at
it as we thought."
