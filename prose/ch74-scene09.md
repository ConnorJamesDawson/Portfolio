# Chapter 74, Scene 9 — Clause Five

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the tower, the second floor. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,490 words.*

---

He told her about the yard afterwards, upstairs, doing her hair,
because Thursday had no entries in it and the hair was the one place
where things got said that were not going anywhere.

He gave her the whole of it: the thumb; the ceiling; the want, written
down in his own hand at two o'clock so that it could not run the audit
from underneath; the three legs; the correction to leg two, which he
delivered verbatim, because verbatim was what it deserved.

Her hands stopped on the twenty-second pass at *I would have said yes
because I want to be kept*, and stayed stopped for a while, and then
went on.

"I signed that attachment order," said Tsunade, eventually.

"You did."

"Two lines of it are mine and Kakashi wrote the third, and I have read
it about forty times since, and there is nothing in it — not one word
— that lets that man say no to *you*." She resumed. "I put a man who
was raised to be useful eight feet from the most interesting work in
this village and I never once asked what that would do to him. You had
to be told by the man himself in a frost-bleached yard."

"He told me on purpose. It was disclosure and it cost him and it was
not in any remit."

"I know what it was." Her voice had gone somewhere flat and careful.
"I'm going to fix the order. Not tomorrow — I'm going to think about
it properly, because a clumsy fix is worse and I have watched what
happens to people when the office gets sentimental about them. But it
gets fixed."

Then he showed her the button.

She turned it over twice under the lamp — pale close-grained
something, a sixteenth too thick, four holes off square, nine passes
of a knife round the chamfer — and did not say any of the four things
he had projected on the walk home.

"Sew it on," she said.

"I cannot sew."

"You run a garment line."

"I run the fabrication side of a garment line and there are four
women on the board who consider my needlework a matter of public
safety." He turned the coat over in his hands. "I was going to put it
in the inner pocket."

"You were going to put it in a *pocket*." She held her hand out for
the coat, and he gave it to her, and she went and got the housewife
tin off the shelf that had six things on it, and sat down under the
good lamp with the coat across her knees. "That man wore it in the
open for eighteen years on the side of himself people look at. In a
pocket it's a keepsake. Sit down, fabricator, and don't watch — I'm
out of practice and my hands are for larger work."

Her hands were not out of practice.

He watched her do it anyway, from the correct end of the room, and
she put it third down on the left, in the row, between the second and
the fourth of his own plain dark ones, and she cut the thread with
her teeth like a field medic and gave the coat back.

*Entry, not written, because it is Thursday and the rule holds and I
am composing this in my head where it will keep,* he thought.

*Eleven years ago I worked out that you read a man by the four or
five wrong objects on him — the ones nobody issued, the ones he
picked. I have used it on eleven hundred people. I used it on Tenzō
in ninety seconds in the eighth month and found a policy where the
objects should have been.*

*I have never once run it on myself.*

*Ran it tonight, in a chair, with a coat on my knees. Findings: issue
kit, plain, correct, chosen for function in every particular. The
sword, which I made to a specification. The notebook, which is a tool.
Boots that fit.*

*Nothing. Eleven years in this trade and a man could have read me off
a wall at forty feet and found a policy, and I would not have known
what he was looking at.*

*I have one now. It is third down on the left, it sits proud, it will
catch on things for the rest of my life, and it was made badly on
purpose by somebody who could have grown it perfectly in a second.*

*It is not a keepsake. She was right about the pocket. It is a
statement of position and it is on the side of me people look at.*

---

She went and got the minute book at about ten.

He knew what was happening the moment she stood up, because she stood
up in the specific way she stood up when she was going to do something
in a format, and by the time she came back with the fitting file under
her arm and sat down in the chair opposite — not beside him; opposite,
across the low table, at negotiating distance — he had run the
projection and arrived and his pulse was doing something disgraceful.

"Specification session," said Tsunade, in the flat administrative
voice, opening the file. "Lower half, full set. Reconvened, the
nineteenth of the third month, twenty-two hundred, present: the model
and the fabricator. Minutes to be taken by the fabricator, who is
required to record specifications verbatim."

"I do not have my—"

"You have four notebooks with nothing in the front of them," said
Tsunade, "and I want this one written down."

He got the pen out. His hand was not entirely level and he elected not
to conceal it.

"Clauses one through four: signed off, delivered, worn, and the
model's satisfaction was notified through the usual channels at the
usual hour in the usual detail, and I would like the record to show
that the notification took *two months* and that the article performed
throughout." She turned the page. "Clause five.

"I drafted all five on the night of the fourth of the second month,
and I wrote that one last, after the other four, in about ninety
seconds, and then I sealed the sheet and put it in this file and gave
you the first four across a calibration rig, and I have been carrying
the fact of it around for six weeks like a brick. Ready?"

"...Ready."

She read it out.

"**Clause five. The article is not consumable.**

"**It is not to be replaced. Not on wear, not on failure, not on
obsolescence, and no successor article is to be manufactured at any
point by any hand.**

"**On failure — seam, fastening, fabric, or structure, whole or part
— it returns to the fabricator, who repairs it by hand, and the
repair is not to be concealed.**

"**Where the fabricator is unavailable, the article waits.**"

She closed the file.

"Read it back, fabricator," said Tsunade. "Those are the terms."

---

He did not read it back for some time, because he was not able to.

*Take it apart,* he thought, when the room came back. *That is what
you have. Take it apart the way you take everything apart, because
you are about eleven seconds from doing something that neither of you
will be able to hold, and the parts are the only place you have ever
been safe.*

*So: parts.*

*Clauses one to four specify the article by reference to the MODEL.
Coverage against her line. Waist against her navel. Drape against her
seat. Every one of them is a dimension taken off a body, and that is
what a garment specification is, and I have written four hundred of
them.*

*Clause five does not mention the model once.*

*Clause five specifies the article by reference to the MAKER. Repair
by the fabricator, by hand. No successor by any hand. Waits, where the
fabricator is unavailable.*

*There is no engineering reason for a single word of that. None.
Repair-by-original-maker is not a garment term; it is a term for
articles that cannot be reproduced, and I could reproduce that set in
four days, and she knows it, because she watched me make it.*

*Therefore it is not a garment clause. It has never been a garment
clause. It is a clause about a maker, laid in a garment file, in the
board voice, in ninety seconds, on the night of the fourth of the
second month —*

*— which is five weeks and three days before the fourteenth of the
third month.*

*Before the format. Before the papers. Before either of us had said
one word of any of it out loud.*

*She wrote it on a night when the only available channel between us
was a fitting file, and she put the whole of it in the only language she
had, and then she sealed the sheet, because in the second month the
sentence "no successor article is to be manufactured" would have said
the thing neither of us could survive being said.*

*And she has carried it for six weeks and disclosed it tonight,
after the week I have had, in the format, at negotiating distance,
across a table — because she does not give things, she MINUTES them.*

*Where the fabricator is unavailable, the article waits.*

*She has buried everybody she ever wanted. Every one. And she sat up
on the night of the fourth of the second month and wrote a clause
which says, in commercial language, on a form, that there is not
going to be a next one, and that if I am not here the thing I made
her simply stops, and she wrote it before she had any way of knowing
whether she would ever get to say it.*

He read it back.

He got as far as *no successor article is to be manufactured at any
point by any hand* and his voice went, comprehensively, for the first
time in front of her in three years, and he sat with the pen in his
hand and let it be gone, because the record got the whole of it or the
record was worth nothing.

"Take your time," said Tsunade, from the other chair, not moving, in
a voice that was not doing very much better. "The minutes will keep."

"...The clause is accepted," he said, when he had it back. "Without
amendment. And I am entering one line under it, as the fabricator,
and it is the only thing I have ever put in a spec file that is not a
measurement."

"Enter it."

"*Received, understood, and reciprocated in every particular by the
undersigned, who has no successor either.*"

---

They did not say anything for a while, which was the correct
engineering response.

And then he did the thing he had been intending to do since Sunday,
because it was a security finding and it did not stop being one for
being raised in the pleasantest room in his life.

"There is one more item and it is the ugly one."

"The twenty-six hours," said Tsunade.

"The twenty-six hours." He turned the pen. "Saturday afternoon to
Sunday evening. Both of us out of this building, together,
unaccounted for, and the entire cover of it rests on one woman
writing the word *private* in a diary. Shizune-san is the finest
signatory anybody ever had and she is one person, and a cover that
depends on one person is not a cover, it is a *friendship*, and I
have written in another part of my life this week that a cover with
no expiry on it stops being a cover and becomes a house."

"And ours has no expiry on it."

"Ours has *daylight* on it, which is a direction and not a condition.
'The day the office can afford it and not one day sooner.' I have
re-read that page every night since the twenty-fifth of the second
month and I have loved it every time and it does not have an
acceptance test in it." He looked up. "You gave me one on Monday over
a tray. *Make it something you can be caught failing at.* I have spent
four days finding out what that clause does to a creed and it is the
most useful sentence anybody has given me this year and I would like
to point it at us."

She was quiet for a moment.

"You want me to say what *afford* means."

"I want a condition a reasonable person could check. Not a date.
Nobody can write a date and I would not accept one. A condition. And I
would like it in your hand under the treaty, and I would like both of
us to be able to look at it every year and say whether it has
happened."

*And she is going to decline,* he thought, *and she is going to be
right to, because it is the largest question in her life and I have
just asked it across a coffee table at eleven at night in the middle
of the best evening of my—*

"The day," said Tsunade, "that somebody could take this hat off me on
a Tuesday, and the lists would still go up on the Wednesday."

He stopped.

"That's it," she said. "That's the whole condition. Not the war, not
the council, not the treaty office. When this village would survive
the hat changing hands on no notice — when it isn't held together by
one fifty-three-year-old being personally irreplaceable — then my
private life stops being leverage, because there'd be nothing to lever
*against*. Until then, anybody who finds out about you owns a piece of
the Land of Fire.

"It's checkable. You can check it. I check it about once a month and
the answer has been no every single time, and I would like it
minuted, since we're minuting, that the answer has been *slightly
less* no for about four months and I decline to say why."

Sazare wrote it in the back of the book, under the treaty, in the
small exact hand, and she took the pen off him and signed it, both
names, the second one larger, and put the pen down.

He did not ask who she had in mind.

He had known since a Tuesday in the old quarter with a bowl in front
of him, and she had never said it, and she was not going to say it
tonight, and a man who took things that were not specified was a man
he had spent his whole life declining to be.

*Not mine,* he thought, and shut the book. *She'll say it when she
says it.*

*And when she does I am going to have to be extremely careful about
my face, which will not be a problem, because I do not have one.*
