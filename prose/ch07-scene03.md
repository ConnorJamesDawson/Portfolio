# Chapter 7, Scene 3 — The Wrong Way

*Act 1, beat 19. Sazare Ruri, age twelve. POV: Sazare. ~2,500 words.*

---

It started at about the tenth hour of the night and he was out in it because the
salt shed had been cleared on the Tuesday.

Four men had come through it at the sixth hour of the morning with a list of
nothing in particular, and had moved everybody on, and had not been rough about it,
and had told the two women who lived at the far end that there were places they
could go — which there were, and which everybody in the shed knew were places you
did not come out of the same as you went in.

So Sazare had been sleeping in a stairwell off the fish market for six nights, and
had been moved out of it twice, and on the seventh night he had given up and gone
walking, because walking was warmer than not walking.

That was the whole reason he was in the upper district.

He had gone up because up was quieter, and because there was a run of covered
walkway behind the tax office where nobody went at night, and because he had four
sen and had worked out that if he did not eat until the following evening he would
still have four sen.

---

The first thing was a bell.

Not the harbour bell. Something higher up and further in, ringing badly — not a
rhythm, just a man pulling a rope as hard as he could — and it went for about
fifteen seconds and then stopped in the middle of a stroke.

Sazare stood still on the covered walkway.

The second thing was the light going out.

The upper district had lamps. That was one of the things about it; the lower
district had lamps at the bridges and nowhere else, and the upper district had them
all the way along, and they were the reason it was quiet at night, because you
could see people.

They went out along the length of the street in front of him, one after another,
in about four seconds, in order.

Then the noise started.

---

He ran the wrong way.

There was no reason for it. He ran up the hill and inward, towards the noise,
because that was the direction he had been facing when it started, and because he
was twelve years old and had spent eleven weeks being extremely careful about food
and sleeping and had never once in his life had to decide anything at speed.

He got about two hundred yards.

Then the street he was in stopped being a street he was in.

He never had a clear account of it. He had a set of pieces that would not go
together: a wall of the tax office coming apart sideways with a noise like a wave
hitting a hull; water going up the whole height of a building and standing there;
a man in the road in front of him, quite close, who was not on his feet and was
still moving; three people going over the roofline above him at a speed that made
no sense; and the fog, which had not been there, which had come up off the harbour
in about a minute and a half and turned the whole hill into a white room with
things happening in it.

He got into a doorway.

He got into a doorway because he could not see and because his legs had stopped
being an argument he was winning, and he put his back against it and pulled his
knees up and made himself as small as the doorway would allow, which was the first
sensible thing he had done since the bell.

---

He was there a long time. Possibly ten minutes.

Twice, people went past. Once it was a man running who fell over about six feet
past him and got up and kept going. Once it was three of them moving together, and
they came through the fog and past the doorway and were gone, and none of them
looked at him, and he did not breathe for the whole of it.

Then something came down the street that was not running.

He could hear it from a good way off, which was the wrong way round; the fog took
everything, and the fog was not taking this. It was a single set of footfalls, not
hurried, and something dragging, and — under it, so low he felt it in the boards
of the door behind him more than heard it — a sound like a heavy sheet of metal
being turned over.

The fog came apart about eight feet in front of the doorway.

---

He was enormous.

That was the only thing Sazare's mind would supply for about a second and a half.
He was enormous and he was bare to the waist, in the fourth month, in the fog, and
there was blood on him from the shoulder down and it was not, from the way he was
moving, his.

He had something across his back that was too big to be a sword and was a sword.

He came level with the doorway and stopped.

He did not turn his head much. He turned it enough, and looked down, and Sazare
looked up at a face he had seen once before over the top of a fried thing on a
wall by the third bridge, four years earlier, and had not stopped thinking about
for a fortnight afterwards.

Zabuza looked at him for perhaps a second.

Then his head came up and round, sharply, towards the top of the street.

And he reached down and took a fistful of Sazare's coat at the shoulder and threw
him.

---

There was no other word for it. He did not pull him out, or push him aside, or
move him. He picked a twelve-year-old up off the floor of a doorway one-handed
without appearing to notice that he had done it and threw him about nine feet
sideways into the mouth of an alley, and Sazare hit the wall with his shoulder and
his hip and went down in the wet.

And the doorway went.

He was looking at it when it happened, from the ground, from the side, which is
the only reason he saw it at all: the whole front of the building — door, frame,
the two feet of wall either side, the step — came in and forward and downward all
at once, as though something enormous had leaned on it from inside, and where he
had been sitting there was suddenly about four feet of stone.

Water went past the mouth of the alley at chest height, running uphill.

---

He did not see what happened next. He heard about eleven seconds of it.

Then Zabuza came into the mouth of the alley, unhurried, and stood over him, and
Sazare lay in the wet with his shoulder screaming and looked up.

He was not, close up, frightening in the way Sazare had spent four years imagining.
He was breathing hard. He had a cut over the eye that was in the way, and he kept
having to blink it clear, and he looked — Sazare could not make it fit and could
not make it go away either — he looked like a man who had been up all night doing
something difficult that had not worked.

"You're in a doorway," said Zabuza.

Sazare said nothing.

"On a *hill*," said Zabuza, "in a *fog*, on *tonight*." He wiped his eye with the
back of his wrist. "Whose are you?"

Sazare's mouth said, "Ruri," before any part of him had decided anything.

Something went over Zabuza's face. It was there and gone in about a quarter of a
second and Sazare, who was twelve, and who was lying in a puddle with a dislocated
shoulder, read it as nothing at all at the time and spent the rest of his life
assembling it.

"Right," said Zabuza.

Then, from somewhere up the hill, a voice shouted two words, and Zabuza's head
turned, and when he looked back down at Sazare his face had nothing in it
whatsoever.

"Go *home*," he said. "Idiot."

And he went up the alley at a speed that made no sense for a man that size, and
the fog closed, and that was the whole of it.

---

Sazare lay in the wet for a while.

Then he got up, which took three attempts because of the shoulder, and stood
holding his own elbow and looking at four feet of stone where a doorway had been.

He worked it out on the way down the hill and he had to stop twice on the way to
be sick.

There was no version of it where Zabuza had saved him. He knew that. He knew it at
twelve and he never once, afterwards, allowed himself to pretend otherwise. A man
in the middle of something like that does not stop to move a child; he moves a
thing out of the way of the thing he is about to do, and the thing being a child is
a detail nobody has time for, and if the doorway had been empty he would have come
down that street at exactly the same speed and it would have gone exactly the same
way.

He had been in the way. That was all. He had been in the way, and had been removed
from the way, by a man who had then gone on up the hill about his own business.

He knew all that.

What he could not get rid of was the quarter of a second.

He went at it the way he went at everything, which was to lay it out and turn it
over and look for the place it was moving. A man in the middle of a failed coup,
bleeding, with two words being shouted at him from the top of the hill, had asked a
child in a doorway whose he was — and had waited for the answer.

That was the part. He had *waited for the answer*. It had cost him perhaps a second
and there had been nothing in it for him, and he had done it anyway, and then
something had gone across his face and gone again.

Sazare could account for all of it except that, and he could not make that come out
to anything at all, and he took it out and looked at it about once a week for a
very long time and never got any further with it than he had got sitting in the wet
at the bottom of the alley.

---

The coup failed.

He learned that the way he learned everything now, which was in a queue, from
somebody's aunt: that Zabuza Momochi had gone at the Mizukage in the night with a
dozen men and had not got within two rooms of him, and that most of the dozen were
dead, and that Zabuza was not, and had gone over the west wall before dawn and out
of the country.

Nobody said the word *rebellion*. Everybody said *that business on the hill*.

For eight days afterwards the village was very quiet and very polite and there
were a great many men on the bridges, and then, in the way of these things, it
stopped being extraordinary, and the fish came in, and the market went back to
normal.

Sazare's shoulder went back in on the fourth day, against a wall, by himself,
which is not how you are supposed to do it and which cost him about a third of the
range in that arm for the rest of his life.

He was twelve years and four months old, and he had had, for perhaps two minutes
in the fog, the first adult he had spoken to in eleven weeks who had known who the
Ruri were.

And the adult had left the country.
