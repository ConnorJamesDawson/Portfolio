# Chapter 43, Scene 7 — Two Columns

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard and the room over the
persimmon tree. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,586 words.*

---

He brought her the notebook on the Wednesday and he brought only the
notebook.

He had thought about it for four days and had constructed, at various
points, nine different sentences to go with it, and had discarded all
nine on the grounds that every one of them was a door held open, and a
door held open is still a thing you have done to somebody.

So he put it on the felt at the clear end of the bench, open at the
right page, with a slip of paper marking the four entries from the
eleventh month, and said:

"Retainer first. It's the four entries under the marker. My hand's bad
and the abbreviations are mine, so if a word doesn't work, ask, and
I'll be in the yard till five."

And then he went and stood at the other bench and did his own work with
his back to her, which took some doing.

---

She was there rather more than an hour.

He heard the page turn eleven times, which was more turns than there
were pages under the marker, and at some point he heard her put the
lens down on the stand and not pick it up again.

When she came over she had the notebook closed around one finger.

"Your hand's not bad," she said. "It's fast. There's a difference and
you'd know it if anybody had ever made you write for a living."

"Was it clear enough?"

"It was clear enough." She put it on the bench between them. "You wrote
down what he said. Not what it meant. Not your version — you've got
your version underneath in a bracket and you've kept it separate, and
that is the single most useful thing anybody has done for me in two
years."

"It's how I keep everything."

"I know. That's why it's useful." She opened it again and turned it
round. "This one."

It was the third of the four, and it was mostly a diagram, and the line
she had her thumb beside was not technique at all. It was a
parenthetical, in his own smaller hand, the kind he put in as a matter
of course because a bench note without conditions was a bench note
about nothing:

*(Slow this evening. Sat down twice. Second time mid-sentence and
finished the sentence sitting, which he has not done before. Room cold
— fire not lit, wood in.)*

Kurihara read it again with him watching, which he understood he was
not supposed to be doing, and did not look away, because looking away
would have been a decision about her and he did not have the standing
to make one.

"Right," she said.

She put her hand flat on the page for a moment. Not on the diagram. On
the bracket.

Then she took it off and turned the book back round.

---

"I'll tell you what it means," she said, in an entirely different
voice, the working one. "The reorder. You won't have it and there's no
reason you would."

"Go on."

"He reordered a sequence four times in the six years I was with him.
Every one of the four, it was because he'd worked out the same thing
could be taught in fewer steps." She said it steadily. "He didn't do it
to make the work better. He never once made the work better by
reordering it, and he'd tell you that himself — he'd say the work is
the work and the order is the *ladder*. He reordered when he wanted
somebody up the ladder faster."

Sazare put down the file he had been holding for some minutes without
using.

"He was compressing a syllabus."

"He was compressing a syllabus, in the eleventh month, for one student,
and the one student was you, and I can't tell you whether he knew what
was coming in the second month." Kurihara squared the notebook against
the edge of the bench. "So I'm not giving you an answer. I'm giving you
the habit. What you do with it is yours."

"It moves the number."

"What number?"

"I had it at eight in ten that he knew," said Sazare. "Since the second
month. It's in a list of things I've written down that I can't resolve
and have stopped trying to."

"And now?"

He thought about it properly, because she had earned that.

"Nine," he said. "And it stays at nine, because a habit over four
instances across six years is exactly one item of evidence and I'm not
going to let it be more than that just because I want it to be."

Kurihara looked at him for a second and a half.

"He'd have liked that," she said, and picked up the lens, and went back
to the second row, and that was the whole of it.

He did not stay, and he did not ask her anything, and he took the
notebook home.

---

He ruled the page that night, and it took him longer than the writing
did.

Two columns, which he had headed nine days ago and left empty on one
side because he had nothing honest to put under it. He had five things
now and he put all five in.

*FAULT — OUTPUT*

*Led with the finding, lost eleven people in nine words. — Nothing.
Cost only. Enter it under cost and leave it there.*

*Corrected Ōkubo in public over four points that changed nothing. —
Ōkubo, volunteer, and a man of fifty-six saying out loud that there is
nobody left who will tell him a number is wrong.*

*Withheld the drafted letter six weeks, knowing why. — Disclosed at
cost. Established the only thing that could not be established any
other way, which is what I do after.*

*Built a covert trial and presented its defensible half first, without
deciding to. — Objection sustained. Scheme improved by the constraint.
Notice goes up Monday. Nine-tenths of the problem solved by the route I
was bored by.*

*Eleven resisted falls. — One committed fall, and Hatake putting the
governor on my mouth, which I designed four weeks ago in stone and
never once thought to point at myself.*

He sat back and looked at the shape of it.

Then he wrote the finding, and it took three attempts, and he kept the
third.

*Four of five faults this month produced something I could not have
designed. That is not a normal month and I am not going to pretend it
is a rate.*

*The finding is smaller than the column looks. It is this: I have kept
fault reports since I was seven and every one of them had one column,
because I believed a fault was a thing with a cost and a lesson and
nothing else in it. That was not caution. It was an accounting error.
I have been writing half a ledger for eleven years and calling it
rigour.*

*A fault in good faith has an output. Sometimes. Unreliably. And the
column exists, and a book kept without it is not more honest than a
book kept with it, it is just shorter.*

He looked at that for a while and then wrote the guard underneath,
because the flattering half had gone in first again and it was becoming
a habit he did not entirely trust.

*Now the part I have to be careful about, and I am writing it on the
same page rather than a different one so I cannot read one without the
other.*

*Every one of the four was a fault I did not choose. I made all of them
by accident, in good faith, doing the thing I was going to do anyway,
and the output arrived afterwards and surprised me.*

*The moment I choose one, it is not a fault. It is a tactic wearing a
fault's coat, and it will work, and it will work on decent people
specifically, because a decent person's whole method for reading
somebody is watching what they do when they have got something wrong.*

*I know it works because it is being done to me, four hundred miles
away, in a courteous hand, by a man who told me he would think less of
me for answering quickly.*

*So: the column stays. And nothing goes in it that I put there on
purpose.*

---

He wrote one more line before he put the pencil down, and it was four
words long, and he had been carrying it since the walk up the hill from
the Division and had not been able to set it down since.

He had gone to Ibiki that afternoon, before the yard, and had said yes,
and had said it in one sentence and had not qualified it, and Ibiki had
said *good* and nothing else and had not looked pleased, which Sazare
had appreciated more than he could have accounted for at the time.

*The letter goes on the second Thursday of next month.*

*Eleven hundred words, unaltered, in the honest version, and it will
help him, and I have been told the fee and I have agreed to it in
advance and in writing, and I would like whoever reads this to have the
fee and the agreement in the same entry and in that order.*

He closed the notebook.

The persimmon was in full leaf now and the window had been open for
four nights, and the shoulder did not hurt at all, and he got about six
hours.
