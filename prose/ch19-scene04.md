# Chapter 19, Scene 4 — The Girl on the Trunk

*Act 3, part A. The woods above Nami. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~2,500 words.*

---

He went up into the woods on the Friday for a piece of hornbeam, because
the plane iron he had traded four hinges for wanted a body to sit in and
nothing else in that country was hard enough, and he found the hornbeam in
about twenty minutes and then spent the next two hours doing something
extremely stupid.

The woods above Nami went up the hill in three bands. Scrub and bramble at
the bottom where the town had been at it for firewood; then a middle belt
of oak and hornbeam and something with a leaf like a hand, all of it
turning, so that the light coming down through it that morning was the
colour of weak beer and lay in long flat bars across the slope; and above
that the black-green pine, straight and spaced and quiet, with the ground
under it dry and rust-brown and giving off that hot resin smell even in
the eleventh month.

He was in the middle belt with the hornbeam under his arm when he heard
the shouting.

It came up from a clearing about two hundred yards along the contour and
he stood absolutely still and listened to it for a full minute, and every
single thing he had learned in four years told him to go back down the
hill.

He did not go back down the hill.

He went along the contour, slowly, on the balls of his feet, in a
drainage cut that ran the way he wanted to go and had a lip on it; and he
came up under a fallen oak with the root plate standing up on end and a
screen of bramble in front of it, and lay down in the leaf mould behind
the root plate with his chin on his forearms, and looked through.

He had no business at all being there and he knew it while he was doing
it. There was a jōnin in that household. There was a jōnin in that
household who had read him off a doorway in a quarter of a second without
turning his head, and if that man had been in the clearing Sazare would
have been dead or standing up with his hands out inside ninety seconds.

He did it anyway. He had done it at ten, on a wall, with his sister
coming up the hill in the fog; and at thirteen, in a drainage cutting
under a road; and at fifteen in an inn doorway in the rain with a bounty
book in his hands.

Curiosity went past every instruction he had, the way it always did,
without looking.

---

The clearing was about forty yards across with the pine coming down one
side of it, and there were three trees in it that had been *used*.

He read those first, because he read objects before he read people and
always had. Three big pines standing apart, and up the trunk of each one,
in a rising line from about four feet to — he put his head back — to about
sixty, was a ladder of cut marks. Not cuts: gouges. Two inches long, an
inch deep, made by something driven in hard and levered out sideways. A
knife-toe. A blade in a boot.

They went up in pairs. Left, right, left, right, in a climbing rhythm, and
about a third of the way up each tree the rhythm went ragged and the pairs
got wider apart and then there were two or three gouges in a bunch where
somebody had tried to save themselves, and then nothing.

Three trees. Three separate heights of failure, four or five weeks of
somebody's life in the bark.

*They're not climbing them,* he thought. *You don't need a knife to climb a
pine.*

Then he looked at the clearing and worked out what he was looking at, and
it took him about six seconds and it was the most important six seconds of
his year.

---

The dark-haired boy was running at the middle tree.

He came in fast from about fifteen feet with his hands in a shape in front
of his chest, and he hit the trunk and went *up* it — running, on the
vertical, four strides, five, six, eight, with his body out at right
angles to the trunk and his arms swinging, exactly as a man runs on flat
ground — and got to about forty feet, and on the ninth stride his foot
came away and he flipped backward off the trunk, cut the bark on the way
past with the knife in his hand, and landed in a crouch in the leaf litter
with about as much fuss as a dropped coat.

He stood up, looked at the mark he had made, and said something short and
unpleasant to himself.

Sazare lay behind the root plate with his fingers in the leaf mould and
did not breathe.

He understood the mechanism instantly and completely, because it was the
only thing in the world he actually understood: *that is control.* That is
the still water, poured out through the soles of the feet, in a quantity
that must be exact and must be held while everything else about the body
is moving. Too little and you come off. Too much and you blow the bark out
and come off anyway. And it is not a static hold, it is a *hold under
load*, changing with every stride, on a curved surface, at speed.

He had spent eight months on a bench above a river trying to hold one
piece of grown stone together for longer than four seconds.

The dark-haired boy took nine strides up a tree at forty feet and lost it
on the ninth, and was annoyed about the ninth.

---

The one in orange was worse and louder and going at it in a completely
different way.

He did not run at the tree so much as attack it. Three strides, four, and
then a burst of light off the soles of his feet that Sazare could see from
sixty yards — visible, wasteful, a foot of bark going out of the trunk in
splinters — and then he came off backward, arms going, and hit the ground
flat on his back with an impact that Sazare felt through the leaf mould.

He lay there for a second.

Then he sat up and shouted something at the tree, and got up, and did it
again.

Sazare watched him do it eleven times in about twenty minutes. He kept the
count without deciding to. Eleven attempts; two of them worse than the
first; one of them — the eighth — noticeably better, high enough that the
boy actually yelled with pleasure at the top before he came off. Then
three worse ones after it, because he had got excited and was pushing.

*There it is,* thought Sazare, from behind a root plate, with the leaf
mould in his collar and a piece of hornbeam under his elbow. *That's the
whole of him. He's the weakest of the three by a distance, he's got no
finesse at all and he's burning four times the chakra to get half the
height, and he cannot stop, and it isn't courage, it's that he has no
other idea. That's a boy who's been last at everything for twelve years
and has worked out that the only thing he's got is not stopping.*

*He'll be dead inside two years.*

He believed that. He would have written it in the ledger. It was the
considered professional assessment of a person who had been alive on the
road for two years and had a great deal of practice at reading what people
were, and it was arrived at honestly, from the evidence, by somebody
paying close attention.

---

The girl was sitting at the bottom of the third tree with her back against
it and her knees up, and a satchel open beside her, and she was, as far
as Sazare could tell, doing absolutely nothing.

She had a canteen. She had a folded cloth. She was watching the two boys
with an expression that he read, at sixty yards, as boredom mixed with
something patient, and every so often she said something to one of them
that made the orange one shout back and the dark one ignore her.

He put her down as the one they had left to mind the water.

He was still doing it — still actually in the middle of the thought — when
the orange one came off the tree for the twelfth time, landed badly, sat
up, and yelled across the clearing:

"Sakura-chan! Oi — *Sakura-chan!* How'd you do the bit where it goes
round?"

And the girl said something Sazare did not catch, and got up, and walked
over to the third tree, and put one foot on the trunk at knee height, and
walked up it.

---

She did not run.

That was the thing. That was the entire thing, and Sazare's hands closed
in the leaf mould so hard that he tore up a double handful of it without
knowing.

She *walked*. Vertically, up a pine trunk, at a stroll, with her arms
loose at her sides and her red dress hanging the wrong way and her hair
falling straight out from her head toward the ground, for about fifteen
feet — and then she stopped, standing at ninety degrees to the world with
one hand on the bark, and turned her head down and said something to the
boy in orange about his feet, and pointed at her own with her free hand.

And stood there. Talking. Not holding on.

She was there for perhaps forty seconds. She was not concentrating; he
could see her face and she was not concentrating, she was *explaining*,
she was doing the thing people do with their eyebrows when they are trying
to make something clear to somebody who is not getting it. At one point
she took her hand off the bark entirely to make a shape in the air with
both hands.

Then she came down — walked down, forwards, which is worse than up — and
stepped off onto the ground and sat back down against the tree and picked
up the canteen.

The orange one went back to the trunk and hit it and came off.

---

Sazare lay behind the root plate in the leaf mould for a long time after
that with his heart going and his face hot.

He did the honest accounting, because he always did, and it went like
this:

She was twelve. He was sixteen.

He had been the best in his year at still water and had been told at ten
and a half never to let anybody find out how good; had held it seven
minutes in front of an examiner; three hours in a drainage cutting under a
road with prisoners going by above him; eleven hours in six inches of
freezing run-off with a professional standing four yards away.

Control was the one thing. It was the entire and only thing. Everything he
was — the four seconds, the grown fixtures, the smoothing his mother's
frames had needed copper for and his hands did not — sat on top of it.

And a twelve-year-old girl had just stood on the side of a tree for forty
seconds, casually, to explain something, and had thought so little of it
that she had taken her hand off the bark to gesture.

She was not better than him. He made himself say that properly and he
believed it: she had done one thing better than he could do it, and he
would have bet a great deal that she could not have held still water for
seven minutes, let alone eleven hours in a drain.

*She was taught.*

That was all. That was the whole of the difference and it went through him
like cold water. Somebody had stood in a clearing and said *here is what
this is called, and here is how much you put out, and here is what it
feels like when it is right,* and she had gone away and done it, and it
had taken her — he would find out later, in a town, from a boy showing
off — one afternoon.

One afternoon. And a name for the thing. And a man with a crutch standing
under the tree who knew what the answer was.

Sazare Ruri had spent eight months alone on a bench above a river with a
hammer and a spoon and thirty-four worthless offcuts, working, correctly
and rigorously and entirely alone, on a question that a woman with a bad
temper had told him one living person on earth could have answered in a
sentence.

---

He crawled out backward down the drainage cut and went down through the
oaks with his piece of hornbeam and did not look back once.

He got to the loft at about the fourth hour and put the hornbeam on the
bench and did not touch it.

At the eleventh hour that night, in the dark, with the tide out and the
mud giving up its cold underneath the floor, he stood up and went over to
the wall by the window and put his right foot flat against it at about
knee height and tried to put his weight on it.

He had no idea what he was doing. That was the point and he was entirely
clear about it: he had no name for it, no instruction, no notion of how
much to put out or through what, and he was reasoning from forty seconds
of watching somebody else's feet at sixty yards through a bramble screen.

He tried it eleven times.

Twice he got his weight up and something took, briefly — a fraction of a
second, no more, a sort of catching, like a hand closing on a rope in the
dark — and both times it went as fast as it came and he came down on the
boards with his bad knee taking it.

The eleventh time he cracked the plaster, which he mended in the morning
before O-Kiyo saw it, properly, with a scrim of net-twine and a filler he
made up out of lime and ash, so well that nobody in that building ever
knew there had been a hole.

Then he sat on the floor with his back against the wall in the dark with
his hands hanging between his knees, and thought, in the flat voice, with
no self-pity in it anywhere, which somehow made it very much worse:

*There is a way to do that. There is a name for it. Somewhere there is a
room where a man stands at the front and tells eleven-year-olds what it is
called, and it takes an afternoon, and I am going to be dead before
anybody ever tells me.*
