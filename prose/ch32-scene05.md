# Chapter 32, Scene 5 — Bed Eleven, Empty

*Act 4, part C. Konoha General. Sazare Ruri, age seventeen. POV:
Sazare. ~1,696 words.*

---

Handa Rokusuke went home on a Wednesday in the second month, after
eight months, two setbacks and one infection that had cost him five
weeks and very nearly a good deal more.

Sazare had known the date for eleven days and had spent nine of them
making him a thing.

It was a bench hook, which is an object of no interest whatsoever to
anybody who does not work wood, and which is a flat board with a stop
at each end that you hook over the edge of a bench so that a piece
being sawn has something to push against. Every joiner owns one. Most
of them make their own in about twenty minutes out of scrap.

Sazare had spent nine evenings on it.

He had made it out of the remains of the four foot of larch he had
carried out from under a veranda in the Land of Waves and had been
using, four inches at a time, for two years — plane bodies, a mallet
handle, the shim under Ōtaki Sue's window. There was about eleven
inches of it left when he started and he had used nearly all of it.

He had made it low: two and a quarter inches, rather than the usual
three and a half, because a man who could no longer stand at a bench
for more than about forty minutes was going to be working sitting down,
and a seated man's forearm comes in at a different height and the
standard hook fights him all day without him ever quite identifying
why.

He had put the stop on the left rather than the right, because Handa
had told him in the fourth month, in the middle of an argument about a
window, that he sawed left-handed and always had and that his master
had beaten him for it until he was fifteen and had then given up.

And he had put a strip of crystal along the working edge of the stop.
Four inches of it, a sixteenth proud, seeded and slow-grown over three
nights and polished flat, because larch is soft and a bench hook stop
gets sawn into a hundred times a week and would be chewed through in a
year, and this one would not be chewed through in thirty.

It was the best object he had made in his life and he had told nobody
he was making it.

---

He gave it to him at the tenth hour, with the discharge papers signed
and Handa's wife downstairs arguing with the porter about a handcart,
and it went wrong immediately.

Handa unwrapped it, sitting on the edge of bed eleven in his own
clothes for the first time in eight months — a jacket that had been
made for a man three stone heavier, which he had been, in the seventh
month of the previous year, on a roof.

He looked at it for a long time.

He turned it over. He found the height. He found the stop on the left.
He ran a thumbnail along the crystal strip and understood what it was
for in about two seconds, which no other person in that hospital would
have done.

And then he put it down on the blanket and said:

"No."

"It's—"

"I know what it is. It's the best bench hook in the Land of Fire and
it's got four inches of you in it and there's not a joiner alive who'd
be able to buy one." Handa's hands were still on it. "And I'm not
having it. Not like this."

Sazare stood at the end of the bed and had absolutely no idea what had
just happened.

"You don't want it."

"I want it more than anything I've been given in twenty years, and if
you put it in that cart downstairs I'll take it home and I'll use it
every day and I will be angry about it for the rest of my life." Handa
looked up. "Because I've had eight months of you, boy. Eight months.
Twice a day. And in the whole of that time you have talked to me about
*wood*."

"You started every one of those conversations."

"I did. I started every single one and I'd start them again." His jaw
set. "And I'm going out that door in about ten minutes and I'm never
going to be in this room again, and you have made me a *thing*, because
a thing can be measured and a thing can be handed over and a thing
means you don't have to stand there and say it."

"Say what?"

"You know what."

"I don't."

"You do." Handa's voice did not rise, which made it considerably worse.
"You know exactly what, because you've known for about a fortnight,
which is why you've been in this ward at hours you're not rostered for
and why you've mended that window three more times than it needed. And
you have spent nine evenings building me a beautiful object so that you
could put it in my hands instead."

The ward went on outside the screen. Somebody's radio. The trolley,
still clicking.

"That's not fair," said Sazare.

"No. It isn't." Handa put both hands flat on the blanket either side of
the bench hook. "I've got a daughter in the Land of Rivers who writes
twice a year and I've got a wife downstairs shouting at a porter and
I've got about four friends left out of the fourteen I had at thirty.
And I've had a boy come and stand at the end of my bed twice a day for
eight months and take my pulse and argue with me about a sill, and I
am not walking out of here without knowing what that was."

---

Sazare stood there.

He had the sentence. He had had the sentence for about eleven days,
assembled and disassembled and put back together in four different
orders, and every version of it had been true and none of them had been
sayable, and standing at the foot of bed eleven with a discharge form
on the trolley he understood — with the total, unwelcome clarity that
arrived whenever a thing finally made sense — exactly why.

*Because if I say it, he'll know it, and he'll take it away with him in
a cart, and I'll have given a piece of what I want to somebody in a
handcart going down the Nakadōri and I will never be able to get it
back.*

*That's all it is. That's the whole of it. It isn't complicated at
all.*

He said the only thing he had.

"There have been four people in my life I could talk to without
deciding what to say first."

Handa did not move.

"My mother," said Sazare. "Ine, who I knew for eleven days in a
rope-walk in Kasado, and who's dead. A man called Tōan who taught me
what a tourniquet was and is probably dead." He stopped. He made
himself keep going. "And you."

"And me."

"Eight months. Twice a day." His voice did something he had not
authorised. "I have not had to work out what to say to you once. Not
once. Not in eight months. And I have had to work out what to say to
every single other person I have ever met in my life, including the
ones I loved, including — " and he stopped there, because that road did
not go anywhere he could walk that morning.

"And you thought a bench hook covered that," said Handa.

"I thought it was the best I had."

"Aye. Well." Handa picked the thing up off the blanket. He turned it
over once more, and put it in his lap, and held onto it with both
hands, and did not look up for a moment. "It nearly was."

---

They got him downstairs in a chair, because the stairs were the one
thing he still could not do, and Sazare pushed it because he was
rostered and because nobody had suggested otherwise.

At the doors, Handa said, without turning round: "The window."

"It's fine. Waxed linen. It'll want doing again in the autumn."

"It'll want doing again in the autumn and you'll do it and there'll be
nobody in that bed who cares." He held up the bench hook. "Come and see
me. There's a shed. Bring something that needs fixing so you've got
something to do with your hands, because you'll be no use to anybody
sat in a chair with a cup of tea and nothing to hold."

"That's—"

"That's the arrangement. Take it or don't."

"I'll take it."

His wife had won the argument about the handcart. They went off down
the ramp toward the Nakadōri, the two of them and a porter and a cart
with eight months of a man's belongings in it, and Handa did not look
back, which Sazare understood, three rungs up a ladder he had not had
in the autumn, to be a kindness and not the other thing.

---

Bed eleven was stripped and remade by the fourth hour.

It went to a woman of sixty with a fractured femur who came up from
admissions at half past four, and who was frightened and in pain and
had nobody with her, and who asked Sazare, while he was writing her
obs, whether the window opened.

He looked at it.

"It sticks," he said. "The sill falls outward about a degree and a
half and the frame swelled in the rain three winters ago and somebody
planed the wrong rail. I'll have it open for you in about four
minutes."

"You know a lot about a window."

Sazare put the board down.

"A carpenter taught me," he said. "He was in that bed for eight months
and he went home this morning and he was the best joiner I ever met."

It was, he noted afterwards, the fifth attempt.

It was also the first one he had made about a person who was not in the
room to hear it, which he suspected did not count, and he wrote it in
the ledger anyway.
