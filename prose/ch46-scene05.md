# Chapter 46, Scene 5 — Three Voices

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, Handa's shed and the west gate. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,633 words.*

---

He took it to Handa on the twenty-fourth because Handa's shed was the
only room he knew where a thing could be looked at without becoming a
report.

The shed had not changed in two years except in the direction all such
places change. Shavings to the ankle in the corner where he had stopped
sweeping. The bench under the window with the vice at the left-hand end
and the eleven chisels in the rack in the order Handa liked and nobody
else understood. The stick hooked over the end of the bench where it
had lived since the winter, and the smell of the place, which was pine
and linseed and the particular sweetish rot of a bag of offcuts kept
four years too long against a project that had not happened.

Handa was seventy-one now and did the dovetails sitting down.

"Right," he said, without looking up. "You've got a face on."

"I've got a thing to show you."

"Is it a rock."

"It's a rock."

"It's always a rock." Handa put the chisel down. "Go on then."

---

Sazare put the rod on the bench and his palm over the seat.

The note came up.

Handa went absolutely still, which Sazare had seen him do exactly twice
in two years and both times over timber.

He did not look at it. That was the first thing. Every single person
Sazare had shown any article to in eleven years had looked at it, and
Handa put his head slightly on one side and looked at the wall.

"Do that again," he said.

Sazare took his hand off and put it back.

"Again."

He did it again.

"Right." Handa reached out — slowly, the way he did everything now —
and put his own broad flat hand on the bench top about a foot from the
rod, and spread the fingers. "Now."

Sazare fed it.

"It's in the bench," said Handa.

"What?"

"It's coming through. Not much of it and it's not much of a bench, but
it's in there." He took his hand off and put it back. "That's beech,
that top, and beech is a liar — carries anything, tells you nothing.
Put it on the oak."

They put it on the oak offcut in the corner and did it again, and then
on a length of pine, and Handa had his hand flat on each of them and
said *mm* twice and *there* once, and at the end of about twenty
minutes said:

"It's a note out of a thing under load, and it's a good one, and I'll
tell you how I know it's a good one — it's the same all the way along.
Bad ones wander."

---

"How many can you hear," said Sazare.

"How many what."

"Voices. Separate pitches, in it, at once."

Handa listened again for a while with his head on one side.

"Three," he said.

"There are four."

"Are there."

"There are four," said Sazare. "Two of them are close together and they
beat against each other about twice a second. The fourth is the highest
and it's the smallest branch, and it's the one that came in last."

Handa sat back.

He did not say anything for a moment and then he said, "Which end's the
high one," and Sazare said, "The top, above the other three, thin," and
Handa nodded slowly, the way a man does when a thing he has known for
years arrives with a date on it.

"Then I've not got it," he said. "That's about right. I lost the top of
it somewhere in my fifties, which is when everybody loses it, and you
find out because you stop being able to hear a saw going off true. It
sings different when it's about to wander and I used to hear it before
I could see it, and one day I couldn't, and I put in for the yard job
about eighteen months after that."

"You never said that was why."

"I never said it to anybody, and I'm saying it now because you're
standing there having just told me there's a fourth one." Handa picked
the chisel back up. "Don't go peculiar about it. It's ears. Everybody's
go. Mine went early because I spent thirty years next to a rip saw and
I'd do it again."

Sazare stood with his hand on a rod that was making a sound only one of
them could fully hear.

"I'll write it down," he said. "Which voice you can't get. It's data
about the article and it's the first calibration I've had off anybody
but me."

"Aye, do that." Handa turned the board round. "And Ruri — I'd not go
showing that to many people."

"Because of what it is?"

"Because of what it looks like," said Handa. "You've been standing in
my shed for half an hour holding a bit of stone that hums, and I've
been thinking about the bench, and that's because I'm a joiner and
seventy-one and past caring. Anybody else is going to see a lad with a
glowing sword."

"It isn't a sword. It's an instrument."

"I know it is." Handa marked a line. "I'm telling you what it looks
like. There's a difference and the difference has never once gone your
way in the whole time I've known you."

---

Ashida told him about it four days later, at the gate, without knowing.

Sazare had gone down with the month's returns because Ashida had asked
him to bring them personally, which meant Ashida wanted twenty minutes,
which had been true on the last Friday of the month for about two
years.

The gate detail's brazier was out for the summer and the shelter smelled
of hot creosote and the tea Ashida made in a way that four separate
people had complained about in writing.

"You'll like this one," said Ashida, sorting. "There's a light in the
quartermaster's yard."

Sazare did not move.

"Second watch have had it three times. West wall, one in the morning,
two in the morning, one of them said four. Blue-white, up over the
awning, on and off." Ashida found the sheet and turned it round. "Now,
Nemura's an idiot and he's had a ghost in that yard before, but Ōta saw
it on the eighteenth and Ōta has never seen anything in eleven years,
so it's gone up as an observation rather than a laugh."

"What did they log it as."

"*Unexplained illumination, quartermaster's yard, recurring.*" Ashida
tapped it. "Which is what you write when you've no idea. It'll sit
there for a month and then somebody'll ask Kanzaki and Kanzaki'll say
something withering and it'll go away."

He poured the tea.

"Mind you," he said, "there's a version going round the second watch
already that it's a technique being worked at night, and there's a
better version going round the laundry that it's the Fifth doing
something she doesn't want anybody to see, and I heard a third one on
Tuesday that I'm not going to repeat because it's daft even for this
village."

"Go on."

"That there's a sword in it," said Ashida, cheerfully. "There's always
a sword in it. Give a village a light at one in the morning and inside
three weeks somebody's got a sword."

Sazare held the cup.

He had it constructed inside about two seconds — *it's me, it's an
article I'm making, it's at the far bench, it will be finished in the
tenth month and it's an instrument for measuring conduction* — and it
was true and it was four sentences and Ashida would have laughed and
written *IDENTIFIED* across the sheet in his terrible handwriting and
the whole thing would have been over.

He did not say it.

He worked out why on the walk back up the hill and did not enjoy any of
the four reasons.

*One: it is not finished and I do not know whether it will work, and a
thing announced before it works is a thing you have to explain the
failure of.*

*Two: Ibiki. There is an unexplained light appearing at night in a
village where I am in a controlled correspondence with a man who reads
my post, and that is an operational fact and it is not my judgement to
make, and I have known that since Ashida turned the sheet round.*

*Three: Handa is right about what it looks like.*

*Four, and this is the one, and I am writing it down because the other
three are all defensible and this is the one that actually kept my
mouth shut in a gate shelter.*

*They have already got a sword in it.*

*Four days. One log entry, three watchmen, a laundry, and a man at a
gate who thinks it is funny — and there is already a story with a sword
in it and the story is better than the article and it did not cost
anybody eleven hundred hours.*

*If I say "it is an instrument for measuring conduction" the story does
not stop. It goes on without me and gets better, and I will spend the
rest of my life correcting it, and I am the worst-placed man in this
village to correct anything, because I correct things and it is my
single most expensive habit.*

*So it is going to be a sword.*

*I have not built one. I have built a rod that sings, and I am going to
put a blade on the end of it four hundred hours from now because a
blade is a solved geometry and I needed a long thin object under load.*

*And it is going to be a sword anyway, because forty people have
already decided, and not one of them has seen it.*
