# Chapter 54, Scene 7 — Counting

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the Hokage's office. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen.
POV: Sazare. ~1740 words.*

---

He had no procedure for it.

That was the first thing and it was almost the whole of it. He stood
about nine feet behind her and went through everything he owned, at
speed, the way he went through a rack when a thing was failing on the
bench, and there was nothing on any of the shelves.

*Ward: three protocols, all of them for a patient, all of them start
with getting somebody else.*

*Book five, chapter three, seven pages on distress in a colleague, and
every single line of it assumes you are permitted to leave the room.*

*Katsuyu would know.*

*Katsuyu is four hundred miles away and it would take eleven seconds
and it would be the single worst thing I have ever done to anybody.*

He was aware, standing there, that he had about four seconds before
doing nothing became the decision, and that he had been in that
position twice before and had got it right once.

He did not decide anything.

He crossed the room.

---

He was bad at it.

He knew that at the time, which did not help, and he had known it since
he was about five: that there was a thing other people did with their
arms and that he had watched it from the outside for eleven years the
way he watched a technique he had no seals for. He had done it exactly
twice. The first was in the ward in the ninth month and the second was
in a shed in the seventh, and both of them had been wrong in the same
way, which was that he had gone in with a plan and executed the plan.

He did not have a plan.

He came up behind her and put his arms round her shoulders and his
forehead against the back of her head, and he got it slightly wrong —
too high, and the left one across her collarbone rather than under it,
and he felt it be wrong and could not correct it without letting go and
starting again, which was not available.

She went rigid.

Every part of her, at once, the way a thing goes when it is bracing to
throw something off, and he had about a second where he knew with
complete clarity that she was going to put him through the window and
that it would be entirely fair.

She did not.

She stayed rigid, and then she was not rigid, and there was no moment
in between that he could find afterwards.

---

He had nothing to say.

Everything he had was a sentence and every sentence was a version of
*it will be all right*, and he had spent four years in a building where
that sentence was the thing you said to somebody's mother in a corridor
at two in the morning when it was not going to be all right, and he
would not say it.

So he said nothing, and stood there, and after about twenty seconds he
found that he had started counting.

He did not choose to.

It was the oldest thing he had. Two fingers on a wrist at fourteen and
a hand flat on a sternum at fifteen and eleven thousand of them since,
in wards and corridors and on a bank beside a river with the water
going past, and it was the one thing his hands did without being told
to.

*Respirations. Twenty-six.*

*High. Shallow at the top and catching on the third and the sixth.
Some of that is the drink and most of it isn't.*

*Twenty-four.*

He kept counting.

There was nothing else in him that was any use and this was the only
instrument he had ever been given, and so he stood behind the Hokage of
Konohagakure at a quarter past midnight with his arms wrong and counted
her breathing, because it was honest, and because it was steady, and
because it was the only way he had ever learned to tell a person he was
paying attention.

*Twenty-two.*

*Nineteen.*

---

She noticed.

He felt her notice — a small change in the shoulders, and then her
head came back very slightly against his, and he thought for a moment
that she was going to say something and he braced for it.

"You're counting," said Tsunade.

Her voice had gone.

"Yes."

"Of course you are."

"I'll stop."

"Don't."

He kept counting.

*Seventeen.*

*Sixteen. Depth up. That is the thing coming down, not the thing going
away.*

"What is it," she said.

"Sixteen."

"What was it when you started?"

"Twenty-six."

She made a sound that was not quite a laugh and had a great deal wrong
with it.

"That's the worst bedside manner in the history of medicine," she said.

"I know."

"Don't stop."

---

She turned round.

He had not expected that and he got his arms out of the way badly, and
she came round inside them and put her forehead on his shoulder, and
then she cried properly, which she had not been doing at the window.

He held on.

That was all he did and it was the only correct thing available and for
once in his life he did not improve it. He put one hand flat between
her shoulder blades because that was where his hand went in a ward and
he could not think of a reason to put it anywhere else, and he stood
still, and he let her.

It went on for a long time.

He did not count that. He was aware afterwards that he had not counted
it and that it was the only sustained period of his adult life that he
could not put a figure to, and he thought about that for a long while
and never got anywhere with it.

At some point she said something into his shoulder. He got about half
of it. There was a name in it that was not his and he did not ask, then
or ever.

At some point after that she stopped.

---

She did not step back.

That was the part he had no model for. He had assumed — he had assumed
without noticing he was assuming — that there would be a clean end to
it, that she would stand up and straighten and put the room back the
way it had been and say something short and unpleasant to reset the
level, because that was what she did.

She did not do it.

She stayed where she was with her forehead on his shoulder and her
breathing coming down, and it kept coming down, past sixteen, past
fourteen, and he stood and let it and did not say anything and did not
move, and about four minutes later it went under twelve and evened out
at the bottom and the catch on the sixth stopped happening.

He knew that pattern very well.

*She's gone.*

He stood absolutely still.

*Twelve, even, no catch. She has been awake since the fourth hour. She
has had — I would put it at nine measures and it was not measures.*

*She is asleep standing in the middle of her own office holding on to
me and she has been asleep for about forty seconds.*

*If I say her name she will wake up.*

*And she will have to be a person about it, in this room, at midnight,
in front of the only witness.*

He did not say her name.

---

He stood there for a further two or three minutes doing arithmetic,
because it was the only way he had of not thinking about the fact that
he was doing it.

*The chair is nine feet. The couch is by the door and there's a coat
and two files on it and it is four feet six and she is five feet five.*

*She cannot stay here. Shizune comes up at the fifth hour, and Shizune
would be kind about it, and the guard changes at the fourth, and the
clerk who opens the records floor has a key to this door and has had it
since the eighth month.*

*There's the room on the second floor.*

He had that for about a second and a half and then took it off the
table, and taking it off the table told him something he had not
previously had in words.

*She sleeps in this building. Four nights in five, at a guess, and I've
never seen the room and I know it exists because the shirt on the
fifteenth of the eighth month had no creases across the back of it at
five past eleven at night and a shirt that has been in a chair since
the fourth hour has creases across the back of it.*

*So: forty feet from the records floor, thirty from the stair, and the
first four people through that building in the morning all know exactly
which door it is.*

*Which is the whole problem. The tower is where she is findable. That's
what a tower is for.*

*There is precisely one building in this village that nobody has any
business opening a door in, and it is the one she doesn't use.*

*Somebody is going to come through that door and the thing they find is
going to be a fact for the rest of her life and none of the versions
are true.*

He looked at the door.

He had been aware, for something over an hour, of the man on the stair.
There was always a man on the stair; there had been a man on the stair
four hundred times; he was one of eight and the eight rotated and the
one tonight had a way of shifting his weight about every ninety
seconds, and Sazare had been hearing it under the conversation all
night the way he heard the lamp.

He listened.

The lamp buzzed.

He listened for a good while longer, and heard nothing at all, and
worked out that he had not heard anything for some time — that the
shifting had stopped somewhere around the middle of the six, and had
not started again.

*He's gone home.*

*Nobody goes home.*

He stood in the dark with the Hokage asleep against his shoulder and
had the first entirely cold thought of the evening.

*Somebody has made a decision about this.*

*And it wasn't me, and it wasn't her, and we were both in the room.*
