# Chapter 6, Scene 1 — Steady Work

*Act 1, beat 14. Sazare Ruri, age eleven. POV: Sazare. ~2,900 words.*

---

His father came back from the guild on the first of the eleventh month at about
the second hour of the afternoon, which was four hours early, with both boxes
still full.

He came into the yard and put them down on the step and stood there.

Sazare had been doing the small lattices. He got up.

"Dad?"

"Where's your mother?"

"Frames."

Ren went past him into the workshop, and Sazare heard him say something, and heard
the humming stop — all nine at once, which meant she had taken her hands off in
the middle, which she did not do — and then there was a long quiet, and then his
mother said, "*What?*"

---

The guild had been taken into the barracks establishment.

That was the phrase. Ren said it four or five times over the next hour and never
managed to say it as though it meant anything, because it did not sound as though
it meant anything; it sounded like a line off a notice, which is exactly what it
was.

What it meant, when he had got it out in order, was this. Inscription was a
strategic trade. The guild had eleven qualified hands and an average age of
fifty-eight and no apprentices coming, which everybody had known for years and
nobody had done anything about. So the guild was now a department. The hall on the
hill was to be a store. The eleven hands, and their brushes, and their books, went
inside the barracks compound, where they would work regular hours for a wage and a
pension.

And raw crystal for inscription would no longer go up the hill on the first of the
month.

It would go to the quartermaster's yard, into the hands of a clerk, on a docket,
and it would come back finished, on a docket, some weeks later.

"That's the same," said Iwao. "That's the same thing with more paper."

"It's not the same."

"It's the *same*. It goes there instead of up the hill—"

"It's not the same, Iwao, because Genzō isn't at the other end of it," said Ren.

---

He had seen Genzō. That was the part Sazare could not get out of his head
afterwards.

Ren had gone up the hill with the boxes as usual and found the front room being
cleared out, and two men carrying the long table into the yard, and Genzō standing
in the middle of it in his coat with his spectacles pushed up into his hair.

And Genzō had been *pleased*.

"He shook my hand," said Ren. "He shook my hand and he said—" He stopped, and
started again. "He said it's steady work. He said, I'm sixty-one, Ren, and I've
been doing my own books since I was thirty and I'm no good at it, and now there's
a wage and a pension and somebody else does the books. He said his knees are bad
and the hill was killing him."

"Well," said Iwao. "That's — I mean, if he's happy—"

"He *is* happy. That's what I'm telling you. He was pleased as anything." Ren put
his hands over his face and took them away again. "He said, come up and see me,
and then he said, no, you can't, can you, and he laughed. He *laughed*, Iwao. He
thought it was funny."

Nobody said anything.

"They didn't take him," Ren said. "That's what I keep — nobody took him anywhere.
He walked in. He's delighted. He's got a wage."

---

The argument started that evening and ran for about four hours and did not resolve
anything, and Sazare, who was eleven and was supposed to be in bed, sat on the
step inside the door and heard all of it.

Iwao wanted to close.

He said it well. He had had two years to get it right and he had got it right, and
he laid it out in a flat, tired voice that was much worse than shouting: that they
should stop selling to the yard altogether. That they should live off the jewellers
— private sales, no dockets, no lists — and take the loss, and take it for as long
as it took. That eight people could live small. That the family had a house and a
wall and a gate and a hundred and ten thousand ryō put by, and that the moment you stopped
being on somebody's paper you stopped being on somebody's paper, and there was no
other way out of a list except to get off it.

"And what do we eat," said Ren.

"Less."

"For how long?"

"Until it stops."

"And if it doesn't stop?"

"Then we'll have been eating less," said Iwao, "and we'll still be here."

Ren wanted to stay useful.

He said that too, and he said it worse, because he was arguing for something he
had begun to be ashamed of. He said that the moment the Ruri stopped supplying
the yard, somebody in the yard would notice, and that a household that had
supplied the barracks for sixty years and abruptly stopped was a household that
had done something. He said that being needed was the only shelter anyone in this
country had ever had. He said that Iwao was proposing to walk away from the one
thing standing between this family and the door, on a *feeling*.

"It's not a feeling."

"It's exactly a feeling, Iwao—"

"They have got Genzō," said Iwao, "in a room, in the barracks, on a *wage*, and he
is *pleased*, and if you cannot see what that is then I don't know what to tell
you."

---

Kohaku came in at about the third hour of the argument.

She had been on the coast run and she came in wet through, and she stood in the
doorway and listened to about ninety seconds of it, and then she said:

"Get out."

Everybody turned round.

"Not the house," she said. "The country."

There was a silence.

"Don't be stupid," said Iwao.

"I'm not being stupid, I'm being early. There's a difference and it's about nine
months wide." She came in and put her bag down. "Nagihama first, then over to the
Land of Hot Water. There's a road. It's not easy but there is one and it's open
and it will not be open in a year."

"On what money?"

"On the eleven hundred, and on selling the frames, and it'll be enough because it
has to be."

"Sell the *frames*—"

"They're *wood*, uncle."

"They are four hundred years—"

"They are *wood*," said Kohaku, "and they are worth about nine thousand ryō and a great
deal of sentiment, and Nao Amagai's father had a workshop too."

---

Sazare's mother had not said anything for the whole of it.

She was sitting at the end of the table with her hands flat on it, and she had been
sitting like that since the second hour, and when she finally spoke everybody
stopped, because she did not raise her voice and did not have to.

"How do you know the road's open?"

Kohaku said, "Everyone—"

"Don't." Hisui did not move. "Don't say everyone knows. You said everyone knows
about the Momoi and your uncle didn't know and I didn't know. You said everyone
knows about the Chiwatari before there was anybody in this district who knew about
the Chiwatari. So don't tell me everyone knows, Kohaku. Tell me how you know the
road's open."

The frames hummed through the wall.

"I've been on it," said Kohaku.

"When?"

"Two months ago."

"On what business?"

"Escort."

"Whose?"

"I can't tell you that."

"No," said Hisui. "I don't imagine you can."

And that was the moment. Sazare, sitting on the step in the dark with his arms
round his knees, could not have said what had happened, only that something had —
that a door somewhere in the house had been quietly shut and locked, and that his
mother had shut it, and that she had done it to protect somebody and he did not
know who.

"We're not going anywhere," said Hisui.

"Mum—"

"I have nine frames and a trade and a house my grandmother was born in, and if I
walk away from all of it in the night with a bag, then everything anybody has ever
said about us is *true*." Her voice cracked on the last of it, once, and she took
it back. "People who run had something to run from. That's what they'll say. That's
what they'll write down. And there are three families on this street who have done
nothing at all except stay, and if we go, every one of them is worse off by
morning."

"That is not your problem."

"It is *precisely* my problem," said Hisui. "That's the whole of what a
neighbourhood is."

---

It ended the way those things end, which is that it stopped rather than finished.

Iwao went to bed at about the fifth hour saying he had said his piece. Ren sat up
another hour with the ledgers open in front of him not looking at them. Hisui went
into the workshop and started a frame at midnight, which was madness, and did four
hours on it, and it came up perfect.

Sazare went to bed and did not sleep.

---

At some point in the small hours his door slid back about a hand's width and
Kohaku's head appeared round it.

"You awake?"

"Yes."

"Thought so." She came in and sat down on the end of his mattress with her boots
still on, which was against every rule in the house. She smelled of the road and
of the outside cold. "You heard all that, did you."

"Some of it."

"You heard all of it."

"I heard all of it."

"Right." She rubbed her face with both hands. "Well. Don't repeat any of it. Not
to Jirō, not to anyone."

"I know."

"I know you know." She sat there. "You did Kuromizu in the rain."

"That was ages ago."

"It was six weeks ago and you did it in four hours and you didn't stop." She poked
his foot through the blanket. "That's not bad, Zare."

He said nothing, because his throat had gone stupid.

"Listen," said Kohaku. "There's one on Thursday. Usual sort. It's the rope-makers'
street, second door past the blue shutters — you know it."

"Sen's old place."

"Sen's old place. There's a man there now called Ōno, he's about forty, he's got a
bad ear so you'll have to say it twice." She got up. "Thursday. Not before. If
I'm not back it doesn't matter, you just take it."

"Where are you going?"

"Out. Same as always."

"You've only just—"

"Go to sleep, Zare."

She stopped in the doorway.

"You'll be all right, you know," she said. "You will. You're the most annoying
person I've ever met and you can sit still for a quarter of an hour and you found
six families off twelve scraps when you were seven, and I have thought about that
more than you would believe." She had her hand on the frame of the door. "Do your
seven minutes in the spring and get your note and go and be in support and get a
house like Jirō's mum's cousin. All right?"

"That's not—"

"*All right*, Zare?"

"All right," said Sazare, baffled.

"Good," said Kohaku, and shut the door.

---

In the morning she was gone, and her bag was gone, and the mattress was rolled,
and there was nothing on it or under it or anywhere in the room.

His mother stood in the doorway of the empty room for a while and then went and did
the frames.

Nobody said anything about it at breakfast. Iwao said it was a good thing, and that
she'd be back inside the fortnight, and that she always came back.

On the Thursday Sazare went to the rope-makers' street, second door past the blue
shutters, and knocked, and said it twice for the bad ear.

The man called Ōno took the package and looked at it and then looked at Sazare for
rather longer than the errand required.

"You're the brother," he said.

"Yes."

"Right," said Ōno, and shut the door.
