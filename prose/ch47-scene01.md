# Chapter 47, Scene 1 — The Violation First

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the Hokage's office. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen.
POV: Sazare. ~1,795 words.*

---

He went up at eleven the same morning on four hours of nothing, having
worked a shift on it, and Shizune took one look at him and said "Sit
down before you fall down" and went in without knocking.

He was in the office ninety seconds later.

Tsunade was at the desk with the short stack at her right hand and did
not look pleased to see anybody, which was the ordinary state of that
room at eleven in the morning.

"You've got a face on," she said. "You had a face on at two. Go."

Sazare put the notebook on the corner of the desk, closed.

"You gave me an instruction at the gate," he said. "Bring it to you
before I write it anywhere. Not after, not the tidy version."

"I did."

"I broke it inside four hours. It's in that book, dated, three in the
morning, four steps and a page of objections." He kept his hands at his
sides. "I'm telling you that first, before the content, and I'd like it
on the record that I came up with it in my coat rather than in a drawer
and that I have not made a copy."

Tsunade sat back.

"Right," she said. "Why."

"Because I can't think without a pencil."

"Explain that. Properly, not as an excuse — I'll be able to tell."

"I don't have an inside process." He had worked out how to say it at
about four and had said it to himself eleven times on the stairs. "I
don't arrive at a thing in my head and then write it down. The writing
is where it happens. A thought I haven't written isn't a thought yet,
it's a pressure, and it goes on pushing until it's on a page where I
can look at it." He stopped. "I sat in the dark for forty minutes. I
went round the same four sentences eleven times and lost the middle of
it twice and would have brought you a worse version three days later.
At about ten past three I understood I wasn't obeying you, I was just
failing to think."

"And then you lit the lamp."

"And then I lit the lamp."

---

Tsunade did not say anything for a moment.

Then she said, "That's my fault," and Sazare, who had constructed four
versions of this conversation on the stairs and not one of them
containing that sentence, said nothing at all.

"I gave you an instruction without asking whether you could carry it
out," she said. "That's the whole of it. I've been managing people for
two years and doing it badly for about eighteen months of them, and the
single most common way to make somebody lie to you is to require a
thing they physically cannot do and then not check."

"You couldn't have known."

"I could have *asked*. Nine seconds. 'Can you hold a thing like that
overnight without writing it down.'" She pulled the notebook towards
her and did not open it. "And you'd have said no, at the gate, and I'd
have said all right, write it and bring me the page in the morning
unrevised, which is what I actually wanted and is what has happened
anyway by the longer road with you not sleeping."

"So it doesn't go in the fire."

"Of course it doesn't go in the fire, don't be theatrical, it's four in
the morning arithmetic in a notebook." She flipped it open. "What
mattered was that it didn't go into a *paper* before I'd seen it, and
it hasn't, and you walked up a hill on no sleep to tell me you'd
broken a rule I set badly. Sit down. You look terrible."

He sat down.

---

She read the four steps twice and the objections once, and then went
back and read objection (c) again, and then closed the book.

"Which of these do you want first," she said. "The part where I tell
you it's good, or the part where I tell you what it is?"

"The second one."

"Wrong answer, but it's the one I'd have picked." She put both hands
flat. "It's good. Steps one and two are yours and they're measurements
and I can't fault them. Step three is a sentence I said out loud in a
yard at two in the morning without noticing I was saying it, and I have
thought about very little else since, and I want you to know that
because I'm about to be extremely hard on step four."

"Go on."

"Step four is where you stop measuring and start wanting." Tsunade
tapped the closed cover. "*Two bloodlines with no common ancestor
cannot both contain the same reservoir, therefore it is outside,
therefore shared, therefore the bloodline is only the shape.* That's
four therefores off a sample of one measured case and one thing a tired
woman said in a yard, and I want you to hear how the last one lands
before anybody else says it to you."

"*A shape is a technique.*"

"Say the next sentence."

He did not say it.

"You've got it," said Tsunade. "You've had it since three o'clock this
morning and you didn't write it down, which is the only place in that
notebook where you stopped."

"I know."

"Say it anyway. In here. Once."

Sazare looked at the corner of the desk.

"If a bloodline is a shape and a shape is a technique," he said, "then
a technique can be taught, and if it can be taught then it was never in
anybody's blood, and if it was never in anybody's blood then there is
no such thing as a kekkei genkai."

The office was very quiet.

"Right," said Tsunade. "Now. Is that true?"

"I don't know."

"Good. Hold on to that, because it is going to be the hardest thing you
ever hold." She got up and went to the window. "I can tell you what
happens to it if it leaves this room, and it isn't what you think. You
think the objection is Kiri. It isn't. It's four streets away."

"The Hyūga."

"*Everybody.* This village is eleven clans in a wall and every one of
them has standing that runs on blood, and there are three of them who
would read that sentence as an attack and one of them who'd read it as
a licence." She had her back to him. "And there is a second half you
haven't got to and I'd rather you got it from me than from a committee.
If most of them are technique — then the ones that *aren't* are a very
short list, and you have just written the first page of a document that
tells anybody who reads it exactly which families are actually worth
taking a child from."

Sazare sat with that.

"I hadn't got that," he said.

"No. You had eleven hours and no sleep." She turned round. "I've had
two months since a splint paper crossed my desk with your requisition
on it and I'd already got most of the way there, and I have been
waiting to see whether you'd arrive on your own, and you have, in the
wrong order, at speed, which is how you do everything."

---

"So what do I do."

"You do exactly what's on the last page of your own book," said
Tsunade, "which is the only sensible thing in it. One question, one
person, and the question is where does the wood come from and does
anything weigh less afterwards. No paper. No hypothesis. You are not
going to prove a general claim about four hundred families this
autumn, and if you try I will stop you, and I would rather tell you
that now than in front of a board."

"And if the answer collapses it?"

"Then you'll have spent a fortnight and learned something, and I will
be relieved, and I'll say so." She came back to the desk. "And if it
doesn't collapse it, you bring me that and we sit down with a bottle
and work out what in God's name to do with it, and I mean that as a
timetable and not as a figure of speech."

Sazare picked the notebook up.

"There's the other problem," he said.

"Which."

"I don't know a Wood Release user. I don't know whether there's one
alive. The only one I can name has been dead for fifty years and I've
been walking through his fabrication for two years reading it as
scenery."

Tsunade did not answer.

That was the whole of it. She did not say *there isn't one* and she did
not say *I don't know* and she did not say anything at all, and she
went back to the stack and moved one sheet from the left to the right,
and she did it four seconds later than she should have.

Sazare watched her do it.

He had read a coat off a man's back at thirteen and a fishmonger's
thirty years off his knuckles in four seconds and he had spent eleven
years being told he was fluent at rung one of a ladder and never
climbed past it, and he sat in a chair in the Hokage's office on no
sleep and read four seconds of a woman not answering a question.

"Don't," said Tsunade, without looking up.

"I haven't said anything."

"You've said the entire thing. You've been sitting there saying it for
about nine seconds and your face is a disgrace." She signed something.
"There are three or four things in this village I hold that are not
mine to hand out, and I hold them for reasons that are somebody else's
and a great deal older than you, and I am not going to breach one of
them because a clever boy read my hands."

"Understood."

"Do you understand, or are you agreeing?"

"I understand," said Sazare. "You can't tell me. That's not the same as
there being nothing to tell, and you've just told me that instead, and
I don't think you meant to and I'm not going to pretend I didn't get
it."

Tsunade put the pen down and looked at him for a long moment.

"Go and ask Hatake," she said.

"For what?"

"For whatever he decides to give you. It's his to give and it isn't
mine, and that is the last sentence I am going to say about it in this
room or any other." She picked the pen back up. "And Ruri — go to bed
first. You are about to ask a man for something expensive and you look
like a hospital case, and he will say no to that version of you out of
sheer professional caution."
