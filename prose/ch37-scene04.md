# Chapter 37, Scene 4 — Necklace or an Earring

*Act 4, part D. Konoha. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare.
~1,639 words.*

---

He asked it at the eleventh minute, before she had finished her first,
because he had learned over about fourteen months that if he did not
say a difficult thing inside the first quarter of an hour he would
carry it the whole evening and take it home again.

"Would you rather a necklace or an earring?"

Tsunade put her cup down.

"...Say that again."

"Would you rather a necklace or an earring." He heard it the second
time and it was worse. "It's a specification question. I've been trying
to make you something for eleven weeks and I can't design it because I
don't have the requirement, and the book says there's no disgrace in
inquiry, and I've now said the word *requirement* out loud about a
person so this is going badly."

"The *book*?"

"There's a book."

"Ruri."

"It's from the civilian library and it's forty-one years old and it's
shelved under domestic economy because the 1961 scheme thinks people go
with the cookery," said Sazare, in one breath, "and it has six chapters
and I've done four of them badly and this is chapter four and I've been
stuck on you since the eighth month."

Tsunade looked at him for a moment.

Then she put her elbows on the table and said, "Right. Start at the
beginning, and I want all of it, and I am going to enjoy this
enormously and I'd like you to know that in advance so you can't
complain later."

---

He gave her all of it, which took about twenty minutes, and she did
enjoy it enormously, and she laughed twice — once at the folder and
once, longer, at *the person who mends a thing has performed a service,
and a service creates an obligation*, which she made him read out from
memory twice and then said, "That is the single most accurate sentence
anybody has ever written about you and it was written before your
mother was born."

Then she came back to the question, which he had assumed she would let
go.

"Necklace or an earring."

"Yes."

"Why those two?"

"Because everything else is out." He had the analysis and gave it,
because it was the part he was actually confident about. "Rings are
out — your strike lands on the heel of the right hand and a rigid band
on either hand is a fracture waiting for a bad angle, and you've never
worn one, and there's a reason. Bracelets are the same problem with a
longer lever. Anything on the wrist that moves the way yours move is a
matter of time."

"Go on."

"You won't wear a second necklace." He said it carefully. "I'm not
asking about the first one. I'm saying that a person who has worn one
object every day for forty years does not add a second, and if I made
you one you would put it in a drawer and be kind about it, and I'd
rather not make a thing whose most likely outcome is you being kind
about it."

Tsunade turned her cup a quarter turn and said nothing.

"So it's ears," said Sazare. "And your ears aren't pierced."

---

"No," said Tsunade. "They're not."

"I checked four times."

"You *checked four times*."

"Across three Thursdays. It's the sort of observation that can be wrong
and being wrong about it makes everything after it nonsense." He was,
he registered somewhere behind the sentence, entirely committed now.
"There's no hole either side, and no scar, and a closed piercing leaves
a mark for thirty years and I've seen about four hundred of them."

"And what did you conclude, since you plainly concluded something?"

"That you've never owned a piece of jewellery you chose."

Tsunade's hand did not go to the necklace. He noticed that it did not,
and noticed that not-doing-it took her about a quarter of a second, and
did not say so.

"That's a considerable leap off a pair of earlobes."

"It's not a leap, it's arithmetic. Every civilian woman over thirty on
the market row has pierced ears. Kaburagi's are done. Emiko's are done.
Yagi's got one side and not the other and I intend to find out why. The
Mizukage wore a drop on the left at the delegation in a hole that was
old and elongated from weight, which means she's worn heavy earrings
for twenty years and doesn't care about the stretch." He kept going,
because the check was running and losing. "You are one of the four
best-paid people in this country. You have been offered — I'd
estimate — several hundred objects by people who wanted something. And
in fifty years you have never once wanted a thing badly enough to sit
down for four minutes and have it done."

"Four minutes."

"About that. It's a straightforward job."

Tsunade sat back.

"You'd know."

"I've done ninety-one," said Sazare. "It was standard trade work. My
mother wouldn't let me do them under fourteen so I did them all in one
year, and the whole of it is a cork, a clean needle, ice and a steady
hand, and I have the steadiest hands in that hospital and there's a
document from the medical corps saying so."

He heard himself finish the sentence.

"That was an offer," he said. "I've just made you an offer. That wasn't
— I was explaining that it's a small job and it turned into an offer
about halfway through and I'd like to withdraw the second half."

"No," said Tsunade.

"Right—"

"No, I'm not letting you withdraw it, I'm going to sit with it for a
while." She was, he saw, entirely serious under about four layers of
amusement. "The Fifth Hokage's ears, pierced in the back room of a
drinking house by an eighteen-year-old fabrication technician with a
cork and a needle. Shizune would have to be sedated."

"I'm seventeen."

"You turned eighteen five weeks ago."

Sazare stopped.

"...Did I?"

"The eleventh of last month. It's on your establishment record, which I
read four times a year." Tsunade watched him do the arithmetic and
arrive at it and looked, for a moment, genuinely sad about something.
"You didn't know."

"I knew the date. I hadn't — nobody's ever—" He stopped. "Chapter six
is about occasions and I skipped it."

"Go back and read chapter six."

---

It was while she was talking about something else entirely — a
Council thing, at length, with feeling — that he noticed the bells.

There were two of them.

Small, metal, one either side of the pendant, mounted on the chain
rather than the bail, about the size of a barley grain each, and he
had been looking at that necklace across a desk and a table for two
years and had catalogued the stone, the mount, the bezel, both
repairs, the thumb-worn patch and the gold's two ages, and had never
once registered that there were two bells on it.

He stopped listening in the middle of a sentence about a bridge.

*They don't ring.*

That was the first thing. He had sat opposite that woman for four
Thursdays and about nine formal meetings and she moved — she was not a
still person, she gestured, she leaned, she had thrown a cup across a
room in front of him in the spring — and he had never heard them.

*So they're not bells. Or they're bells that have been stopped.*

*Or they're stopped and I can't see how from four feet, which means
they were stopped by somebody good, which means somebody thought about
it, which means at some point somebody sat down with an eighty-year-old
necklace and made a decision about the noise.*

And the second thing, which arrived about a second later and which he
liked considerably less:

*I have looked at this object more closely than at anything else in my
life and I missed two components on it for two years.*

---

"You've gone somewhere," said Tsunade.

"There are bells on it."

"...What?"

"On the chain. Either side of the stone. Two of them, about a barley
grain each, and they don't make any sound at all and I've never
noticed them and I have been looking at that necklace since I was
sixteen years old."

Tsunade put a hand up to it, briefly, and then took it away.

"They're original," she said. "They've never made a noise. I don't know
why and I've never asked anybody."

"Somebody stopped them."

"Or they were made stopped."

"Nobody makes a stopped bell," said Sazare. "That's — you'd make a
bead. A bell is a thing with a specific construction and the whole
point of the construction is that it rings, and if you want a thing
that looks like a bell and doesn't ring, you cast it solid and you
save yourself four operations."

"So somebody stopped them."

"Somebody stopped them and did it well enough that I can't see how at
four feet in this light." He was already reaching for the paper napkin
and had a pencil out of his coat before he registered that he was
doing it. "Can I—"

"No, you may not take my necklace apart in a bar."

"I wasn't going to take it apart."

"You had a pencil out."

"I was going to draw it."

"You were going to draw it," Tsunade agreed, "and then you were going
to think about it for six weeks, and then in about the fourth week
you'd have asked me whether you could look at it in better light, and
we both know how that ends."

He put the pencil away.

"That's the second time tonight I've done something without deciding
to."

"It's the fourth," said Tsunade. "You've been doing it all evening. I'd
have mentioned it earlier but I was enjoying myself."
