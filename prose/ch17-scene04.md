# Chapter 17, Scene 4 — The Point

*Act 2, part D. Kasado. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,750 words.*

---

The block-pulley came over on the Friday with a boy from the chandlery
and a message that there was no hurry and would he say what it would be.

Sazare took it in both hands and turned it over twice.

It was a good one — a double, elm shell, bronze pins, thirty years old
and worth mending — and it had been binding for a month because somebody
had at some point replaced one pin with a bolt of the wrong gauge and
packed the difference with leather, and the leather had swollen, and the
sheave had been running slightly out of true ever since and had worn
itself an oval.

He mended it on the Saturday. It took him five hours. He made a new pin,
which meant borrowing the boatyard's forge for forty minutes and being
seen to do it, which was fine; he trued the sheave, which was the fiddly
part; he cut a leather washer that was actually the right thickness, and
he greased it with the yard's tallow and worked it a hundred times until
it turned with one finger and made no sound at all.

He knew, the entire time, exactly what he was doing.

That was the thing about it. There was no confusion anywhere in him and
he was not lying to himself about a single part of it. He sat in the back
of a boatyard for five hours on a Saturday in the eleventh month and did
five hours of genuinely excellent work for a woman he had been paid two
hundred ryō to kill on the Wednesday, and he did it well because he could
not do it any other way, and he charged her thirty coppers, which was
fair, and she sent back forty.

He had been here before. He knew that too. Nine weeks and a river port
ago there had been a brass lantern with a pillar a quarter-turn out, and
a man who had laughed at him about a smith, and a hinge-plate aged badly
on purpose so it would read as six years old.

He was aware, sitting on a bench with a pulley that turned with one
finger, that he had now done this twice, and that the shape of his life
had a repeat in it, and that repeats in a shape mean the shape is
finished and is only going to go round again.

He put the thought in the tray.

---

The plan was the point, and the point was the best piece of work he had
ever done in his life, and it took him one afternoon because the town had
already done all of it for him.

Everybody in Kasado knew about the current at the point. It was the
first thing anybody told you and the second thing and the third: that the
ebb came round the head of the spit and went down the outside of the
shingle in a set that would take a boat sideways, and that it ran deep
right up against the shelf, and that there was no wading out of it, and
that in eleven years it had taken two children, a man off a bicycle, and
a dog belonging to the toll clerk which had become a byword.

The shingle at the point had a shelf. It shelved gently for nine feet and
then it did not shelve at all; it went down like a step, and the step was
where the set ran, and at the bottom of the ebb on a north-easter the
step was six feet from the waterline and under about eighteen inches.

A woman of fifty-two, alone, at first light, six mornings out of seven,
walking east along the tideline with a basket, on wet shingle, on a
shelving beach, with a bad set running four feet from her boots.

It required nothing. That was the whole horror of it and he sat with that
too. He did not need a knife, or a rope, or a fixture, or one grain of
material, or any part of the only thing in the world that was actually
his. He needed to be at the point at first light and to have forty
seconds, and the sea and the shingle and the entire population of Kasado
would do the rest, and would go on doing it for years, in inns, saying
*I always said. Six mornings out of seven, at her age, on that beach. I
always said.*

He walked it on the Monday and again on the Tuesday and he did not touch
one thing.

---

She came round the toll shed at ten past six on the Wednesday with the
basket on her arm, in the grey, on the ebb, in a north-easter, and Sazare
was sitting on the shingle above the tideline forty yards along with his
back to a beached and rotted skiff, being a small shape in a coat that
nobody looks at twice.

He got up when she was eight feet away, because she was going to see him
in the next two steps and a man who stands up is a man who was sitting
down, and a man who does not stand up is a thing crouching on a beach.

"Morning," said Ine.

"Morning."

"You're up early." She had stopped. She was looking at him with the
frank, entirely untroubled interest of a woman who has met perhaps four
hundred thousand people. "That pulley turns better than it did when I
bought it."

"It'd been packed with leather. Somebody put the wrong pin in it."

"I know they did. Fourteen years ago and I'm still cross about it." She
shifted the basket to the other arm. "Are you stopping at Kasado? Only
there's work here if you want it and half the coast doesn't know it
yet—"

He went for her at the third word from the end.

---

It went wrong in the second second, which he ought by then to have
expected, because it had gone wrong in the second second every single
time.

He had it right up to then: eight feet, downhill, on the outside, off the
foot that worked, and he got both hands on her and got her turned and got
her into the water in the first three strides exactly as designed, and
the water came up round her to the knee and then to the thigh as they
went over the shelf, and it was going to work, and it did not work.

She was fifty-two years old and she had been sculling nine hundred yards
of tideway four hundred times, and she had shoulders and forearms like a
carter, and she had a hand on the gunwale of a beached skiff for the
first half-second and did not let go of it, and Sazare weighed eight and
a half stone.

She did not go down. She went *round*.

He was in the sea to the waist with a grip on a woman's coat that was
doing nothing at all, and her elbow came back into his ribs, into the two
that had never properly healed, and everything below his sternum turned
white — and then he was sitting in eighteen inches of cold water four
feet from a bad set, with the whole of him not working, and she was
standing over him.

She had a scull in her hand. She had picked it out of the beached skiff
in the time it took him to hit the water and she had it back over her
shoulder like a woman about to split wood, and she was between him and
the beach, and Sazare Ruri, who had been given four hundred ryō for being
careful, sat in the sea at first light and understood that he was about
to be killed on a shingle spit by a chandler.

She did not swing.

She stood there with the scull up and her chest going, in the grey, with
the water round her knees, and looked at him.

And Sazare watched a woman of fifty-two do, in about four seconds, the
thing he did — the assembling; he had never in his life seen anybody else
do it — and he watched every part of it arrive on her face in order: the
boy on the beach at ten past six; the boy who had been in the back of the
second boatyard for eight days; the summer; the two civil men at her
counter; the skiff off its painter at the top of the flood.

Her arms came down.

She did not drop the scull. She simply stopped holding it as a weapon and
let it rest across her forearm like a thing she was carrying, and looked
at him with an expression he was not able to identify at the time and
identified perfectly about a year later, in a different country, and it
was the expression of a person who has finally been sent a bill she has
known about for months.

"Right," said Ine.

---

"How old are you?"

He did not answer.

"You're sixteen," she said. "Seventeen at the outside. You've a Water
accent under that and you've got a leg that's been done by somebody who
knew what he was at." She was breathing hard. Her voice was completely
level. "Then they've had you off somebody too."

The sea went past them both.

"Don't," said Sazare.

"I'm not doing anything to you, son," said Ine. "I'm looking at you. It's
half past six in the morning and I'm going to be dead in about four
minutes and I'm not going to spend them being nice to you, and I'm not
going to spend them screaming either, because there's nobody within four
hundred yards and I'd rather use the time."

He got his feet under him in the water and did not stand up.

"So here's what I'm doing with it," she said. "Listen, because I'm only
going to be able to say it once, and because you're the only person
standing here."

And then Ine, chandler, of Kasado, on the third-year list out of a
village of two hundred people, gave the handover.

"There's thirty-one people in the two inns on the south quay and about a
third of them are going to be told on Friday that the price has gone up.
They'll come to my counter. There'll be nobody at my counter. Somebody
has got to tell them not to pay it twice, because they will, they always
do, they'll sell the ring and pay it twice and then they'll be on that
beach at the point with nothing at all, and if you have ever seen that
happen you'll know why I'm using my four minutes on it.

"There's a man on the far side called Bunzō. Charcoal burner. Cart, and a
reason to be on the Ōgi road at that hour, and he's been coming down
through the pines for me for a year and a half and he has never once
asked me a question. He'll come Tuesday and he'll come Friday and there
will be no boat, and he'll come the Tuesday after that as well, because
he's that sort, and the third time somebody will be watching that road.
Get word to him. Tell him to stop. Tell him from me — say *she said
stop*, he'll know it's real, I've never told him to stop anything.

"There's a jar under my counter with nine chips of quartz in it and
you'll know what they're for or you wouldn't have found me. Break them.
Don't put them out and don't let anybody else put them out, because the
board's no good now and a board that's no good gets somebody killed.

"And there's a thin ledger on the shelf behind the counter with about
four hundred names in it that aren't real, and I was always going to burn
it and I've left it too late, which is exactly what everybody does. Burn
it. Tonight. Not tomorrow."

She stopped. The tide moved.

"That's it," she said. "That's the lot."

---

Sazare stood in the sea and could not make anything at all come out of
his mouth.

"You'll do it," said Ine — not a question; a fact stated by a woman who
had been reading people across a counter for thirty years and had one
piece of reading left in her. "You'll do all of it. You mended my pulley
better than it's ever been in fourteen years and charged me thirty for
it. You'll do every word of it and it won't help you one bit and I'm not
saying it to be kind."

"Why are you saying it, then?"

"Because it's the work," said Ine, "and you're the only one here."

And she put the scull down on the shingle, carefully, out of her own
reach, which was the last decision she ever made and was made for the
same reason as all the others: because a woman standing in the water with
an oar in her hand is a woman who is going to be found with a mark on her
that a coroner would have to explain.

She stood up straight in eighteen inches of cold water at the top of the
ebb, on a shelving beach, four feet from a set that had taken two
children and a man off a bicycle, at half past six in the morning, and
she looked out at the strait she had crossed four hundred times.

"Go on, then," she said. "It's a bad set today. It won't be long."

---

It was not quick and it was not clean and there was nothing in it that
anybody could have called mercy, and he did it, and he stayed the whole
time, because the whole point of the thing was that she had to go into
the set and not be found on the beach, and going into the set meant
holding on.

It took a little under three minutes.

Afterwards he sat down on the shingle in his wet clothes in the grey and
was extremely careful about several things.

He put the scull back in the beached skiff, in the place it had been, at
the angle it had been, because he had noted the angle.

He walked the tideline back forty yards and picked up the basket, which
had gone over at the second second, and he set it down again at the point
where she would have set it down if she had put it down to look at
something, on its side, with the weed spilled out of it in the direction
the wind was pushing everything else on that beach.

He went over the shingle for four minutes on his hands and knees and
found nothing of his and had not expected to.

He got a good look at the shelf and the set and satisfied himself, with a
detachment he did not examine, that she would not come ashore at Kasado
and would very probably come ashore, if she came ashore at all, three
miles down the coast in the way the small skiff had come ashore three
miles down the coast in the summer, which was a thing everybody in the
town would remember without being prompted.

And then he walked back into Kasado along the tideline at ten to seven in
the morning, wet to the chest, which was unremarkable, because it was
half a mile of beach in a fishing town in the eleventh month and there
were four other men on it wet to the chest already.

---

That evening, in a boatyard, by lamplight, with two hundred ryō in his
coat and two hundred more coming, he wrote the fifth line in the book
that had no numbers in it.

*Fifth. Kasado. Eleventh month. Nine days. Water. Called an accident by
the whole town before I had got my coat off, and there will be no dispute
about it ever.*

He looked at it for a while.

Then he added the two words that ended every entry, in the same hand, at
the same size, in the same place, exactly as he had added them four times
before, because that was the form and the form was the whole of what he
had left:

*No material.*
