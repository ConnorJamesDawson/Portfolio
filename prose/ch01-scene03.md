# Chapter 1, Scene 3 — Cousins

*Act 1, beat 4. Sazare Ruri, age seven; Kohaku Ruri, thirteen. POV: Sazare.
~5,000 words.*

---

Kohaku came home on the fourth day, at the hour when the lamps went on, wet to
the knee and eating something she had bought on the way.

Sazare heard the gate and was across the yard before anyone else had got up. She
saw him coming and turned side-on and put her free arm out at the last moment so
that he ran into it instead of into her, which was a thing she had started doing
the year she got tall.

"You're wet," he said, into her sleeve.

"I've been in a ditch."

"Why were you in a ditch?"

"Because it was there." She got her arm round his head and steered him back
towards the door by it, which he permitted. "Have you eaten?"

"No."

"Liar. You've had something, I can smell it."

"I had *one*."

"One what?"

"One of the fried things."

"You've had three of the fried things," said Kohaku, "or you'd have said what
they were," and let go of his head, and went inside to be exclaimed over.

---

His mother came out of the workshop with her sleeves still pinned and looked her
daughter over the way she looked over a frame in the morning — one pass, top to
bottom, no expression at all — and then said, "Ditch?"

"Ditch."

"Both knees?"

"Both knees."

"There's hot water," said Hisui, and went back to the frames, and that was the
whole of it, and Sazare had understood since about the age of five that it had
been an entire conversation and that he had not been told what about.

His father was more straightforward. His father said "*there* she is," and put
another bowl out, and asked her three questions about the road in a row without
waiting for the answers, which was how Ren showed affection to everyone
including strangers.

Iwao said, "You're thin."

"I'm not thin."

"You're thin. Sit down."

Kohaku sat down.

---

She ate like the road. Sazare watched her do it with unqualified admiration — she
went through two bowls and most of the fish and the last of the pickles and a
quantity of tea that would have kept a horse standing, and she did it while
talking, and none of it seemed to cost her any effort at all.

"So there's the client," she said, "and he's got this dog."

"Ah," said Ren, settling.

"He's got this dog, and he will not go anywhere without the dog, and nobody has
mentioned the dog. It's not in the brief. There is no dog in the brief. And this
thing is the size of a *table*—"

"How big's a table."

"It's the size of *that* table."

"That's not a big table."

"It's a *dog* the size of a *table*, it doesn't have to be a big table to be an
unreasonable dog," said Kohaku, and Sazare laughed so hard he had to put his cup
down, and she carried straight on, entirely serious, describing four days of
increasingly complicated logistics arising from a dog.

It was a very good story. It had the client in it, and the client's dog, and the
client's opinions, and a ferryman with a grudge, and a stretch of road where
somebody had stolen all the mile-markers to build a wall; and by the time she had
finished, Sazare could have drawn the dog from memory.

He could not have said where any of it had happened. Nobody was named. Nothing
had a date on it. It was four days of somebody's life with every hook filed off,
told so well that the shape of what was missing never showed.

"Where was this, then?" said Ren, reaching for the tea.

"Up the coast."

"Up past the Kasumi place?"

"Bit past." Kohaku pushed her bowl across for the last of the rice. "Have you
seen the wire's up again?"

"*Don't*," said Ren, and Iwao said "*fourth time this year*," and the table went
where it always went, and the coast road did not come up again.

---

Afterwards, when the dishes had been argued over and dealt with, Sazare got the
earring out of his sleeve.

He had been carrying it around for four days. It had been in his sleeve, and then
under his pillow, and then in his sleeve again, and it had picked up a certain
amount of the general grime of a seven-year-old in the process, so that he had to
give it a rub on his shirt before he could hand it over, which spoiled a plan he
had rehearsed several times.

"I made you a thing."

Kohaku held out her palm without looking, because she was working a knot out of
her hair. Then she looked.

She turned it over twice. She held it up between finger and thumb the way Iwao
had, and the way their mother had, which he had never noticed any of them
learning from each other.

"Hollow," she said.

"I *know*."

"No — Zare." She turned it again, and tilted it. "It's hollow *here*. Look. Just
at this end. The rest of it's solid all the way through. Did you see that?"

He had not seen that.

He took it back and held it up against the lamp and there it was: the grey
emptiness stopped about a third of the way along, and past that the little crooked
lozenge went dense and clear right to the tip.

"That's new," he said.

"That's new." She took it off him again. "How long did you feed it?"

"Nine hours. Nearly."

"*Nine.*" She whistled. "I did four, when I was you. Four and then I went to
sleep and it came out like a bit of chewed bread." She turned it once more and
then, matter-of-factly, threaded it through her ear, which he had not expected at
all and which made something go tight and hot behind his face. "Right. That's
mine now."

"It's not finished. Mum said not to polish it too far but I haven't polished it at
*all*—"

"Good. It looks like you meant it." She flicked it with a fingernail and it made
a very small sound. "Now come here, I've got something for you."

---

What she had was a flat cloth roll about the length of her hand, and inside it,
in little sewn pockets, were twelve chips of crystal.

They were nothing. They were scrap — the off-cuts and failed corners that came
off a bad shelf when it got broken up, none of them bigger than a fingernail,
several of them cloudy, one of them so hollow you could see the pattern of the
cloth through it.

Every single one had marks on it.

"Genzō?" said Sazare.

"Genzō. He does them on the offcuts to keep his hand in. He was going to throw
them out." She sat down cross-legged on the floor with her back against the
cupboard and started laying them out in a row on the boards. "Sit."

He sat.

"Right," said Kohaku. "Game."

"What sort of game?"

"A good one. Shut up." She turned the twelve chips face up and pushed them
around until she was satisfied with the order, which took a while, and which
appeared to have rules. "Now. Some of these are cousins."

"Cousins?"

"Cousins. Family. Some of these marks are related to each other and some of them
aren't, and you can tell — you can *always* tell — if you know how to look." She
sat back. "Find me two cousins."

Sazare looked at the twelve chips.

They were, at first, meaningless. Twelve smears of hair-thin figures on twelve
bits of rubbish, all of them different, none of them anything.

Then, slowly, one of them stopped being meaningless.

"That one," he said, and pointed. "And that one."

"Why?"

"They've both got — the long bit. That goes round." He drew it in the air with a
finger. "It goes round and it stops before it gets back. They both do that."

Kohaku picked up the two chips and looked at them and put them back down.

"Yeah," she said. "They're cousins. Find me another pair."

He found another pair. It took him a long time and he got it wrong twice, and
each time she said "no" without any particular kindness and put the chip back
where it had been, and the third time he got it right and she said "yeah" in
exactly the same tone she had used for "no," which he found he preferred to
praise.

By the fourth pair he had stopped looking at the marks as pictures and started
looking at them as — he had no word for it. As handwriting. As the *way* somebody
had gone about it.

"There's four families," he said, after a while.

Kohaku had been picking at the frayed edge of the cloth roll. She stopped.

"Say that again."

"There's four. There's the round-and-stop one. There's the one with the little
sand-marks in the middle. There's the one that's all straight, like a fence. And
there's—" he pushed two chips together, "—there's these two, that are like the
fence but the lines don't touch. That's four. That's four families and then those
three at the end aren't anything, they're just doodles."

His sister looked at him for a moment.

"They're not doodles," she said. "They're a family too. You just haven't got
enough of them to see it."

"Oh."

"But four out of five." She scooped the chips back into the roll, pocket by
pocket, in an order. "Off twelve scraps, first go. That's not bad, Zare. That's
actually not bad at all."

"Can I keep them?"

"They're yours. That's why I brought them." She tied the roll and put it in his
hands and closed his fingers over it. "We'll do it again when I'm back. I'll get
more off him. There's about twenty families and I can't see half of them, so
don't get cocky."

"What do they *mean*, though?"

"Nothing. To us."

"But they must—"

"Zare." She got up off the floor with a grunt, because her knees were bruised.
"They mean things to Genzō. They don't mean anything to me and they're not going
to mean anything to you, and I don't care, because that's not the game. The game
is telling them apart. Anybody can learn what a thing *means*. Hardly anyone can
look at a page of scribble and go" — she snapped her fingers — "*those two, those
two are the same hand*. That's a different thing. That's a rarer thing."

"Is it useful?"

Kohaku laughed.

"No," she said. "It's completely useless. That's why it's a game."

---

She was gone again by the middle of the next morning, but before she went she
took him out with her, which she almost never did, and which he did not think to
question for a single second.

They went down through the fish market, and she bought him one of the fried
things and did not comment on it, and they came out on the far side into the
narrow streets under the east wall where the rope-makers were, and she stopped at
a corner and got something out of her coat.

It was a small package. It was wrapped in waxed paper and tied with string, and
it was about the size and weight of a pack of cards, and it did not rattle.

"Right," she said. "Job for you."

He straightened up about an inch.

"Second door past the blue shutters, on the left, up there." She pointed with her
chin. "There's a woman called Sen. She's about a hundred. Give her that and say
it's from Kohaku and she'll say thank you and you come straight back here. Don't
go in. She'll try and make you go in and give you something with anise in it. Do
you like anise?"

"No."

"Nobody likes anise. Don't go in."

"What is it?"

"It's a present."

"What *sort* of present?"

"The sort that's none of your business," said Kohaku, and put it in his hands.
"Go on."

He went.

The street went up. The blue shutters were where she had said, and so was the
second door past them, and so, when he knocked, was Sen — who was not a hundred
but was certainly ninety, and who was extremely small, and who took the package
in both hands and pressed it flat against her chest like something she had been
waiting for since morning.

"From Kohaku," said Sazare.

"From Kohaku," said Sen. "You'll be the brother."

"Yes."

"You'll come in and have something."

"I can't," he said, with what he considered to be enormous tact. "She's waiting."

"She's always waiting." Sen was already turning back into the dark of the house.
"She's the most impatient girl I ever met in my life. Wait there."

She came back with something in a twist of paper. He took it, because you did.

"Thank you."

"Tell her I said the roof's holding," said Sen, and shut the door.

---

Kohaku was where he had left her, sitting on the corner post with her boots
hanging, watching a rope-maker walk backwards down the length of the street.

"Well?"

"She said the roof's holding."

"Ha," said Kohaku, and dropped off the post. "Good. That's good." She took the
twist of paper out of his hand, opened it, looked at it, made a face, and gave it
back. "Anise."

"I said I didn't want to go in."

"You did the right thing." She cuffed him lightly round the back of the head and
started walking. "You'll do that again for me, will you? Next time I'm back?"

"Yes."

"Even if it's raining."

"Yes."

"Even if it's *raining*, and it's the other side of the cutting, and you have to
go past the tanners—"

"*Yes*," said Sazare, appalled that she could think otherwise.

"All right then." She put her hands in her pockets. The earring caught the light
when she turned her head, small and crooked and hollow at one end, and she had
not taken it out overnight, which he had checked. "Come on. Race you to the
steps."

"You always win."

"I'm going to keep winning, too," said Kohaku. "That's what's so brilliant about
it," and she was gone, and he went after her, and he did not think about the
package again for five years.
