# Chapter 46, Scene 1 — Work With No Output

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard, after hours. Sazare
Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,413 words.*

---

The core was finished in six nights and that was the last thing about
the article that behaved the way he expected.

Four inches and a quarter, laid slow, from one of the six invasion
grains, and on the sixth night he put the graver down at about twenty
past three and looked at a rod of pale crystal on a felt and understood
that he had spent six nights on the easy part.

Because the rod was not the article.

*The article is the ways in it,* he wrote, that night, in a hand that
had started to slope from tiredness. *The rod is the volume the ways
live in and I have made the volume. Six nights.*

*Now I widen them, and widening is not laying. Laying is going forward
into empty stock. Widening is going back over what is already there,
along the same run, again, and again, and each pass takes it out by
some amount I cannot express because I have no unit for it.*

*Four months of that.*

---

The sixth month came in wet and stayed wet, which broke the heat and
did nothing for anybody's temper, and the yard under the awning was the
only dry bench and there were three people who wanted it.

He worked at night.

Four hours, five nights a week, from about ten until two, at the far
bench under the sky when it was clear and under the awning when it was
not, with a lamp and a felt and a rod of pale crystal that looked
precisely the same on the fourteenth night as it had looked on the
sixth.

That was the problem and he had not costed it.

*I have never done work that does not show.*

*Everything I have made in eleven years has had an output at the end of
an evening. A setting is further on than it was. A splint plate exists
or it does not. Nine hundred hours of research produced nine hundred
entries and every one of them was a line on a page I could count.*

*This produces nothing. There is no line. The rod weighs what it
weighed. It measures what it measured. I have put fifty-six hours into
it and I have, on every measure I own, exactly what I had on the sixth
of the month.*

He sat with that on the eleventh night and did not enjoy any part of
it.

*I do not know whether it is working.*

---

He tried to measure it and could not, and the failing of that took him
four days and taught him something he did not want.

Weight: unchanged, to the limit of the brass balance, which was the
best balance in the yard and read to a hundredth of a gram and was not
remotely good enough.

Dimension: unchanged, to the limit of the calipers, likewise.

Optical: he could see nothing at all through Kanzaki's lens, which was
a disgrace, and had been called a disgrace by a woman who had gone on
using it for six weeks and had then brought her own.

He went to the sealing office on the fifteenth and bought a triple.

It cost him four months of the jewellery money and he knew inside about
nine seconds that the clerk had priced him — had seen a young man in
hospital clothes who wanted a good lens and wanted it that afternoon,
and had added something between a fifth and a quarter — and he stood
there and worked out the true figure to within a few hundred yen the
way he had worked out true figures since he was seven, and then paid
the asking price without a word.

He wrote it up that night because it was the most interesting thing
that had happened in a fortnight.

*Overpaid by approximately twenty-two per cent, knowingly, to a man who
saw me coming, and did not haggle and did not walk out.*

*I have never once done that. I have talked a man down over four
hundred yen on a chain I did not need. It is the single thing I am
best at in the world after the crystal and I did not use it.*

*Reason, and I want it accurately: I could not stand in that shop for
eleven more minutes. Not because of the money. Because the correct move
was to say "I'll think about it and come back Thursday," and Thursday
is four days, and I have fifty-six hours in a rod I cannot see into and
I could not stand to have four more days of not knowing.*

*I paid twenty-two per cent for four days.*

*Note also: there is a triple lens eleven minutes further on, in a
fitted case with a worn strap, whose owner would have lent it to me
inside four seconds if I had walked into the sealing office on a
Tuesday or a Thursday and asked.*

*I bought a lens to avoid a conversation. That is what the twenty-two
per cent was actually for and I would like it entered under the correct
heading.*

---

The lens showed him the ways and did not answer the question.

He could see them now — running the length of the rod from the seat
end outward, branching four times, the whole architecture of the thing
laid out under a good triple at last, and it was, he thought, the most
beautiful object he had ever made and he had made a great many objects.

He measured a way at the third branch on the fifteenth night and wrote
the figure down.

He measured the same way at the same branch on the twenty-second night,
after twenty-eight further hours of work, and got a figure that was
larger by an amount inside his own measurement error.

He did it again. And again on the twenty-third, four times, and
averaged them, and the average was larger and the spread was larger
than the difference.

*So: either it is working slowly, or it is not working and I am
measuring my own hope.*

*I cannot separate those with this instrument. I do not think I can
separate them with any instrument I could get in this country.*

He put the lens down and sat back in the wet dark with the lamp going
and the rain coming off the awning in a line of separate falls that did
not quite keep time with each other.

*This is the point at which a person stops.*

*I want to write that down while I am in it rather than afterwards,
because afterwards I will have either stopped or not stopped and I will
construct a reason, and the reason will be tidier than this.*

*Fifty-six hours become eighty-four. No output. No measurement that
resolves. Four months to go, on an article that will either be the
best thing I have ever made or four hundred hours of a rod, and no way
of knowing which until it is finished.*

*Every long piece of work I have ever admired — the six hands on that
scroll, three hundred years of it; a man who spent three years on one
technique and named the stages afterwards; Kanzaki keeping nine hundred
dishes for thirty-one years against a question nobody had asked yet —
every one of them had this in it and none of them wrote it down,
because it is not interesting and there is nothing to say about it.*

*It is just the part where you keep going and cannot tell anybody why.*

---

He kept going.

He wrote the hours at the top of each night's page and nothing else
about them, because there was nothing else, and by the last week of the
month the page ran:

*84. 88. 92. Nothing to report.*

*96. Nothing to report.*

*100. Nothing to report. Exercise 1: two.*

*104. Nothing to report.*

And then, on the twenty-eighth night, in the wet, at about half past
midnight, with a hundred and eight hours in it and no reason whatsoever
to expect anything, he finished a pass at the fourth branch and put his
palm flat over the seat end of the rod the way he had a hundred times,
to check the seat, which was the ordinary check.

And a small amount of him went into it, the way a small amount always
did.

And the rod made a sound.
