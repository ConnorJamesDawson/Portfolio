# Chapter 42, Scene 3 — Of the Hand and the Shoulder

*Act 4, part D. Konoha General and Handa's shed. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,676 words.*

---

He said it on the thirteenth morning, before she had got the sheet out.

"Chapter five is next and I'm not doing it with you."

Sakura stopped with her hand in the satchel.

"Chapter five is the one about touch," said Sazare. "Ordinary friendly
contact. The book says it's a language of about nine words that most
people learn before they can speak and that a person who hasn't
acquired it can't get it from a description, which I've now proved
twice by trying. It has to be practised. It can't be practised on you."

"...Right."

"I want to give you the reasoning, because I've worked out that
withholding the reasoning is how a correct decision gets read as a
strange one." He said it the way he said a fault report, which was the
only register he had for anything that mattered. "You're fourteen and
I'm eighteen. You're junior to me in that building and I have signed
two of your assessments. Those are both real and neither is the one
that decides it."

"What decides it?"

"That a person practising is a person who isn't certain. Uncertainty is
exactly what makes contact unreadable — a hand that hesitates is a hand
that could be four different things and the other person has to pick
one. And an unreadable contact from a man four years older and two
grades up isn't a neutral event no matter what's in his head, because
what's in his head isn't available to anybody. It's the same failure as
the gate barrier. It doesn't matter what I meant. It matters what it
does to whoever's standing in front of it."

Sakura let the satchel strap go.

"That's most of it," she said.

"What's the rest?"

"The rest is that I'd have had to decide what to do about it." She was
looking at the window and not at him. "That's the bit men never get to.
Even if I worked it out right in four seconds — even if I knew straight
away it was the book and not anything else — I'd still have spent the
four seconds. And then I'd have spent an evening on it. And you'd never
have known I'd paid it, because I'd have been perfectly normal the next
morning, and that's the whole point of the cost, it doesn't show up
anywhere you can see it."

Sazare got the notebook out and then, deliberately, did not open it.

"I'll write that down tonight," he said. "Not now."

"Why not now?"

"Because you just told me something that cost you something and my
first instinct was to file it, and filing it in front of you would turn
it into a finding, and it isn't one. It's yours." He put the notebook
back in his coat. "That's criterion four. I nearly did the audit."

Sakura laughed — properly, out loud, once, on a stairwell at
twenty-five to eight.

"Nine out of ten," she said.

---

He went to Handa's that evening.

The shed had the doors open, which they had not been since the ninth
month, and the whole of the yard smelled of the sawdust going warm on
the pile at the end of the day, and there was that particular fourth-
month light that gets in low under a roof and makes the air visible
along the plane of the bench. A blackbird was going through its
repertoire on the fence with the persistence of something that had
nowhere else to be. Handa had the stick hooked over the arm of the
chair the way he always did now and a plane in his hands and a mitre in
the vice that was not going well.

Sazare cut four dovetails badly.

He had been cutting dovetails badly for eleven months and had improved
from *unusable* to *bad*, which Handa maintained was the largest
improvement available in the trade and the only one that took real
effort.

Then he tried to put a hand on Handa's shoulder.

It went wrong from about the second inch. He had rehearsed the
geometry, which was the first error, and he had chosen the moment,
which was the second, and what actually happened was that he raised his
right arm from a standing start at a distance of three feet, travelled
it through the air with the deliberation of a man setting a stone, and
placed his palm flat on the top of Handa's shoulder, and left it there.

The plane stopped.

"What," said Handa, "are you doing."

"Chapter five."

"Chapter *five*."

"Of the Hand and the Shoulder." Sazare removed the hand. "It's about
ordinary contact. I've never done it. I've had my hands on about four
thousand people professionally and I have never once put a hand on
anybody I actually knew, and I've been carrying the chapter since the
ninth month and I decided this evening to stop carrying it."

Handa put the plane down on its side, which he did for very little.

"Right," he said. "Well, first off, that was the worst thing that's
happened to me in this shed and my pelvis broke in it."

"Yes."

"It went on for about four seconds."

"Six. I counted."

"You *counted*—" Handa took his cap off, looked at it, and put it back
on. "Lad. You can't put it on the *top*. Top of the shoulder's what you
do to a horse. It goes on the back, here, or it goes on the arm, and it
goes on for about as long as it takes to say a short word, and mostly
you're already saying the word, that's the whole trick of it. It's not
a manoeuvre. It's punctuation."

"That's better than eleven pages."

"It's not better than eleven pages, it's just shorter, and I've had
forty-six years of it and the bloke who wrote your book hadn't." Handa
went back to the mitre. "Now stop trying to do it and hand me the
forty-five."

---

It happened forty minutes later and he did not notice it happening.

They had got the mitre out of the vice and established that the fault
was in the stock and not in the cut, which Handa had known for an hour
and had wanted somebody else to say, and in the course of establishing
it Handa had said, apropos of the grain and of nothing:

"That nurse came back, by the way."

"Which nurse."

"Little cross one. Tomoe." Handa turned the board over. "Second week of
last month. Brought the bun. You put her up to it."

"I asked her whether she remembered a man in the fourth-month ward with
a pelvis in three pieces who was rude to her about a bun eleven years
ago. She said she remembered four of them and asked which."

"That's about right." Handa examined the end grain with a great deal
more attention than the end grain required. "And I was rude to her
again. Straight off. Couldn't stop it. She'd got as far as the door and
I said something about the candle and she gave me a look that could
take paint off, and then she sat down for twenty minutes and told me
what happened to her hand."

"What happened to her hand?"

"Ask her yourself, it's not mine to hand out." He set the board down.
"Eleven years I've had that bun going round my head. Eleven years. And
it turns out the woman remembers four of us and doesn't think about it
at all, and I could have had that fact any time in the last decade by
walking two streets and asking, and I didn't, because—"

He stopped.

"Because you'd have had to walk two streets and ask," said Sazare.

"Because I'd have had to walk two streets and ask," Handa agreed, "and
it's easier to have a thing going round your head for eleven years, and
that's a stupid way to run a life, and I'm forty-six and I'd do it
again tomorrow."

And Sazare said, "You wouldn't," and put his hand on Handa's back,
about where the shoulder blade finished, for approximately the length
of a short word, and took it off again, and reached past him for the
try square.

It was four minutes before he realised.

He was measuring off the shoulder of the mitre when the whole of it
arrived at once — the distance, the duration, the placement, the fact
that he had been mid-sentence, the fact that there had been no check
running and no count and no geometry and no chapter five anywhere in
his head at all — and his hand stopped on the square.

Handa did not look up from the vice.

"Aye," he said. "That one was fine."

---

He walked home the long way, past the west market, which was shut, and
did not slow down going past it.

*Third time,* he wrote, at the low table, with the shed still on his
coat.

*The birthday arrived when I stopped planning it. The finding arrived
when I stopped writing it up. The hand arrived while I was reaching for
a try square.*

*Three is not a coincidence. Three is a rate.*

*I have been treating this as a series of separate failures of
preparation and it is not that. It is one mechanism and it is the same
mechanism every time: the check is the thing that breaks it. Running
the check makes the hand hesitate, and a hesitating hand is
unreadable, which is the exact reason I gave Sakura this morning for
why I could not practise on her — and I gave it as a fact about her
position and it is also a fact about the check.*

*I do not yet know how to stop running the check. I know that it is a
check and not care, because care did not make the hand stop; care put
it there.*

*Also: Handa says the top of the shoulder is what you do to a horse.
Eleven pages did not contain that.*
