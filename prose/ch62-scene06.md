# Chapter 62, Scene 6 — Then Bang All You Like

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the east side, second floor. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~2580 words.*

---

He went back at two for the last of it and there was almost no last of
it: the futon rolled against the wall for the landlord, the low table
that had never been his, the two blankets, the bowl.

He carried the bowl down to Kurihara Nao and she took it and said, "It's
yours," and he said, "It's yours," and she said, "I gave it to you," and
he said, "I know, and I'm going, and it's a good bowl and you'll want
it," and she looked at him for a moment and then took it and said, "Well
then," and shut the door, and that was the whole of it, and he stood in
the passage for about four seconds.

Then he went up one flight and stopped at the bottom of his own stair,
where Ōtaki Sue's door was.

The kettle was going.

*It has been going every day for seventeen days.*

He knocked, hard, three times, flat of the hand, low down where she would
feel it in the frame — which she had taught him in the ninth month of his
sixteenth year by opening the door on the fourth night and writing *KNOCK
LOWER. FRAME NOT DOOR* and handing it to him without any expression
whatsoever.

She opened it in about nine seconds.

---

Ōtaki Sue was seventy-three and four feet eleven and in the same grey
cotton wrapper, worn a little whiter at the elbows than it had been two
years ago, and her hair was cut off short and level at the jaw the way it
had been cut off short and level at the jaw since about the war.

She looked up at him.

Then she looked at him properly, which was a thing she did that nobody
else did, and which had taken him about four months to get used to and
about a year to understand: she read his whole face, deliberately,
without hurrying, for as long as she needed, and she did not pretend she
was not doing it, because there was nothing else for her to go on and
she had never once been embarrassed about it.

She had been doing it to him for two years and three months.

She stood back and let him in.

---

The room was the same.

Table under the window. Two chairs. The kettle. A shelf with about nine
things on it and every one of them had been there since he first came
down, and he had catalogued them at seventeen out of habit and had never
once had a use for the catalogue.

And on the wall beside the door, on a nail, a slate.

It had one thing written on it in a fast, square, beautifully controlled
hand, in chalk that had been there so long it had gone into the surface
and would not have come off with water.

*THEN BANG ALL YOU LIKE*

She had never wiped it. She had never wiped it and she had never
mentioned it and neither had he, and it had been on that nail since the
ninth month of his sixteenth year, which was the third day he had known
her.

---

She poured the tea before anything, because she always poured the tea
before anything, and then she sat down opposite him and put the working
slate and the chalk on the table between them and turned it very
slightly towards him, which meant *go on*.

He wrote:

*I AM MOVING OUT TODAY.*

She read it.

She did not look up for about two seconds.

Then she took the chalk out of his hand — she always took it, she had
never once waited to be handed it — and wrote underneath, in the square
hand:

*I KNOW. 17 DAYS.*

He looked at that.

Then he wrote:

*HOW.*

She wiped the slate with the side of her fist and wrote:

*YOU STOPPED WORKING ABOVE ME.*

---

He sat at a table in a deaf woman's kitchen and had to put the chalk
down for a second, which was the second time in three years he had had to
do that in that room.

*Of course she knows.*

*She has known since the twenty-eighth of the eleventh month. She has
known since the first night the bench did not go, and she told me in the
eighth month of last year — no, she did not tell me, Ashida told me,
in a corridor, that she hears it through the floor.*

*Two channels. She has got two. This table twice a week, and eleven
hours a night of a man working over her head, and she has had the second
one since I was sixteen and it stopped seventeen days ago and she has not
come up the stair to ask, because she is seventy-three and it is a stair,
and because she has never once asked me a question I had not sat down at
this table to be asked.*

He picked the chalk up.

*I AM SORRY. I SHOULD HAVE COME DOWN.*

She read it, and wiped it, and wrote:

*YES.*

And then, before he could do anything with that, she wiped it again and
wrote:

*BUT I KNEW WHY.*

---

She got up and went to the shelf and took something down from behind the
nine things, which he had catalogued at seventeen and which had therefore
been the wrong catalogue for two years.

It was a slate. A smaller one, an old one, cracked across one corner and
mended with wire.

She had written on it and not wiped it.

*24/11*

*WHAT HAS HAPPENED TO YOUR FACE*

He sat and looked at it.

*The twenty-fourth of the eleventh month.*

*I came down on the twenty-fourth because it was a Tuesday and we write
on Tuesdays and Fridays and I have not missed one in two years, and I
came down and she wrote that, and I wrote something back, and I have got
no memory whatever of what I wrote.*

She turned the slate over.

He had written it in his own hand, in the clerk's hand his mother had
made him do properly at seven years old, and it was three words.

*I CANNOT SAY.*

---

*Right.*

*Sit with it before you do anything.*

*I wrote that on the twenty-fourth of the eleventh month and it was
completely true. It was true in every particular. On the twenty-fourth
there was no arrangement, there was no sentence, there had been a kiss on
a cheek in a kitchen and one in an office and I had three readings and no
way of picking between them, and I could not have said what had happened
to my face if my life had depended on it.*

*It was the honest answer and I have never once regretted it.*

*And she kept the slate.*

*She has kept a slate with three words on it behind nine things on a
shelf for three weeks, and she has not wiped it, and she wiped everything
else, and she has been sitting under an empty room for seventeen days
with it up there.*

*And on the eleventh of this month I wrote the same three words to a man
in the Land of Sound and they were the first lie of my life.*

*And I have got the entry for it and I wrote it out properly and I said*
a true sentence deployed to produce a false belief *and I did not soften
it.*

*The same three words, seventeen days apart, in the same hand.*

*One of them the most honest thing I had, and one of them the worst thing
I have ever done.*

He put the small slate down on the table very carefully.

Then he picked up the chalk.

---

*Now.*

*There is no sound in this room.*

He stopped with the chalk on the slate and did not write for about four
seconds, because it had arrived all at once and he wanted to have it
before he acted on it.

*There has never been any sound in this room.*

*Nobody has ever overheard anything in here. Nobody could. There is no
voice, there is no volume, there is nothing for a man on a landing to
catch through a door and there is nothing for anybody to have heard
through a floor.*

*It is written, and then it is wiped, and there is nothing left.*

*It is the only room in Konohagakure where I could say the whole of it
out loud, and it has been four feet from the bottom of my own stair for
two years and three months, and I have used it to talk about hinges.*

He wrote.

*I FELL IN LOVE.*

He looked at it, and then wrote the rest of it before he could stop, in a
smaller hand because he was running out of slate:

*ON THE 11TH OF LAST MONTH. IT IS NOT ALLOWED. NOBODY CAN KNOW. THAT IS
WHY I COULD NOT SAY.*

He turned it round.

She read it.

---

She read it twice, which she did not usually do, and then she looked up
at him.

And her whole face came apart into an enormous gap-toothed grin.

She thumped him on the chest with the flat of her hand, hard, twice —
exactly as hard as she had done it on the third day, over a piece of
crystal on a floor at six seconds — and took the slate off him and
wiped it with her sleeve until there was nothing on it at all, and then
went at it a second time with the cloth off the kettle handle, and then
held it up to the window and looked at it against the light.

Then she put it down and wrote:

*GOOD.*

And underneath:

*I KNEW ON THE 22ND.*

---

He wrote: *HOW.*

She wiped it.

*I HAVE SEEN THAT FACE. I HAD IT.*

He stopped.

She was already writing.

*1958. HE WAS 24. I WAS 26. HE COULD NOT WRITE.*

Wipe.

*I TAUGHT HIM. TOOK 2 YEARS. HIS HAND WAS TERRIBLE.*

Wipe.

*11 YEARS. THEN THE 2ND WAR.*

She put the chalk down for a second and picked it up again.

*NOBODY IN THIS BUILDING KNOWS THAT. YOU ARE THE 3RD PERSON I HAVE TOLD
IN 40 YEARS.*

---

He sat in a chair at a table in a kitchen on the second floor of a
building on the east side and could not do anything at all for about
eleven seconds.

*She has had this the entire time.*

*Two years and three months. Twice a week. Four hundred slates about a
window catch and a stair-nosing and a tap with the wrong washer in it,
and about nine hundred lines of the two of us being extremely funny about
the bathhouse door.*

*And underneath all of it there is a man who could not write and it took
her two years to teach him and his hand was terrible.*

*I have got a catalogue of nine objects on that shelf that I made at
seventeen out of habit.*

*Nine objects and I never once asked her one question about any of them.*

*She has been reading my face for two years because it is the only
channel she has got.*

*And I have had the same channel available the entire time and I have
used it to look at her hand.*

He picked up the chalk.

*I NEVER ASKED YOU ANYTHING.*

She read it, and made a noise — she made noises sometimes, and they were
not shaped, and she had no way of knowing what they sounded like and had
never appeared to care — and wrote:

*NO.*

Wipe.

*NEITHER DID ANYBODY ELSE. 40 YEARS. YOU WROTE TWICE A WEEK.*

Wipe.

*THAT IS NOT NOTHING, BOY.*

---

They sat there for a while.

Then she wrote the last one, and she took her time over it, and it came
out in the square hand with every stroke where she meant it, which he had
seen her do about four hundred times and had never once seen her hurry.

*I WILL NOT FEEL YOU ANY MORE.*

He read it.

She wiped it and wrote underneath:

*THAT IS THE PART I MIND. NOT THE TEA.*

And then, before he had got the chalk:

*BUT I FELT YOU FOR 2 YEARS AND I HAVE HAD 40 WITH NOTHING OVER MY
HEAD.*

Wipe.

*SO WE ARE UP.*

---

He wrote — and he did not build it, and it came out badly and in the
wrong order and he had to go over the top of a word he had got wrong:

*I WILL COME ON TUESDAYS.*

She read it.

She looked at him for a long moment with the whole of her attention, the
way she had looked at him in a doorway on the third day while he
apologised to a deaf woman in a flat voice with his hands at his sides.

Then she wrote:

*NO YOU WILL NOT.*

Wipe.

*YOU WILL COME EVERY TUESDAY FOR 3 MONTHS. THEN 1 IN 2. THEN YOU WILL BE
BUSY.*

Wipe.

*THAT IS NOT A COMPLAINT. IT IS WHAT HAPPENS AND IT IS CORRECT.*

Wipe.

*COME WHEN YOU COME. DO NOT MAKE IT A DUTY. I HAVE HAD ENOUGH PEOPLE
DOING THINGS OUT OF DUTY TO LAST ME.*

She put the chalk down and pushed the slate an inch towards him, which
meant *go on*.

He wrote one word.

*TUESDAYS.*

And she looked at it, and then she laughed — properly, out loud, at a
volume she could not hear and had never been able to moderate, which was
the loudest noise anybody made in that building — and she wiped the slate
with her sleeve and did not write anything else.

---

Sakura was in the doorway.

He did not know how long she had been there. The door had been on the
latch since he came in because Ōtaki Sue kept it on the latch in the
daytime, and there was no way to knock on it that she would have felt
from a table, and Sakura Haruno had come up a passage at some point in
the last four minutes and had stopped.

She was not looking at either of them.

She was looking at the wall by the door, at a nail, at a slate that had
five words on it in chalk that had gone into the surface.

*THEN BANG ALL YOU LIKE*

"What's that?" she said.

Sazare stood up.

"That's the first thing she ever wrote to me," he said.

"What does it—"

"I was sixteen and I got a thing to hold for six seconds and I laughed on
the floor," said Sazare, "and she felt it through her ceiling and hit it
with a broom handle, and I came down the next night and apologised to a
deaf woman out loud with my hands at my sides for about a minute before I
worked out what was wrong with me.

"And she let me get all the way to the end of it.

"And then she asked me what was funny."

Sakura did not say anything.

"It's been on that nail for two years and three months," said Sazare.
"She wipes everything. She's never wiped that one and neither of us has
ever mentioned it."

Behind him, Ōtaki Sue got up to see to the kettle, and did not look
round, and had not heard one word of it.
