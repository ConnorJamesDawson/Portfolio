# Chapter 11, Scene 1 — Small Repairs

*Act 2, part C. Sazare Ruri, age fourteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

The second year he became, without anybody deciding it, the town's mender of
small things.

It began with the smith, who came up in the spring about his shoulder — the
hammer shoulder, the one every smith over forty owns — and stayed to drink tea
on the porch, and produced, with the elaborate offhandedness of a man
producing the true reason for his visit, a twist of cloth with a stone in it.

"My girl's betrothal piece," he said. "Her mother's before. Chipped it on the
well-head, and she won't wear it now, and she won't say why she won't wear
it, and the wedding's the eighth month." He turned the twist in his enormous
fingers without opening it. "The jeweller down Shiranui says it wants
regrinding and he'd have it a month and the price of a calf. And Tōan says—"
he looked deeply uncomfortable — "Tōan says his lad knows stone."

Sazare looked across the porch at Tōan, who was reading a supplier's list and
did not look up, in the manner of a man conspicuously not looking up.

The stone was river jade, cloudy green, cut in the old flat oval that country
pieces were cut in, and the chip was off one shoulder of it: a bright raw
bite, small, catastrophic, exactly where the eye went first. Sazare turned it
under the light. His hands knew it before he did — the family had finished a
thousand bought stones in the off months; growing was the trade's heart, but
cutting had paid its rent — and he heard himself asking for grits and a strap
of leather and a fortnight, and the smith left looking as though he had got
away with something.

It took him nine evenings.

He had no wheel, so he did it the old way, the slow way, lap and grit and
patience, and he did not try to hide the wound — you could not; the stone was
too shallow for regrinding, whatever the Shiranui man said, and taking the
whole face down would leave it thin and dead. Instead he did what his mother
had done with number four the morning a clerk had come to the house: he made
the fault the feature. He cut the chipped shoulder back to a clean small
facet, a deliberate flat among the curve, and matched it with a second on the
opposite shoulder, so that the stone caught light now at two points where it
had caught it nowhere before, and what had been a flaw became a style.

The smith's daughter wore it at her wedding in the eighth month. Sazare was
not at the wedding, but half the town was, and the town talked, and after
that the small broken things of three valleys found their way up the road to
Tōan's porch: cracked hairpins, a merchant's seal-stone worn past reading, a
string of coral beads a dog had personally redistributed. He fixed what could
be fixed and said plainly what could not, and charged tradesman's rates that
Tōan reviewed once, called ruinously low, and repriced.

None of it was growing. That door stayed shut, and the tray on the porch rail
stayed level and damp and untouched.

But it was stone under his hands in the evenings, and the smell of wet grit,
and the particular quiet of finishing work — and some nights, bent over the
lap with the lamp hissing, he caught himself half-listening for a hum out of
the next room, nine notes, none of them quite in tune, and would have to
stop for a while and stand on the porch in the dark until the steam and the
river put everything back where it went.

---

Gonta came down from the timber camp on the first of every month, in
principle for the leg, in practice for the cards.

The leg had healed as well as legs heal; he walked with a hitch that would
be his to keep, and had already turned it into a manner. He was fifteen, and
loud, and constitutionally unable to shuffle without dealing off the bottom,
which he did so badly that watching him try was better entertainment than
honest cards would ever have been.

"You're doing it again," Sazare said.

"Doing what."

"Your thumb. Your whole thumb does — that." He demonstrated. "Every time you
go to the bottom, your thumb sticks out like it's asking directions."

"My thumb," said Gonta, with dignity, "was crushed by a log, and has a life
of its own, and you'd know that if you had any—" and lost the rest of it,
because Sazare had reached across and dealt off the bottom clean, faces
down, cards riding flush, nothing moving but two fingers.

There was a silence.

"Right," said Gonta. "Right. You're doing that at the autumn fair, at the
chestnut stall, on Big Ichi, and I'm holding the money."

"No."

"Half's yours—"

"No," said Sazare, and dealt the next hand fairly, and could not entirely
keep the corner of his mouth where it belonged, which Gonta noted aloud and
at volume, because a thing like that, in that house, in those years, was
news.

---

The bathhouses were where the town actually lived, and by the second year he
had learned their whole society: which pools belonged by custom to the
miners, which to the old women, which hour the farmers came, who could be
spoken to in the water and who went there precisely to be spoken to by
nobody.

Tose held the fourth pool of the old bathhouse the way a daimyō holds a
province.

She was eighty-one, and had kept the bathhouse herself for fifty years
before her knees resigned, and she came to Tōan's fortnightly for the knees
and stayed two hours for the audience. She had decided the first month that
Sazare belonged to her — "the quiet one, the one that scrubs the copper; his
hands are wasted on you, Tōan, look at them" — and thereafter she told him
things, on the porch, unasked, in a rolling flat drawl that took no
questions and needed none.

The springs were the blood of the country — this was Tose's constant text —
and the country was too poor to rob and too warm to hate, and this was not
an accident. "You want to know why nobody's ever conquered us, boy? Nothing
to take. Water you can't carry, and hills, and old women. The great nations—"
she said the words the way you'd hold up something a dog had brought in —
"the great nations need a *thing*, don't they. A mine, a port, a grudge.
We're all bath and no strongbox. It's the safest way to be in the whole
world, and every fool in every great country pities us for it."

Sazare scrubbed the copper and listened.

He listened the way he did everything now, collecting, and what he collected
off Tose across a year of fortnights was a country. Not the shape of it —
Mizuho's eight strokes had done the shape — but the *grain*: which valleys
had married into which; where the road-shrines were and whose dead they
carried; why the north pools ran milky in the fifth month; what the town had
done in her grandmother's day when a warlord's tax men came up the road
(fed them, bathed them, sent them home fat and empty-handed: "an army can
take anything in the world off you except a good time, boy; a good time
you have to be *given*").

Half a sentence, Mizuho had spent. *One of the nine that don't matter.*

He sat on a porch in the middle of the half-sentence, in the steam, with an
old woman explaining seventy years of it to him because his hands reminded
her of somebody, and he had long stopped being able to hold the two things
in his head at once — the map with five blobs on it, and the ground, which
went on mattering in every direction for as far as anybody walked.

---

The autumn fair was the mistake.

He had meant only to pass along the top of it — the fair took the shrine
street and the river street both, and the errand took him across the head of
the shrine street, and the whole thing was as safe as a market, was a
market, stalls and noise and chestnuts and Gonta somewhere in it losing
money with his eloquent thumb.

Then the drums came up the street, and the crowd turned.

It happened the way water happens: four hundred people in motion becoming,
in a breath and a half, four hundred people stopped and facing one way, at
a procession coming down under the lanterns — and Sazare's body left.

That was the honest description, and he assembled it afterwards from the
outside, because he was not present for the middle of it. The middle was:
crowd, all facing; a platform of light coming toward him above heads; and
then a gap, and then he was two streets away in the dark of a woodyard
with his back against a stack and his heart trying the boards of his chest
one at a time, and no memory whatever of the distance between.

He stood in the woodyard until the drums had gone down the river street and
the crowd noise had gone back to being market noise — loose, multidirectional,
safe — and then he walked home the long way, along the water, in the smell
of sulphur and fried batter.

He told Tōan, because the house's laws did not require it but the trade's
did: an assistant who could vanish was a fact with medical standing.

Tōan heard the whole of it, nodded, and asked one question.

"The fair, or the facing?"

"The facing."

"Markets no trouble? Queues? The bathhouse at the busy hour?"

"None."

"Then it's not crowds, and we won't waste a year treating you for crowds."
He put a jar back on its shelf. "It's the arrangement. You know where you
saw the arrangement last, and so do I, and there's no herb for it. What
there is—" he wiped his hands, "—is the door. Any room you're in, know your
door. Not to use it. Knowing it is usually enough — a body stands easier
next to a way out. And the year that stops working, you tell me."

"Does it stop working?"

"Sometimes it stops being needed," said Tōan. "That's different, and it's
slower, and it's not a promise. Wood's low."

The wood was not low. Sazare went and split it anyway, two-handed, in the
blue evening, and the axe came down clean, and from the fair on the far
side of the town the drums came small across the water, going away.
