# Chapter 9, Scene 3 — What She'd Have Said

*Act 2, part A. Sazare Ruri, age thirteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

Bandō was standing about four feet away with his cap in his hand, because he had
been reading the wall, because everybody read the wall.

It was the first time in eight days that the whole of him had been turned
towards Sazare, and the effect was of a door that has been painted shut coming
open, and Sazare stood very still, the way he stood when it mattered, and
waited to be hit.

Bandō did not hit him.

He looked at him for a while — down at him; there was a great deal of down — and
then he looked back at the wall, at a patch of it, and Sazare followed his eyes
and found, in the middle height, in a strong plain hand:

*Tomi.* And a line through it. And after the line, in the same hand, not a
different one, the word: *no.*

He had written it himself, then. Asked his own question and answered it, in the
same chalk, so that nobody else would ever have to tell him.

"She put you on the floor," said Bandō. "In the shed. Three times."

"Yes."

"She told me about it." His voice was very level, the way Iwao's had been level
across a table, the register of a man keeping his hands still by keeping his
voice still. "Not the night it happened. After. She said a boy walked the chain
cold, at night, at twelve, and Ryō tested him on the floor like an idiot,
because the floor was the only test Ryō had." He turned the cap over in his
hands, once. "She thought that was funny. Testing a lamp by hitting it."

Sazare said nothing. There was nothing in his chest to say anything with.

"I'm not going to tell you it wasn't you," said Bandō. "You've had that off
Ryōsuke, the three things. He does the three things." The cap went over again.
"And I'm not going to tell you it was you, because I was there, and I know what
the order was, and I know what four men on a platform means, and I've done the
count more times than you have. You'll do the count for the rest of your life
and it won't ever come out. That's not a punishment. It's just sums that don't
finish."

"Then what," said Sazare — and it came out of him in a voice he did not
recognise, thirteen years old and cracked down the middle, the first thing he
had asked another human being for since the district office — "what am I
supposed to *do*?"

Bandō thought about it.

He thought about it properly, the way the men at the weighbridge had thought
about horses, turning it over, taking his time, and Sazare stood in front of
him and shook slightly and let him.

"She'd have said—" Bandō stopped. Started again. "Tomi patched people. That was
hers. Fifteen years, every door we ever went through, she went through last,
because she was no use at the front and every use at the back. And what she
said about it — she said it to Ryō once, when he tried to move her up the
order — she said: *the front of the door is spoken for. Somebody has to be the
back of the door.*"

He put his cap on.

"You've been the front of a door once," he said. "Everybody saw how you liked
it. So that's what I'd do, if I were you and thirteen and had the hands for it.
I'd find out whether I was any good at the back."

And he walked out of the exchange into the evening, and he never once in his
life said another word to Sazare — not from coldness; they simply were never
again in the same country — and it took years for Sazare to understand what had
been given to him in front of that wall, by that man, at what cost, on the
ninth day of the worst year of both their lives.

---

The argument about him happened on the seventh day, in the back room, and he
heard all of it, because the back room shared a wall of planks with the net
store, and because he was thirteen and had been raised on a step by a door.

He did not even have to be still for it. He simply had to be a boy sorting net
weights on the other side of a wall, which is what he was.

"—not the question." Anzu's voice. "The question's where he's least of a
liability, and the answer's inland, end of."

"The answer's a boat south." A man's voice he half knew — one of the boat crew.
"There are people in the capital who'd house him. Proper people. He's the last
of a — you don't leave a thing like that sitting in a net loft, Anzu, he's—"

"Say what he is."

A pause.

"He's *valuable*," the man said, and had the grace to say it as though the word
had cost him something.

"He's a bloodline," said Anzu, flat. "Say it, if we're saying it. He's the last
of a line, and there are four different kinds of people who'd want him for
that, and two of those kinds are supposed to be on our side. That's exactly why
he goes inland to Tōan, and not to the capital, and not to *proper people*, and
not anywhere anybody with a ledger and a long view can get a look at him and
start having ideas about stud value—"

"Nobody said—"

"Nobody ever says it," said Anzu. "People just start being very interested in
whether a boy's healthy. I've been at this eleven years. Inland. Tōan. He gets
his arm back and he learns a trade with his own name off it, and in five years
if he wants to be valuable to somebody that'll be his to decide. It is not
going to be decided in this room while he's thirteen, and it is certainly not
going to be decided by us, because if we do it, then the whole of what we're
for is a lie with better manners."

A long silence.

"Ryō?" said the man.

And Ryōsuke, who had been in the room the whole time — Sazare had not known he
was in the room, and took note, again, that Ryōsuke was a man you did not know
was in the room — said only:

"Kohaku asked me twice whether the boy was carrying anything real. She was rude
about it the second time." A chair moved. "Her whole life was spending people
well. She wouldn't spend him at all. I'd call that the last order she gave."

Nobody argued with that.

Sazare sat on the other side of the wall with a net weight in his good hand,
and found that he was crying, without any noise, which was a skill now, like
everything else.

---

Ryōsuke walked him out on the ninth day, as far as the edge of the town, where
the coast road turned off up the first valley and became the inland road.

There was a carter going up to the spring towns with dried fish and lamp oil,
who had agreed for a coin to a passenger, and who was in no hurry, and whose
horse was extremely fat. Everything Sazare owned went up on the tailboard in
one hand: the cloth roll, the coat, Jirō's belt-purse with nothing in it, kept
anyway.

Ryōsuke gave him three things, because he was Ryōsuke.

"One. Nobody in this country is looking for you, but Kiri hunts abroad — not
often, not everywhere, but they do it, and they hunt bloodlines harder than
they hunt debts. Your protection is that their file says a part-trained boy who
grows hollow. So: no crystal where anyone can see. Not a splinter. Not to show
off, not to pay a debt, not to save a life you could save some slower way.
The day you grow in daylight is the day the file gets corrected."

"I don't grow any more," said Sazare.

"I know," said Ryōsuke, in a voice that knew everything that sentence was
carrying, and went on.

"Two. There are people who collect what Kiri throws away. Not governments —
collectors. There's one in particular, and his people are polite, and patient,
and they pay for introductions, and every so often somebody who smiles well
turns up next to a boy like you knowing a word like Shōton and being kind about
it. Kindness from strangers who already know what you are — you walk. You
don't explain. You walk."

"Who is he?"

"If you're ever close enough to need the name," said Ryōsuke, "something's
already gone wrong. Three."

He took out a twist of waxed paper, and Sazare knew what it was by the weight
before it was in his hand: crystal chips. Cloudy, worthless, a dozen of them.

"The station moves. Towns change. People change. But anywhere on this coast
that's ours — an exchange, a chandler, a net store — there'll be a rope-peg
board, and the second peg from the left is the postbox, same as the chandler's
on the west quay, because I'm an old man and I only had the one idea." The
corner of his mouth moved, a quarter-inch, the first time in seventeen days.
"You read hands. So: any of those twelve, wedged flush, means *ours, safe,
knock*. You keep them against the day you need a door. And if a chip's there in
a hand you don't know—"

"Then it isn't yours any more," said Sazare, "and I walk."

"You walk," said Ryōsuke.

He stood back from the cart. The carter clicked at the fat horse, and the whole
arrangement came into motion at the pace of the countryside.

Ryōsuke did not say goodbye, and did not say good luck, and did not tell him he
would be all right. He stood in the road with his hands in his pockets and
watched, and just before the bend took him out of sight he called, in the flat
carrying voice of a man saying a completely ordinary thing:

"She was rude about it the second time because I'd asked her *why not use him,
he's a natural*. You were. She said—" and the bend was coming, and Ryōsuke did
not hurry — "*not him. He doesn't get to be useful. He gets to be somebody's
brother.*"

The road turned. The town went away behind the shoulder of the hill.

Sazare rode up into the green country with the steam columns standing over the
valleys, and he did not see the sea again for two years.
