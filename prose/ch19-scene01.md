# Chapter 19, Scene 1 — Four Konoha

*Act 3, part A. Nami, the Land of Waves. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~2,300 words.*

---

He saw the first of them on the plank walk at about the second hour of the
afternoon, two days after the veranda, in a thin grey drizzle that had
been going since dawn and had put the whole town under a low ceiling of
cloud the colour of dishwater.

The tide was out. The mud under the walk lay slick and shining and dimpled
with rain, giving off its cold iodine smell, and a heron stood in the
middle of it forty yards out, entirely still, ignoring everybody. The
boards were greasy. Water came off the eaves in strings.

And coming up the walk toward him, in single file because the walk is
three feet wide, were four people who did not belong to the Land of
Waves.

Sazare stepped aside into a doorway to let them by, which is what
everybody does on that walk, and put his eyes on the middle distance,
which is what he had done for four years.

He got all four of them in the eleven seconds it took them to pass.

---

**The girl was in front.**

Twelve, maybe thirteen. Pink hair — actually pink, a pale washed-out
rose, long, held off her face with a red cloth band and going darker and
heavier as the rain got into it. A red sleeveless dress over dark shorts,
the dress soaked across the shoulders. Wide green eyes. A very high
forehead that she had done something about with her fringe, unsuccessfully,
and was conscious of; he watched her put a hand up to it twice in eleven
seconds without knowing she was doing it.

She was walking point on a client detail in a foreign country and she was
*doing it properly*. That was what made him look at her twice. She had the
right interval — six feet, not four, not ten — and she checked the
doorways as she came, one at a time, in order, left then right, the way
you are taught and the way almost nobody bothers with after the first
week; and when she checked his doorway she looked at him for about a third
of a second and her eyes went to his hands and then off, and she did not
break step.

She was frightened. He could see that too. It came off her the way it
comes off anybody: a fraction too much in the shoulders, a jaw doing a
little more work than a jaw needs to.

*She's the one doing the job,* he thought, *and she's the only one of them
who's scared, and those two facts are the same fact.*

**The old man was second.**

Tazuna, in the same sleeveless jacket and the same white cloth on his
head, gone darker in the wet, walking in the middle of a four-person box
with his hands in his pockets and an expression of monumental
self-consciousness, like a man being escorted to his own surprise party.
He was not drunk. That was new. He looked, if anything, ten years younger
and about four times as awkward.

He saw Sazare in the doorway and gave him a nod — one, small, without
stopping — and Sazare gave it back, and neither of them said anything, and
Sazare stood in a doorway on a wet plank walk and felt something in his
chest do a thing he had no name for.

**The dark-haired boy was third and Sazare had him assessed in under two
seconds, which was two seconds more than it deserved.**

Twelve. Black hair, thick, standing up at the back in a way that was not
an accident. Dark eyes set deep. A high-collared blue shirt, soaked
through, with a white crest on the back of it — a round fan, red at the
top and white at the bottom — that meant nothing whatever to Sazare and
that he registered anyway, because he registered everything. Bandaged
forearms. Very clean weapon pouch. Good boots, better than the girl's.

He moved beautifully. That was simply true and Sazare noted it without
pleasure: economical, balanced, no wasted correction on the greasy boards,
the walk of somebody who has been correctly taught from the age of five by
people who knew what they were doing.

He did not check a single doorway.

He went past Sazare at four feet with his hands in his pockets and his
chin up and did not look at him, and Sazare — sixteen, five foot five,
soaked, with a bad knee and a mender's roll under his arm and four lines
in a ledger nobody could read — stood in the doorway and thought, quite
calmly:

*You are the best of them and you are the one I'd be least worried about,
because you have been told you are the best of them, and I've watched a
man with a scull in his hands work out in four seconds what you didn't
work out in four feet.*

**And the fourth one was making a noise.**

---

He was blond. Very blond, sun-yellow, sticking up in every direction and
plastered flat where the rain had got at it. Orange — an orange
tracksuit, an actual orange, in a country of grey and green and
brown, with blue at the shoulders, which was the single most tactically
illiterate garment Sazare had ever seen on a person who was supposed to be
in this trade. Blue eyes. Three thin dark lines on each cheek that
Sazare took for scars and dismissed. A forehead protector with a leaf on
it, worn on the forehead, correctly, shining wet.

He was filthy. That was the thing that actually snagged, for about a
quarter of a second: not town-filthy, *woods*-filthy — bark scrapes down
both forearms, pine needles in the collar, a long green smear up one leg
of the orange, and the specific grey exhaustion round the eyes of somebody
who has not been sleeping indoors.

And he was talking. Loudly, continuously, up the back of the file, at the
dark-haired boy, who was not answering.

"—and I *did*, I got past the fourth branch, you weren't even *there*, you
went off and did your—"

"Naruto," said the girl, without turning round.

"I'm just SAYING—"

"You're saying it at a *volume*."

Sazare watched him go past.

He was twelve, and he was two inches shorter than the other boy, and he
was carrying his weight badly, and his stance when he stopped — he stopped
twice in eleven seconds, both times to argue — was a mess. He had no
awareness of the doorway with a stranger in it four feet away. None. He
went past it inside arm's reach at a full shout with his back half turned
and his hands out of his pockets doing something illustrative.

Sazare put him in the tray in the place where he put things that were not
going to matter.

*The loud one is the weakest and knows it, which is why he's loud. He'll
be dead inside two years or he'll be a chūnin who does the paperwork. He's
the one they let carry the bags.*

He was, in fairness to himself, entirely correct about the evidence in
front of him.

---

**The fourth of the four was the one he thought about for a week.**

He came last, thirty feet back, which was itself a piece of information,
because a man who walks thirty feet behind a client detail in a hostile
country is either useless or is covering the whole of it and has decided
that the back is where the problem will come from.

He was tall and he was slouching and he was *hurt*.

Crutch under the left arm. Not a stick — a proper crutch, cut for him or
adjusted for him, taking real weight. Left leg not being used at all
below the knee. Right hand free, which was, Sazare noted, the correct
choice and had cost him something in balance to arrange.

Silver hair. Not grey; silver, and standing up on one side. A dark
close-fitting mask over the whole lower half of his face, and his
forehead protector down over his left eye, so that the entire visible
portion of the man was one dark eye and about three inches of skin.

He was reading a book. In the rain. On a crutch. It was small and orange
and he had it open in his free hand and was turning the pages with his
thumb, and he was — Sazare would have staked the whole of his coat lining
on this — not reading one word of it.

Because as he came level with the doorway, the eye came off the page and
went over Sazare: the hair, the roll, the boots, the leg, the hands, and
back to the face, in about a quarter of a second, without the man's head
moving at all.

And went back to the page.

Sazare stood absolutely still in a doorway on a plank walk with the rain
coming off the eaves in front of him and his heart going like a hammer.

*Still water,* said a room full of eight-year-olds in the fog. *Nothing is
being poured in and nothing is going out.*

The tall man went past at a limping unhurried pace, four feet away, turned
a page, and did not look up again.

---

He was two streets inland before he trusted his hands enough to unroll the
canvas and check a chisel he had no reason to check.

Then he sat down on a mooring bollard at the top of the town in the rain,
with the gulls going over and the smell of wet tar coming up off the quay,
and did the assessment properly, because he could not not.

*A jōnin.* That was not a guess. Nobody stands thirty feet back with a
crutch and a book and reads a stranger's boots without turning his head
unless he has been doing it for fifteen years. He would be the one who
fought the man on the road; he would be the reason the man on the road was
lying on a mat four miles round the north shore with his chest strapped.

*And he is hurt.* Badly enough for a crutch, ten days on. Which meant
chakra, not bone — bone he would have splinted and walked on, because they
do; a man who cannot put a leg down ten days after a fight and has not
been carried anywhere is a man whose *system* is out, and that takes weeks.

Which meant, and Sazare sat with this in the rain for some time, that
the single most dangerous person in the Land of Waves was currently
operating at a fraction of himself, was perfectly aware of it, and had
therefore arranged the entire detail — the girl on point, the interval,
the thirty feet, the free right hand, the book — around the fact.

He is conserving. He is going to spend what he has left, once, at a moment
he chooses.

Sazare had been on the receiving end of a professional exactly once, on a
shingle beach at Tomari, and had eleven seconds and a hole in a drain to
show for it. He looked down the hill at four people going along the plank
walk in the rain and thought about a man in a fisherman's house with two
punctures in his neck and a strapped chest, up eleven days early, drinking
knitbone and safflower out of a cup with both hands.

Two of them. Both hurt. Both waiting.

Both of them entirely certain of how it was going to go.

---

He got the rest of it over the following two days, because Nami had never
in its life had anything this interesting to talk about and could not stop.

Four of them, staying at Tazuna's. Three children and a man. The children
were *ninja*, apparently, which the town found difficult and had decided
to be pleased about. They had fought the Demon on the road and the man had
put him down and the boy in orange had done something everybody's account
of disagreed about.

They were up in the woods above the town every day from first light.

The girl was polite and helped Tsunami with the washing-up.

The dark one did not speak.

The one in orange had eaten, on the first night, an amount of food that
had entered Nami folklore inside forty-eight hours, and had gone out into
the trees afterwards and had not come back until the small hours, and had
done it again the next night, and the next, and Tsunami was worried about
him and had said so at the water queue.

And the man with the crutch had not left the house.

Sazare listened to all of it with a kettle in his hand or a lock in
pieces on his knee and put every part of it in the tray, loose, unsorted,
turned different ways up, and did not lay any of it beside anything else,
because he had had a great deal of practice by then and because he already
knew what the shape was and did not want it assembled in front of him.

There was going to be a fight on that bridge.

It was going to be a man with a chest wound and a boy in a white mask on
one side, and a jōnin who could not walk and three twelve-year-olds on the
other, and an old drunk in the middle of it who had built fourteen sets of
forms in the water and had lied to a hidden village about the rank of a
job.

And there was, in the whole of the Land of Waves, precisely one person who
had eaten at both tables.
