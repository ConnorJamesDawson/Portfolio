# Chapter 38, Scene 6 — No Return Address

*Act 4, part D. Konoha General, the second floor. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,371 words.*

---

It came up with the internal post on a Wednesday in the tenth month,
in the wire tray on the second-floor desk, between a pharmacy
requisition and a laundry note.

The auxiliary had put it there without comment because there was
nothing about it to comment on.

*Medical Crystal Technician*
*Second Floor*
*Konoha General*

No name. No street. No village on the outside, which meant it had come
in through the ordinary post and been sorted internally, and Sazare
stood at the desk with it in his hand and worked out in about four
seconds that this was the only interesting thing about the envelope and
that it was very interesting indeed.

*That post does not exist outside this building.*

*Kaburagi invented it in the second month of last year. It is not on the
establishment roll under that title; the roll says* technician,
fabrication, medical *and there are three of us. It appears in the
hospital's internal directory, on a card by the sluice door, and on
about eleven forms that do not leave the second floor.*

*Somebody outside this village addressed a letter to a job title that
only exists on a card by a sluice door.*

He did not open it at the desk.

He took it to the sluice, and shut the door, and opened it standing up.

---

It was one sheet, written in a clear, small, entirely unhurried hand,
and it was courteous throughout.

*I hope you will forgive an unsolicited letter, and the discourtesy of
not signing it. Please be assured that the second is not carelessness
and that I am aware of what it costs the recipient.*

*I have read your degradation study.*

Sazare put his other hand on the edge of the sink.

*Not the summary. The materials-test schedules, with the six-hour
interval, and the fifty measurements out to nine days. I do not propose
to say how. I would only observe that a hospital with eleven
departments generates a great deal of paper and that not all of it goes
where it is supposed to.*

*I write because your finding is correct and because nobody has told
you what it means.*

*You established that the sequence degrades before the quantity does —
that at day four the stored charge is still substantially present and
the order inside it has begun to slip, and that by day six a
constrict-then-support pattern has become something closer to both at
once. You wrote three days on the label. That was the right decision
and I would have written three.*

*You then concluded, in your own notes, that this was a fault of the
medium.*

*It is not a fault of the medium. It is what happens to every stored
instruction in a substrate that is not itself alive, and it is the
oldest problem in the discipline you have accidentally entered, and
there are perhaps four people in the world who have worked on it. I do
not believe any of them are in the Land of Fire.*

He read that paragraph three times.

*You have, I think, been working alone. I recognise the shape of it in
your schedules — the second-guessing, the repeated controls, the
extraordinary care taken over the four-per-cent question, which is the
kind of care a person takes when there is nobody to tell him it does not
matter. I would say, without any wish to flatter you, that the work is
better than it has any business being and that this is precisely why
you cannot see where it is going.*

*I enclose nothing, ask nothing, and propose nothing.*

*I will observe only that the cross-medic result — seventy per cent, plus
or minus six, consistent across four trials — is not a confound in your
design. It is the finding. You have been treating it as noise around the
question you meant to ask, and it is a considerably larger question than
the one you meant to ask, and if you sit with it for a fortnight you
will see why.*

*If you should ever wish to correspond on the matter, the man who brings
the fish to the west market on the second and fourth Thursday of the
month will carry a letter and will not read it and does not know my
name.*

*You are under no obligation and I would think less of you if you
answered quickly.*

There was no signature.

---

He stood in the sluice for a considerable time.

The first thing he did was not what he expected. He did not think about
who had written it. He did not think about the fishmonger, or the
paper, or the fact that a foreign hand had put his job title on an
envelope.

He thought:

*It is the finding.*

*Not a confound. The finding.*

And he was four sentences into working out why — the patient's tissue
had learned Yagi's pattern over six repetitions, so what the stone
carried was not an instruction but *an instruction the body already
recognised*, which meant the seventy per cent was not a loss at all, it
was a measurement of how much of the effect had been living in the
patient the whole time and not in the crystal —

— when he stopped.

He put the letter face down on the bench.

*That took nine seconds.*

*Nine seconds. He wrote four lines about a result I have had on my desk
for fourteen months and could not do anything with, and I have solved a
piece of it standing in a sluice, and he did that on purpose, and he
did it in a letter that asks for nothing.*

Sazare Ruri, who had been read very accurately by a great many
institutions and had never once been read by a person, stood with both
hands flat on a sluice bench and understood exactly what had been done
to him.

*He has not offered me anything. He has offered to be interested.*

*And there is nobody in the world who has ever done that.*

---

He did four things in the following eleven minutes and he did all of
them correctly, and he was fairly proud of that afterwards and also
aware that being proud of it was not the point.

He did not put the letter in his coat.

He put it back in its envelope, and he went to the desk, and he wrote a
receipt slip in his own hand — *one sheet, one envelope, received
second-floor internal post, approx. 10.40, no return address* — and he
signed it and dated it and clipped it to the outside.

He went to Kaburagi's office. She was not in it. He did not leave it on
her desk.

He went to the admissions desk and told Emiko he needed her for four
minutes and did not explain in the corridor, and Emiko put the bed
state down and came without asking, which he had by then stopped being
surprised by and had not stopped noticing.

And then he stood in Sister Kaburagi's empty office with the ward's
charge nurse as a witness and wrote a second slip, longer, describing
the envelope, the title on it, the fact that the title did not exist
outside the building, and the eleven-minute interval between opening it
and reporting it — because the interval was going to be asked about, by
somebody, at some point, and he would rather it was in his own
handwriting than in an estimate.

"You're shaking," said Emiko.

"Yes."

"Do you want to sit down?"

"No. I want you to read both slips before I hand them over, because
you're going to be asked whether I wrote them in front of you, and I'd
like you to be able to say yes without having to think about it."

Emiko read both slips.

"Ruri," she said. "What's in the letter?"

Sazare looked at the envelope on the desk between them.

"Somebody's read my work," he said, "and understood it, and found the
thing in it I couldn't find, and told me, and asked for nothing."

"That's not an answer to what I asked."

"No," he agreed. "It's the answer to the dangerous part."
