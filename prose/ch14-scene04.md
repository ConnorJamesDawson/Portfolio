# Chapter 14, Scene 4 — The Fortnight

*Act 2, part D. The southern coast. Jirō, age fifteen. POV: Jirō. ~2,400
words.*

---

The village was called Ōura, eleven houses and a smoke-shed on a hard
little bay three days south of Shiranui, and the stone-mender had stopped
five weeks in it, and Jirō knew that before anyone said so, because the smoke-shed
door hung on a hinge that had been mended by somebody who loved the work,
and nothing else in Ōura had been mended by anybody at all.

The cover was the debtor. It was Rei's cover, paper-school to its bones,
and it worked because it was boring: three guards retained by a Shiranui
factor to trace a young man who'd skipped on an advance — apprentice
mender, quiet, scarred right forearm, might have worked his passage
through. Papers to show. A fee for trouble. Nobody on any coast alive was
surprised by a story about money.

The widow Ō-Yone had housed him. She was sixty-odd, and she smoked a
short pipe with the economy of a woman who had rationed her whole life,
and she stood in her doorway with her arms folded and answered Rei's
questions like a woman paying a toll: exactly, and no more.

Stayed five weeks, midsummer. Paid board weekly, in advance,
never owed a day. Mended the smoke-shed hinge, and her kettle, and the
church of small broken things the village brought him once it understood
what it had — she gestured with the pipe at half of Ōura in a single
economical arc. Kept to himself. Walked the tideline of an evening. Gone
in the second month, south, on a grain barge, and no, he'd left no word,
because none of her business, and none of theirs.

"He'd a debt on him, missus," said Rei, mild as milk.

"Then he'll pay it," said Ō-Yone. "That one settles up." And shut the
door, courteously, in their faces.

Jirō wrote: *subject stopped Ōura, 5 wks. Departed south by water,
eighth month. Local account consistent.* And underneath, in the box the
form kept for an assessor's remark, after a while, the pen not hurrying:
*Settles up.*

---

The doctrine was the fortnight.

You took the first account and you went away, and you let the village
talk itself out about you, and you came back after fifteen days with one
new question and all the old ones, and what you were reading the second
time was not the answers. It was the distance between the answers. A
rehearsed story comes back word-perfect, because the family has sat
round the table getting it straight. A true one comes back *worn* —
shorter here, blunter there, a detail lost, a detail surfacing — because
the truth is not a text, it is a place people are standing in, and
people shift their feet.

He had learned the doctrine at eleven, from the receiving end, at his
mother's table.

They came back to Ōura on the sixteenth day in rain, and Ō-Yone stood in
the same doorway with the same pipe and the same folded arms, and gave
the same toll — shorter here, blunter there; the kettle had dropped out
of the account and the tideline walks had gained a detail, that he'd
stand at the water an hour at a time, of an evening, looking east — and
Jirō stood behind Rei's shoulder in the wet with his hat brim down,
fifteen years old, listening to a woman say it straight and say it the
same, arms folded against people with papers and a fee for trouble, and
he was eleven, and the door was his own door, and the voice was his
mother's voice to the width of a grain: *I fed him. He went up and down
to the Academy with my son. That's the whole of it.*

*They come back after a fortnight and they ask you the same questions
and they see if you say it different.*

He had it now, the whole shape of it, from both sides at once, and
there was nowhere to put it, so he put it where everything went — in
order — and heard himself asking, in the pause Rei left him, the one
new question, the one the doctrine required, in a voice that came out
of him level and professional and belonged to somebody he was still
getting used to:

"The evenings he stood at the water, missus. Did he ever say what he
was looking at?"

Ō-Yone took the pipe out of her mouth and looked at him — at him, not
at Rei; at the age of him under the hat brim, the way the old ones
always found the age — for longer than the question was worth.

"East," she said. "I told you. There's nothing out there but weather
and the island trade." A draw on the pipe. "He wasn't looking *at*,
son. He was looking *from*. You'll know the difference yourself, I'd
say, or you will."

They walked the three miles back to the road-head in rain. Rei was
pleased; consistency closed the account; Ōura went in the file as
*exhausted, no further value* — the phrase the paper school used for
*leave these people alone now*, and the one mercy the whole apparatus
had built into itself, and Jirō wrote it with more care than the form
strictly wanted, because he knew, in units of laundry contracts,
precisely what the phrase was worth to eleven houses and a smoke-shed.

Kōga spoke that night at the fire, which happened seldom enough that
both of them stopped chewing.

"He leaves them better," said Kōga, to the flames, in his slow
unhurried gravel. "Every stop on this coast, he leaves it better than
he found it. Hinges. Kettles. Dowries." A long pause, wood settling.
"That is how we are walking a cold trail warm, and he cannot stop
doing it, because it is not tradecraft to him, it is what he is." Kōga
turned the spit a quarter-turn. "Kindness has a gait, boys. You can
track it like anything else that walks. It will take him somewhere he
can be got at, and we will be a fortnight behind it when it does."

Rei nodded, professionally satisfied, and made a note.

Jirō sat with his tea and his face in order, and hated the fire, and
the rain, and the trade, and the truth of it — because it *was* true,
it was the best piece of tracking doctrine he'd heard spoken aloud on
the whole detail, and it was also, said otherwise, this: the only
thing in three years that has made your friend findable is that
nothing they did to him has managed to stop him being kind. That is
the trail. You are following the kindness. You, of all the people
they could have sent.

*Don't round up,* he told the fire, uselessly. The fire had nothing
to report.

---

The trace freshened all down that coast like weather coming.

At Ōura it had been five weeks old; at the grain port of Sakihama,
where a wharfinger remembered a young hand off a barge who'd re-set a
snapped block-pulley in the time it took to argue about it, three
weeks; at the fish camps on the long spit, where somebody's split
knuckles had been cleaned and strapped *proper, like a barracks
medic does it* — Jirō wrote that phrase down twice, once in the
ledger and once somewhere else — nine days.

Nine days. Southern axis. The files' three debt-grade deserters had
been quietly returned to the folder's back pocket; nobody had
mentioned them for a month. In the evenings now Rei read the coast
charts south — the long broken country of inlets and ferry-stages
that ran down toward the border straits and, beyond them, the
scatter of poor islands and poorer causeways that the maps called
the Land of Waves and the traders called *the thin coast* — and
marked ferry-stages in his small exact hand, and Kōga oiled what
Kōga oiled.

And on the last night before the border towns, Rei opened the
kit-roll and set Jirō's mask on his knees, white, wave-marked, the
eye-slits making nothing smaller yet because it was face-up on his
lap looking at the tent roof.

"South of the straits we're not merchants' guards," Rei said. "Past
the border there's no residency to offend and no treaty worth the
candle — it's retrieval ground, and we work it as what we are.
You've not worn it operational." It was not a question. "First time
it goes on for true, something happens to the range of things you
find you can do. Every man it happens to thinks he's the first.
I'm telling you now so that when you feel it, you'll know it's not
you — it's the mask, it's *built* to do that, the village has been
building that exact thing for two hundred years." He turned in his
blanket, ending it. "Sleep."

Jirō sat up a while with the fire dying and the mask on his knees.

Three years. He'd be tall now. He'd have the scar under his sleeve
and a barge-hand's shoulders and a way of standing at the water of
an evening, looking from; and somewhere down that thin coast he was
mending his way south at nine days' distance, leaving a trail of
better hinges, and the finest tracer of his intake was coming down
the same coast behind him carrying a white face in a kit-roll and a
flag that said *alive, undamaged, premium*, and an unfinished
sentence of his mother's, folded corners-in, three years old:

*You bring him to me before you bring him to them. I'll feed him,
and I'll look at him, and then—*

He packed the mask.

"It gets lighter," the storesman had said.

It had not, so far, got lighter.
