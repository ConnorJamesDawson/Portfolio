# Chapter 26, Scene 3 — The Knee

*Act 4, part B. Konoha General. POV: Nurse Yagi. ~1,590 words.*

---

Nurse Yagi had known Sazare Ruri for four months and had never seen him
entirely still.

Even asleep on the sluice table after a late control exercise, one hand closed
periodically as though smoothing something too small to see. Under anaesthetic
the fingers stopped. The absence made him look younger and much more seriously
ill.

"Chakra exhaustion," said Dr Fujita. "Severe, not terminal. Dehydration. Mild
concussion. Two cracked ribs. Shoulder subluxation reduced itself. Left knee
is the interesting part."

"Nothing about this is interesting."

"You brought me a sixteen-year-old with a nine-year-old reconstruction
performed by somebody using wire."

Yagi looked through the viewing seal at the joint. The chakra image showed
bone in pale bands, soft tissue as moving grey and foreign material as three
bright, straight lines beside the kneecap.

"That's wire?"

"Was. Mostly mineral deposition now. The original injury took part of the
medial ligament off the bone. Somebody put it back under field conditions,
anchored the patella and got the skin closed. Not elegant. Effective enough to
keep a seven-year-old walking."

"He said a woman in Kiri."

"A woman in Kiri did good work with bad tools. Then nobody reviewed it for
nine years."

The new injury had torn through the old repair. One wire anchor had pulled
free. Scar tissue crossed the joint in thick diagonal bands, testimony to
every year Ruri had forced it through a movement it could not make correctly.
The cartilage beneath the kneecap had worn unevenly. The muscles on the
outside of the thigh were overdeveloped; those on the inside had learned not
to be trusted.

Yagi had watched him compensate without naming it. He turned his whole body
instead of pivoting over the left foot. He used the right leg first on stairs
and made the difference look like habit. When lifting patients, he placed the
left foot half a pace forward so the knee never bent under the load. None of
it had appeared dramatic enough to interrupt a ten-hour shift. Hospitals were
full of people working around old damage.

That was the excuse. The less comfortable fact was that he had presented them
with eleven deliberate cuts on his forearm and asked to learn medical control,
and nobody had asked what else hurt.

"Was this visible on admission?" Fujita asked.

"He wasn't admitted as a patient."

"During the amnesty examination."

"They assessed fitness and threat. He walked."

Fujita adjusted the viewing seal. "People do."

"We stabilise and wait," Yagi said. "He can consent when he wakes."

Fujita was already washing his hands. "We stabilise only, the free anchor
continues cutting the ligament. We repair the fresh tear without correcting
the old alignment, we fix him in the position that caused it."

"He didn't consent to reconstruction."

"He presented unconscious during a mass-casualty event with an unstable joint
and internal foreign body. Standard is restoration of function where
practical. It is practical now. It will not be after swelling sets."

"He didn't present. He fell over while working."

"Put that on the form."

There were fourteen people waiting for Fujita and three who could not wait.
The operating room had lost one window and gained two cracks in the western
wall. Every steriliser tray was in use. Outside, something large enough to
alter the light moved beyond the village and nobody in the room looked towards
it.

Yagi looked at the boy's knee again.

"Do it," she said.

They used local chakra anaesthesia despite his unconsciousness because
chakra-exhausted patients sometimes woke badly. Fujita opened along the old
scar. Beneath it he found the work of a medic whose name nobody knew: three
neat holes drilled by hand, wire twisted with consistent turns, a strip of
tendon taken from somewhere else and used to replace what had been lost. One
anchor had corroded. One had migrated. The third remained exactly where it had
been placed.

"Nine years," Fujita said.

"You've said."

"I'm admiring it."

He removed the wire, trimmed scar tissue and repaired the current tear with
green medical chakra concentrated to a line finer than thread. Yagi held the
kneecap in correct tracking while he rebuilt the damaged surface beneath it.
The work took forty-three minutes. Twice they stopped to receive emergency
cases in the adjacent room. Once the floor moved and plaster came out of the
ceiling vent.

During the first stop, an orderly brought in a chūnin with a chest wound and
no pulse. Fujita closed Ruri's incision under a sterile chakra film, crossed
the room and worked for six minutes before calling the time. During the
second, they gave their remaining prepared anaesthetic to a girl whose arm had
been crushed below the elbow. Ruri received none because he was already
unconscious and because resources observed no fairness except sequence.

Between interruptions, the sounds from outside altered. Isolated explosions
gave way to whistles, shouted sector numbers and the repeated clatter of
stretchers through the rear entrance. Organised response, late and
unmistakable. The hospital staff heard the village regaining shape through
walls they could not leave.

Yagi held the patella while Fujita laid the final repair. Medical chakra
illuminated the old drilling scars, the new green join and the narrow place
where cartilage would have to regrow rather than merely close. She adjusted by
fractions until the joint moved in one plane.

"There," Fujita said.

It was an ordinary word for nine years of pain ending while the patient knew
nothing about it.

When Fujita tested the joint at the end, the lower leg bent without shifting
sideways.

Yagi had never seen Ruri's do that.

"He'll need six weeks," she said.

"For combat."

"He doesn't do combat."

From outside came the sound of people lifting bodies off crystal.

Fujita looked at her.

"Six weeks," Yagi said.

They splinted the leg straight and moved him to the men's end because there
was nowhere else. Bed two had been discharged that morning to attend the
finals and had returned with a broken wrist, but he was ambulatory and lost
the argument. Handa watched from bed eleven while Yagi and an orderly
transferred Ruri.

"Window held," Handa said.

Yagi tucked the blanket beneath the splint. "Apparently."

"He went out because of us?"

"He went out because somebody was lying in the road."

"And stayed?"

Yagi looked at Ruri's hands. Dried blood filled the lines of the right palm.
The left sleeve had been cut away with the crystal plate, which now sat in a
washbasin on the floor because nobody had found a container capable of holding
it. Even unconscious, the skin of both hands showed small chakra burns: red
branching marks from the heel to each fingertip.

"He came back," she said.

At noon a Hyūga woman arrived with a sealed evidence case and asked whether
the patient could answer questions.

"No."

"When will he?"

"When his chakra system decides to speak to the rest of him."

"Estimate."

"Tomorrow. Possibly the day after."

The woman looked towards the basin. "Is that the forearm structure?"

"It was attached to the forearm structure."

"May I take it?"

"No."

"It is material evidence in an active security investigation."

"It is also a medical restraint he grew through his clothing while somebody
was stabbing him. We removed it from a patient. It stays until he wakes and
tells us whether taking it will do anything to him."

The Hyūga considered arguing. Yagi allowed her face to explain the current
institutional balance.

"I will seal the room," the woman said.

"You will not seal a ward."

"The basin."

"You may seal the basin."

The woman applied four paper tags to the rim without touching the crystal.
Through the clear face of the evidence case Yagi saw blue-grey fragments, each
wrapped separately and numbered. There were more than sixty.

"What did you see?" Yagi asked.

The Hyūga finished the fourth tag before answering. "His chakra left through
one hundred and eight pathways. Some divided after leaving him."

"Is that possible?"

"It occurred."

"That wasn't what I asked."

The woman looked at Ruri. "Not safely. The pathways in both arms show
abrasion. There was leakage through the chest and throat. He maintained
exclusions inside the network."

"Exclusions?"

"Places he prevented from responding. The hospital steps. Three friendly
personnel. A body whose status he could not have known. When one of them
moved, the exclusion moved."

Yagi understood anatomy better than chakra geometry, but she understood
attention. "While controlling the rest?"

"Yes."

"How many people did he kill?"

"We have not finished separating cause. More than twenty."

The Hyūga said it without admiration. That made the number worse.

"Could he do it again?"

"Not today."

"That wasn't what I asked either."

For the first time, uncertainty entered the woman's face. "I don't know."

"How many?" Handa asked after the woman left.

"Fragments?"

"People."

Yagi did not ask how he knew there was a number. Everybody in the building
knew. It had entered with the casualties and passed from bed to bed ahead of
any official account.

"Nobody has finished counting," she said.

"Twenty?"

"More."

Handa looked towards the repaired window. The bottom corner of the glass had a
white star in it from the resonant strike, but the sash remained shut.

"He'll hate that," he said.

"The window?"

"Being talked about."

"Yes."

"What will you tell him?"

Yagi checked Ruri's pulse. Fifty-four, regular, stronger than it had been in
treatment.

"That he is not to put weight on the leg," she said.

"Will he listen?"

"No."

"Then?"

She raised the bed rail on both sides.

"We adapt."
