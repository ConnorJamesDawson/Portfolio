# Chapter 32, Scene 4 — The Slate

*Act 4, part C. Konoha, the east side. Sazare Ruri, age seventeen.
POV: Sazare. ~1,385 words.*

---

Ōtaki Sue's room was directly beneath his and had been for eighteen
months and he had been in it perhaps ninety times, and it was the only
room in the Land of Fire he had ever entered without first working out
where he would put crystal.

It was nine feet by eleven. One window onto the same tiled roof his own
looked over, though from a floor lower and at a worse angle, so she got
about two hours of direct light in the afternoon and had arranged the
entire room around them: the low table under the window, the chair
turned to face it, a mirror on the opposite wall hung at a height that
made no sense for a woman of four feet eleven until you worked out that
it was angled to throw the light back onto the table.

She was seventy-two now. The wrapper was the same grey cotton, worn
white at the elbows, and she had a second one exactly like it that she
alternated, and Sazare had established over twelve months that both
had been made from the same bolt at the same time by the same person
and that the person had been her.

She moved slowly and without any hesitation at all, which is a
combination most people her age do not manage; they get slow and
careful in the same season. She had got slow and stayed certain. She
put her feet down flat and deliberately, and she never once looked at
the floor.

Her hands were spotted and thin across the back and the knuckles had
gone, and she wrote with the pen held in a grip he had only ever seen
in her, thumb and two fingers with the third tucked under, which he
suspected was a workaround for something that had stopped bending
about a decade ago and which she had never mentioned.

She had the kettle on before he had got the slate off the hook.

---

*I HAVE A PROBLEM AND I CANNOT SAY IT OUT LOUD*, he wrote.

Ōtaki Sue read it. Then she took the chalk out of his hand and wrote
underneath, in her fast square hand:

*THEN YOU ARE IN THE RIGHT ROOM*

He laughed, which he did perhaps four times a year, and she saw it
happen and looked extremely pleased with herself and made a *go on*
gesture with the chalk.

It took him eleven minutes to write it out, in pieces, wiping the slate
four times.

*A MAN TOLD ME TO SAY ONE TRUE GOOD THING TO ONE PERSON*

*I HAVE TRIED FOUR TIMES*

*EVERY TIME I DESCRIBE AN OBJECT*

*I CANNOT GET TO THE PART THAT IS ABOUT THEM*

She read the last one for longer than the others.

Then she wrote: *WHY NOT*

*I DO NOT KNOW*

*YES YOU DO*

He looked at that for a while.

*IT IS NOT USEFUL*, he wrote. *A FAULT IS USEFUL. YOU CAN DO SOMETHING
WITH A FAULT. IF I TELL SOMEBODY A THING IS GOOD THEY CANNOT DO
ANYTHING WITH IT*

Ōtaki Sue read it, and put the chalk down, and went and poured the tea,
which took her about ninety seconds, and Sazare sat on the floor by the
low table and understood that he had just been told his answer was not
good enough by a woman who had not said a word.

She came back, sat, and picked the chalk up again.

*THAT IS THE SECOND REASON*, she wrote. *WHAT IS THE FIRST*

---

He was there for another forty minutes and she got it out of him in
about six exchanges, which he did not appreciate at the time and
thought about a great deal afterwards, because she did it entirely
without asking a single question about his feelings.

*IF I SAY A THING IS GOOD I AM SAYING I LIKE IT*, he wrote, eventually.

*YES*

*THAT IS INFORMATION ABOUT ME*

*YES*

He stopped.

Ōtaki Sue waited with the chalk in her hand and the enormous patience
of somebody who had spent sixty-three years waiting for people to
finish writing.

*I HAVE SPENT MY WHOLE LIFE MAKING SURE NOBODY HAS INFORMATION ABOUT
WHAT I WANT*, he wrote.

He looked at it on the slate.

It was, he thought, the truest sentence he had ever put down anywhere,
and he had put it in chalk, in a room with one other person in it, who
could not hear him say it and would not be able to repeat the tone of
it to anybody because there had not been one.

*BECAUSE OF THE PEOPLE IN YOUR COUNTRY*, she wrote.

*PARTLY*

*AND*

*AND MY SISTER NEVER ASKED ME FOR ANYTHING SHE TOOK IT AND I WOULD
HAVE GIVEN HER ALL OF IT IF SHE HAD ASKED AND SHE NEVER ASKED AND THAT
IS THE WHOLE OF WHAT WAS WRONG*

He wiped it before she had finished reading it, which was the first
time he had ever done that, and she reached out and stopped his hand
and read the rest of it and then let go.

*SO*, she wrote. *YOU LEARNED THAT WANTING IS DANGEROUS*

*YES*

*AND A COMPLIMENT IS A SMALL PIECE OF WANTING*

*YES*

*GIVEN AWAY FOR NOTHING WITH NO WAY TO GET IT BACK*

*YES*

Ōtaki Sue nodded slowly, and wrote one word, and underlined it twice:

*GOOD*

---

*WHY GOOD*, he wrote.

She turned the slate round to her side of the table and wrote for
considerably longer than she had written anything all evening, and when
she turned it back it was full, corner to corner, in that square
controlled hand.

*I HAVE BEEN DEAF SINCE I WAS 9. I AM 72. THAT IS 63 YEARS*

*IN 63 YEARS NOBODY HAS EVER GIVEN ME A COMPLIMENT*

*NOT BECAUSE THEY DID NOT THINK THEM. MY HUSBAND THOUGHT THEM. I
KNOW HE DID*

*BUT A COMPLIMENT IS A THING PEOPLE SAY QUICKLY WHEN IT OCCURS TO
THEM AND THEN IT IS GONE. NOBODY STOPS AND FINDS A SLATE*

*IT IS TOO SMALL A THING TO BE WORTH THE TROUBLE. SO IN 63 YEARS
EVERY SINGLE ONE OF THEM HAS BEEN THROWN AWAY BEFORE IT REACHED ME*

Sazare read it twice.

He had come down those stairs ninety times. He had mended her window
catch four times because she opened it with her foot. He had mended the
stair nosing and the mangle and the kettle handle and had never once,
in twelve months, written her a single word that was not either
practical or a joke.

He took the chalk.

He had four attempts on the way to it and wiped three, and the fourth
one he wrote slowly, in his mother's clerk hand, and he had to stop
twice in the middle of it and did not wipe it either time.

*YOUR HANDWRITING IS THE BEST I HAVE EVER SEEN.*

*I WAS TAUGHT BY A WOMAN WHO MADE ME COPY STOCK LISTS UNTIL I CRIED
AND I HAVE SEEN THE HANDS OF CLERKS IN FOUR COUNTRIES AND YOURS IS
BETTER THAN ALL OF THEM.*

*IT IS THE FIRST THING I NOTICED ABOUT YOU AND I HAVE THOUGHT IT
EVERY TIME FOR EIGHTEEN MONTHS AND I HAVE NEVER SAID IT BECAUSE I DID
NOT KNOW THAT WAS A THING PEOPLE DID.*

He turned the slate round.

Ōtaki Sue read it.

She read it twice, and then she put one hand flat on the table beside
it, and did not write anything at all for a considerable time, and
Sazare sat on the floor of a room on the second floor of a block on the
east side and watched a seventy-two-year-old woman receive the first
compliment of her adult life, in chalk, from a foreign boy who had
needed five weeks and four failures and an old man in a training ground
to get there.

Then she wrote, underneath it:

*I AM NOT WIPING THIS OFF*

She did not, either. She bought a new slate the following week for
ordinary use and that one went up on the wall beside the mirror, where
the afternoon light hit it, and it was still there eleven years later
when Sazare came back to that block for the last time and found the
room emptied and the slate left deliberately on the sill.
