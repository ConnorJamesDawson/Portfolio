# Chapter 10, Scene 2 — What the Arm Said

*Act 2, part B. Tōan, Yumoto. POV: Tōan. ~2,400 words.*

---

Tōan had been reading bodies for thirty-four years and the boy's was a short
book in a bad hand.

He did the proper assessment in the fifth week, when the rice had had time to
say its piece — you could not read a starved body accurately any more than you
could survey a house in a flood — and he did it in the morning, in good light,
with the stove hot, and he talked the whole way through it in the flat
inventory voice that frightened nobody because it had nothing in it to catch
on.

Malnutrition, long, and layered: a first hard season perhaps a year and a half
back, a partial recovery, then a second season worse than the first. It was
written where it was always written, in the teeth and the hair and the wrists,
and in the growth he hadn't done — the boy was thirteen and built like a
careful eleven. That would partly come back. Some of it would not; some of it
was height and breadth the lad had simply been billed for in a year when
nobody could pay, and the bill did not come round twice.

The shoulder: dislocated, reduced late and badly — against a wall, Anzu's bird
had said, and the joint agreed — then used for a year because there had been no
other option on offer. It had taught itself a wrong way to sit and would need
to be untaught, which was slow, dull, painful work, the kind patients abandoned
in the fourth week when the novelty wore off. He did not think this one would
abandon it. That was a separate concern.

Then the forearm, and Tōan stopped talking.

He turned it palm-up on the cloth and looked at it for a long time, and did
what he always did with a wound that had a story it wasn't telling: he shut
his eyes and read it with his hands and let it say its piece in its own order.

Three exits. No entries.

He had assisted on blast wounds, spar-splinter wounds off the boats, an
unpleasant business years back with a swordsmith's failed billet — things that
went *into* an arm and out the far side. This was not that. Nothing had gone
into this arm. Everything had come *out* of it: out through the palm, out
through the back of the hand, out along the flat of the forearm in a long
split seam — outward, all of it, from a point of origin somewhere around the
middle of the palm, from *inside a hand the size of a girl's*.

The bone had channels in it. He read them twice to be sure. Grooves, healed
glass-smooth, as though something had run *along* the bone on its way out and
polished as it went.

He knew what Shōton was. Everyone of his generation from that country knew
what the Ruri were, roughly, the way you knew what any of the old trades were:
jewellers' crystal, quartermasters' stock, a small clan out of the lower
district that kept to its work. He had carried one of their pieces himself for
nine years, set in the butt of a field kit, a little blue lozenge with a
preserving mark on it, and never thought about it once.

So he sat in the good light with the arm in his hands and did the sum the
wound demanded: somewhere, at some point about fourteen months ago, this child
had grown crystal *out of his own body* — no frame, no medium, no four hours;
out of the blood and the bone, at speed, and the speed was written in the
scarring the way a hurry is always written — and the arm had been the price
of it, and nobody had sent word about *that*, which meant it was a thing that
was not sent word about.

Fourteen months ago.

He was a careful man and he made himself finish the arithmetic he had been
declining to start since Anzu's bird came. Fourteen months back, in the Land of
Water, in the fifth month — the boats had brought it up the coast the way the
boats brought everything. A public sentencing in the Kirigakure fish market. An
interruption. A crystal spike eleven yards long, through a sworn executioner,
in front of four hundred people. Nine carried out regardless. The family's
name had travelled with the story, because that was the point of stories like
it.

The boy had been in the room next door for five weeks, splitting wood
one-handed, banking half his rice.

"Right," said Tōan, to the arm. He put it down gently on the cloth. "We'll
start Monday. It'll hurt, and it'll be dull, and it'll be dull and hurt for
about a year, and at the end of it the hand works. That's the trade. I'm not
going to sell it to you better than that."

The boy said: "It already doesn't work. So it's a good trade."

There was, occasionally, a sentence out of that child that told you everything
about the household he'd been made in. Tradesmen's arithmetic, no self-pity in
it anywhere, and underneath it — you had to know where to listen — a bottom
like bedrock. Tōan had treated soldiers of forty who could not have said *it
already doesn't work* in that tone about their own ruin.

"Monday," he said, and rolled the cloth up, and did not say any of the rest.

---

The rest he carried out to the woodpile that evening and turned over while he
squared the logs the boy had split askew, which he did after dark so the boy
would not see it done.

He had left that country twenty-two years ago over a floor.

He did not think of it oftener than monthly now, which he counted a mercy. He
had been thirty-one, barracks-trained, nine years qualified, and his posting
every spring was the same posting: the long room off the Academy hall with the
drains in the floor, and the row of pallets, and the wait. The graduating
class went in. The doors were barred. And his whole trade, everything the
green thread was for, waited outside on the pallets for what came out.

Some years, eight came out. Some years, fourteen. You patched what could be
patched. He had been very good. He had hated it with a hatred so complete and
so useless that it had long stopped feeling like anything at all, and he had
gone on being very good, spring after spring, because the ones who came out
deserved a very good medic more, not less, for what had been done to them —
and that reasoning had held him nine years, and he was aware it holds most
people most of their lives, in one uniform or another.

Then came the spring when the doors opened and nothing came out to him at
all.

One boy, walking, unmarked, who did not stop at the pallets and did not need
him and looked through him and the drains and the whole arrangement as though
it were weather. And behind that boy, a room in which Tōan's entire year of
work lay finished before it began.

He had stood in the doorway of that room with his kit in his hand, a
green-thread man, a mender, and understood with total clarity that he was not
outside the machine, patching what it broke. He was a component. He was the
part that kept the machine respectable — proof, standing on the pallets every
spring, that *care was taken*. The drains and he were fixtures of the same
room, and the drains were the honester fixture.

He had finished the season, because even that spring there were other rooms
and other work. Then he had taken his kit and his pension credit and walked
up the coast and over the strait, and found a wet green country that leaked
hot water and had no hidden village and no bloodline registry and no long
room with drains, and he had been mending miners in it for twenty-two years,
and the boy who walked out of that door had gone on to whatever such boys go
on to, and Tōan had never learned what and never asked the boats.

And now the same machine, a generation on — reformed, everyone said; the exam
was long abolished, everyone said — had sent him up the inland road a
thirteen-year-old with channels polished into the bones of his arm, and
Tōan was going to do what he did, which was the only thing he had ever done:
stand between a body and its own speed, and say *not yet*, and mend what
came out of the door.

He squared the last log and stood a while in the cold.

Through the wall, faintly, the boy cried out in his sleep — the third hour;
it was always the third hour — one word, and then caught himself even
sleeping, and went quiet the way trained things go quiet.

The word was *third*. It was always *third*. Tōan had two guesses at what it
was counting and hoped, in the way a man hopes about weather, that he was
wrong about both.

He went in, and lit the lamp on the compounding bench, and got the good
scales down, and began measuring out valerian and dried hop against an order
that no one in the village had placed, tapping the spoon on the jar rim a
little more often than the work required, until the house on the other side
of the wall breathed slow again.

Monday, he had said.

On the Monday they began.
