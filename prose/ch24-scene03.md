# Chapter 24, Scene 3 — Twelve Grains

*Act 4, part A. Konoha, the room over the persimmon tree. Sazare Ruri,
age sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,900 words.*

---

He built the bench first, because a dead man's advice is worth more than a
living one's and Handa Rokusuke was not dead but had put it in the same
register.

Thirty inches. Vice on the right. He made it in nine evenings out of scrap
from the yard behind the hospital laundry and two lengths of decent stuff
Handa's wife brought round in a handcart in the sixth month with a note in
it, and it was the ugliest piece of joinery in the Land of Fire and it did
not rack when he leaned on it, and he had it for eleven years.

Then he started on the seeds.

---

*Growth is a race between arrival and arrangement.*

He had that sentence on a requisition form in his coat and had read it
about four hundred times, and the whole of the next eleven weeks came out
of it, and the reasoning went like this and he wrote all of it down.

*Everything I grow under pressure comes out with a plane in it, because
the material arrives faster than it can find its place, and it lands
wherever it lands, and it lands wrong in the same way all across one layer
because it is all arriving at once from one direction — from me.*

*So the piece has a fault running through it before I have finished making
it, and when somebody hits it, it does not break where they hit it. It
parts along the fault. Every time. In four seconds.*

*I cannot make the material arrive more slowly, because the entire problem
is that I have four seconds.*

*Therefore I have to make the arrangement faster.*

*And you cannot make arrangement faster. Arrangement is the material
finding its place, and it takes as long as it takes.*

He sat with that for nine days and got nowhere at all, and then went back
to the gemstone volume with the hand-coloured plates and read the page and
a half about the eastern provinces again — the growers who made large
single crystals for lens work, patiently, over months, in tanks — and the
sentence was not even a sentence, it was a subordinate clause:

*...the process being commenced, as always, from a seed of the same
material, previously formed and of good habit, whose arrangement the
accruing substance adopts...*

*Whose arrangement the accruing substance adopts.*

Sazare Ruri stood up in the second room of a village library so fast that
he knocked a stool over, and Ochiai said "*Oi*" from the front desk
without looking up.

---

*It does not have to arrange itself.*

*That is the whole of it. That is what I have been getting wrong since I
was seven years old, and my mother got it wrong, and her mother, and four
hundred years of us, and it is not a failure of skill, it is a failure of*
starting point.

*Every single thing I have ever grown, I have grown from* nothing. *Out of
a sand tray, out of render, out of wet clay, out of my own palm on a kerb
in the Land of Waves — the material arrives at an empty place and has to
work out where to go while it is going there, and under load it has about
a second to do it in and it does it badly.*

*But if there is already something there — a piece, small, made properly,
made* slowly, *with no fault in it at all — then the arriving material has
nothing to work out. It copies. It lays itself down on a pattern that is
already correct and it does it at whatever speed I give it, because
copying is not thinking.*

*I cannot make arrangement faster.*

*I can make it* unnecessary.

---

The seeds took him eleven weeks and they were, without any question, the
finest work he ever did in his life, and every one of them was made to be
destroyed.

He grew them at the bench, at night, after ten-hour shifts, on a scrap of
dressed granite under the window, and the rule he set himself in the first
week and never once broke was: **as slowly as I can bear.**

Not slowly. As slowly as he *could bear*, which turned out to be a
different and much harder specification, because a grain of crystal the
size of a grain of rice can be grown in about four seconds and he was
giving them ninety minutes each.

Ninety minutes. For one grain. Sitting absolutely still with two fingers
on a piece of granite and the feed held so low and so level that for the
first three weeks he kept losing it entirely and having to start again,
because there is a floor below which a thing you are doing stops being a
thing you are doing and becomes nothing at all, and he had never in his
life had to find that floor.

The still water did it. He worked that out in the second week and it made
him laugh out loud alone in a room: eleven hours in a culvert at fifteen
with a professional four yards away, seven minutes in front of an examiner
at ten, three hours in a drainage cutting — the entire useless
distinguished catalogue of a boy who had been extremely good at holding a
bowl of water absolutely still and had never once been told what it was
for.

*It was for this.*

He made forty-one and kept twelve.

The other twenty-nine went in the failure tin, and every one of them was
better than anything he had grown for money in two and a half years, and
he weighed them and entered them and let Kanzaki Uta take them away.

The twelve he kept were each about the size of a grain of rice, colourless
rather than blue-grey, and so clear that on the ninth week he put one on a
printed page in the lamplight and read the type through it.

He tapped them with a chopstick, all twelve, at the bench, one at a time,
with the window shut.

Not one of them made a sound.

---

He tested it in the eleventh week and he was extremely careful about how,
and he thought about that for four days beforehand and wrote his
reasoning down, because he had a conditions document with five clauses on
it and one of them said *no combat training of any kind, with any person,
in any discipline*.

*I am not going to strike anything. I am not going to have anybody strike
anything. I am not going to do it fast, standing up, or under anything
that could be described as a drill.*

*I am going to do exactly what a fabricator does, which is to grow a
sample and load it in a vice until it fails, and write down the number,
because that is a materials test and Kanzaki does about nine a week and
there is a printed form for it.*

*And I am going to enter all of it in the book.*

He borrowed the small engineer's vice off the hospital maintenance store
with a chit, and a spring balance, and did it on the bench under the
window on a Sunday in the seventh month with the persimmon in full leaf.

**Control sample.** Grown from nothing, fast, the way he had grown a bar
in the mouth of a culvert at Tomari and a lip along eight feet of
unparapeted kerb at the Great Naruto Bridge: a bar four inches by half an
inch, out of the granite, in about a second. He put it across the vice
jaws and wound it down and watched the balance.

It went at eleven pounds and it went the way it always went — all at once,
along its own plane, with a sound like a dropped cup, into two pieces and
a scatter of grit.

He wrote it down.

**Test sample.** He set a seed on the granite, and put two fingers on it,
and grew the same bar off it, in about the same second.

It came out looking identical. He could not tell them apart on the bench
and never could afterwards, which he came in time to think was the most
important single fact about the entire discipline.

He wound the vice down.

It went at nineteen pounds.

And it did not go all at once. That was the part he sat with for about a
quarter of an hour afterwards with his hands flat on the bench: it
*cracked* — a short dull tick, at fourteen pounds, nothing like a dropped
cup — and it held, visibly damaged, for another five turns of the handle,
and then failed in three pieces rather than into grit.

Sazare Ruri sat in a rented room in Konoha at seventeen minutes past the
ninth hour on a Sunday and understood that he had, at sixteen years and
seven months old, alone, with a library card and a vice and eleven weeks
of ninety-minute nights, moved the number.

---

He did the other test on the Tuesday and it took him four seconds and he
already knew the answer.

He grew a bar from nothing on the bench, in a second, and put his palm flat
on it and held it and counted.

Four. It went at four, the way it had gone at four in a rope-walk in
Tsuzura and on a bench above a river and in a shed in front of four
strangers and a mask.

Then he set a seed, and grew the same bar off it, and put his palm on it
and counted.

*One.*

*Two.*

*Three.*

*Four.*

The tick came at four — he *felt* it, up through the palm, the same small
hard sound in the same place — and he braced for the rest of it and the
rest of it did not come.

*Five.*

*Six.*

It went at six.

Sazare sat on the floor of his room with grit on his hands and his back
against the bench and laughed until he had to stop because the woman
downstairs banged on her ceiling.

---

Six seconds.

He wrote the sober version down the following morning, underneath the
delighted one, because he had a rule about auditing the flattering half of
a ledger. It came out shorter than he expected and it did not say what he
had assumed it was going to say.

*It is not a technique and it is not power. It does nothing for size,
speed, reach or range, and nothing at all to anybody standing in front of
me. A man who wanted me dead in seven seconds still gets me in seven.*

*What it does is: a thing I put up stays up for six.*

*And it costs a grain, and a grain costs ninety minutes, and I have
twelve. So today I am fifty per cent harder to kill, twelve times, and
then I am back to four until I have spent twelve more nights.*

He got as far as writing *today* and then stopped with the pencil on the
paper, because he had put that word in without deciding to and it had
changed the sentence underneath it.

He sat back.

*Nine years.*

*Nine years of benches. The lattice, the frames, the tray, the flat, the
grinding, the throws, the recall, the two-inch shank in a rope-walk — nine
years of getting better at everything I do with the four seconds, and not
once, in any of it, at any hour, on any bench, in any country, did the
four seconds move.*

*It moved on Sunday.*

*It moved because of a grain of stone the size of a rice-corn, made by a
method I got out of a book for fifteen-year-olds, in a room nobody has
ever heard of, in eleven weeks.*

He looked at the twelve lines of it for a long time and then wrote the
part he was actually frightened of, which was not the shortage.

*I don't know what the number is.*

*I know four. I know six. I do not know six of what — six because a seed
has more courses in it than I can lay under load, or six because *this*
seed does, or six because this is as much as ninety minutes buys. I have
one seed design. I have made forty-one and kept twelve and they are all
the same design, because I did not know enough to vary anything, and the
first one that worked I copied forty times.*

*A seed can be grown off a seed. I have not tried it.*

*A seed does not have to die in the wreckage. It dies because I put it in
the middle. I have not tried the corner.*

*A grain takes ninety minutes because I sit still for ninety minutes. It
does not follow that ninety minutes is what it takes.*

He put the pencil down.

*Four seconds is a fortune,* said a ruined ronin, on a fence, in the
steam, at Yumoto. *Wars have been bought with less.*

*It's six,* thought Sazare Ruri, at a low bench under a window in the Land
of Fire, in the seventh month, at sixteen years and seven months old.

*And I am not going to find out where it stops by sitting here guessing,
and I have got the rest of my life, and there is a building four streets
away with forty books in it and a man on the desk who lets me take three
at a time.*

---

Kanzaki Uta looked at the vice test on the Thursday for about ninety
seconds without speaking.

He had written it up properly — the control, the sample, the balance
figures, the seed weights, the ninety minutes, all of it, on a materials
test form he had asked her for and which she had given him without
comment three weeks earlier.

"Nineteen pounds," she said.

"Nineteen."

"Off eleven."

"Off eleven, and it doesn't go into grit any more, it goes into pieces,
which for your purposes is—"

"For my purposes," said Kanzaki Uta, "it means a seal blank that gets sat
on in a pack does not become a bag of sand." She put the form down. "How
many of these seeds have you got?"

"Twelve."

"How long does one take?"

"Ninety minutes and I can do two a night before my hands go."

"So a blank made this way costs me an extra ninety minutes of you and a
grain I can't have back."

"Yes."

Kanzaki pushed her glasses up onto her forehead and did a piece of
arithmetic that Sazare could watch happening.

"Then you'll do it for the ones that matter and not for the rest," she
said, "and I'll get you a proper quota split so you're not being marked
down for it, and I want six a month and I'll take fewer plain ones for
them, and — " she stopped. "How did you find this out?"

And Sazare Ruri, standing in a shed at the back of the quartermaster's
yard with a materials test form in his hand, opened his mouth to tell her.

All of it. The whole of it: the cleavage plane on page eleven of a book
for fifteen-year-olds; the eastern provinces and the tanks; the four
seconds and the six; a culvert at Tomari and a kerb-line on a bridge and a
boy of fifteen dead on a headland; the fact that the number had moved for
the first time since he was fifteen years old and that there was not one
single person on this earth he had told.

And he shut it again, because the whole of it after the first two sentences
was a conversation about *what happens when somebody hits it*, and he was
a probationary foreign national under a total prohibition on combat
training in any discipline, and a man who volunteers that his stone now
survives an impact for six seconds instead of four has just told the
village he has been thinking about impacts.

"There's a library," he said. "The civilian one, off the Nakadōri. There's
forty books on rocks in it and nobody's ever read them."

"Hm," said Kanzaki Uta.

---

He wrote the seeds into the audit book that night, all twelve, in a row,
in his own hand, in the ruled columns.

*0341 | 9th, 7th month | seed, single, unfaulted | 0.4 g | retained |
S.R.*
*0342 | 9th, 7th month | seed, single, unfaulted | 0.4 g | retained |
S.R.*

— and so on, down the page, twelve times, at four-tenths of a gram apiece,
four point eight grams in total, which is about the weight of a
thimble.

He looked at the twelve lines for a while with the lamp going and the
persimmon dark outside the window.

Four hundred years of his family. A district that did not exist. A woman
who could hold a piece under a lattice for six hours and had never known
it was unusual. Every single technique he had, invented twice — once by
people who were all dead and once by him, alone, on benches, in four
countries.

And here it was at last, the sum of all of it: the best work anybody in
his line had ever done, twelve grains of colourless stone the size of
rice, that would not make a sound if you struck them, sitting in a tin on
a bench in a foreign country —

— entered in a government ledger at four-tenths of a gram each, weighed on
a Thursday by a woman in a leather apron, in the sixth column, in
pencil.

*Auditor: K.U.*

He put the lid on the tin and went to bed, because he was on at six.
