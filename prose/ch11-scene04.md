# Chapter 11, Scene 4 — It's Warm

*Act 2, part C. Sazare Ruri, age fifteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,700 words.*

---

He rode down with Hachirō on the first of the month, on the pretext of
seeing the road, which deceived nobody, least of all the horse.

"You'll be wanting the exchange," said Hachirō, as they came down out of
the snow-line into the green. "See your money off yourself for once. Very
proper. A man should watch his wages leave at least the one time — it
builds character and ruins the temper."

"How's the camber?"

"A disgrace," said Hachirō, gratified, and gave him the full state of the
road for an hour: the winter's slips, the second bridge-keeper's ongoing
crimes, a new checkpoint down at the coast fork — "customs men, they say;
they've a list and no manners; you'll not be on any list, riding up here
with lamp oil" — which Sazare filed where he filed everything now, without
comment, in the collection.

The war came up the way it always came up with Hachirō, sideways, as
freight. "Boats are queer this spring," he offered, to the horse's ears.
"Anzu's had three in a month — that's a lot of walking cargo — and the
herring's paying twice what it should, which means someone with money is
buying boats' silence, which means someone with more money is asking
boats questions. Something's moving over the water. The lads at the
exchange have stopped saying it's a shame, and started saying nothing,
and you can take that to the bank sooner than herring."

Something's moving. Sazare rode with it a while, the way you ride with a
stone in your boot to the next flat place. The tenth went down the
mountain in the lamp-oil manifest every month, and every month it was
received, which was the whole of what he knew, and more than he was owed.
Kohaku's road ran two ways. Everything on it did.

They came to the shrine at the middle of the second day, and Hachirō
stopped the cart, as he had stopped it every month for four years, and
got down to wash his face and stand a moment with his cap off.

Sazare got down too.

---

The spring ran at blood-heat out of the rock, the way it had run since
before the road, and the pool below it steamed in the cold morning, and
the shrine above it was the small grey weathered kind, no bigger than a
dog kennel, that nobody official had tended in a hundred years and
somebody unofficial had tended every week of them: the stone lip swept,
a cup on a chain, two winter oranges going soft on the offering shelf.

Hachirō washed his face, and wrung his cap for no reason as he always
did, and stood with his eyes shut.

Sazare put his hand in the water.

It was warm. It was exactly the warmth — he had known it would be and
had come anyway; had come *because* — of a tray of sand at four in the
morning with his mother's hands flat on it; of a pool up behind a
treatment house where a ruined arm had learned to be an arm again; of
the ground of this whole undefeated country that had nothing worth
taking and gave it away all day.

*She put her hand in and said, it's warm. As though I'd done it for her.*

He took the earring out of the fold of cloth in his sleeve.

He had brought it without letting himself frame what for. It was no good
to sell — hollow at one end, leaning, seamed — and it was not for sale,
and it was not for wearing; there was no ear in the world now with a
torn place the right shape for it. It was the first thing the new hands
had made, and it belonged to the dead, and all of his own dead were in
sealed rooms under a fog five hundred miles east, in a country he could
not enter, on a list he could not read.

But the dead, he had learned this year, in this country, might be a
thing you could give to sideways. Lamps went down one river and were
gone; nobody asked whose light was whose. An old woman held a pool open
forty minutes for miners she docked her own books for. A carter's wife,
forty years gone, still got her husband's cap off every month at a
spring she had once touched with one hand.

He leaned in through the steam and set the earring on the offering
shelf, between the two oranges, where the rising warmth off the pool
would keep the frost from ever quite reaching it, and stepped back, and
did not say anything, because there was nothing that needed informing.

Hachirō opened his eyes.

He looked at the shelf a moment — at the small crooked blue thing
standing between the oranges, catching the early light at the clear
end, hollow at the other, unmistakably nobody's work but a
part-trained hand's, and unmistakably *worked*, in a country where no
such thing was made — and then he put his cap back on and climbed up
and took the reins, and said nothing at all for a mile.

At the flat place past the milestone he spoke, to the road, in the
voice he kept for the camber and his wife:

"She'd have said you'd done it for her."

"I know," said Sazare. "That's why."

The cart went on down, into the green country, with the steam standing
over the valleys behind them, white and lit and climbing; and in the
manifest that month, under lamp-oil, sundries and carriage, the tenth
went where the tenth went, and the road carried all of it without
comment, the way the road carried everything — southward, seaward, home.
