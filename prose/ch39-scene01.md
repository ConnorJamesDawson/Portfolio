# Chapter 39, Scene 1 — The Year They Stopped

*Act 4, part D. Konoha. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare.
~1,444 words.*

---

It came up because she asked him about something else entirely, which
was how everything came up.

"What do you do at the turn of the year?"

"Nothing."

"Nobody does nothing. The whole village shuts for four days, there's
about eleven hundred people on that market row on the last night and
the hospital runs on a skeleton because half the staff have gone to
their mothers'." Tsunade turned her cup. "You were on the ward. I
checked the roster, because I was in the tower with a stack of border
returns and I wanted to know who else was working."

"I took the four days. Somebody has to and the ones with families
shouldn't."

"That's the answer to what you did. I asked what you *do*."

Sazare looked at the window, which had the year's first proper cold on
it, running with condensation on the inside because the place was full.

"Nothing," he said. "I mean it as an answer and not as a deflection.
There isn't a thing. I don't have one."

---

"Since when?"

And that was the question, and he had never once been asked it, and he
found that he had the answer to about four decimal places and had been
carrying it for six years without ever taking it out.

"The year I was eleven," he said.

Tsunade waited.

"My mother marked things. That's — I want to start there, because
people assume the opposite when I tell them and then I have to go back
and fix it, and I would rather not have to fix it with you." He put
both hands round the cup and did not drink from it. "She marked
everything. Name days, the turn of the year, the day my uncle's
daughter was born, the anniversary of my grandmother. She kept them in
the same book she kept the order lists in, at the back, in the same
hand, and I copied that book out four times a year from the age of
seven because that was how I was taught to write."

"So you know the dates."

"I know every date in that book. I could give you eleven of them now."
He turned the cup a quarter. "And she stopped in the year I was
eleven, and she did not stop because she was busy, and I want to say
that plainly because it is the version people reach for and it is not
true and she is not here to correct it."

"Then say the true one."

"The district went bad."

---

He said it flatly, because it was flat, and because he had established
a long time ago that the only way he could get through any of this was
to give it in the order it had happened.

"The inventory came in the year I was ten. Two clerks and a ledger, and
they counted everything in the workshop, and they counted us, and they
wrote down what we made in a year. Nobody explained it and my mother
told me it was ordinary and I believed her, and looking at it now she
almost certainly believed it herself."

"And the year after?"

"The round-ups started. Not us — other families, in other wards, and
you would hear about it four days late and second-hand and everybody
said it was a security matter." He kept his eyes on the cup. "And the
year I was eleven my mother stopped putting the dates in the back of
the book. She did not say she was stopping. She simply did not write
the next one down, and I noticed, because I copied that book out four
times a year, and I noticed in the *spring* that the previous eleventh
month was not there."

"Did you ask her?"

"No."

"Ruri."

"I was eleven and I had already worked out that there were things in
that house nobody said out loud, and I had been carrying my sister's
parcels across the district for two years without knowing what was in
them, and I was extremely good at not asking." He set the cup down.
"So no. I didn't ask. And I have thought about that particular
not-asking more than about almost anything else I have ever done,
because it is the first one. Everything I am is downstream of a boy
deciding not to ask his mother why she had stopped writing the dates
down."

---

Tsunade did not say anything for a moment.

"You said it wasn't that she was busy," she said. "How do you know?"

"Because the order book kept going." He said it without any weight at
all, which was the only way he could say it. "Same hand. Same four
times a year. Same discipline, right through, to within eleven days of
the end. She had time to write everything else down. She did not have
time for the other kind, and the difference between those two things
is the whole of what I know about what she thought was coming."

"That's not a small thing to know."

"It's the only thing I know. That's the part." He looked up. "She died
when I was thirteen and a half and I never asked her one single
question about any of it, and there is no version in which I find out
now whether she stopped for a year or whether she had stopped for
good, and I do not know whether she would have started again if it had
gone differently, and I am aware that this is the same problem I have
about four other things and that I have not got better at it."

"No," Tsunade agreed. "You haven't."

"You could tell me it doesn't matter."

"It matters enormously and I'm not going to insult you." She refilled
her own and, as had become the arrangement, did not touch his. "What I
will tell you is that there's no way through it. There's no method.
You'll get to sixty and you still won't know, and you'll have stopped
minding about four days out of five, and the fifth day it'll be exactly
as bad as it is now."

"That's not encouraging."

"It's not meant to be encouraging, it's meant to be *accurate*, and
you've told me four times that you'd rather have the second one." She
drank. "So. Nothing at the turn of the year since you were eleven."

"Nothing at the turn of the year since I was eleven."

---

"And your own," said Tsunade.

"That's a different question and the answer's shorter."

"Go on."

"The last one anybody marked was my tenth. There was a thing my mother
did with the lamp and I am not going to describe it because I have
tried twice in a notebook and it comes out sounding like nothing." He
turned the cup. "The eleventh, twelfth and thirteenth I don't remember
at all, which I have checked and re-checked, and I think the honest
finding is that there was nothing to remember. Fourteen and fifteen I
was on the road. Sixteen I found three days late off a dated docket in
Kasado, and I remember exactly where I was standing and what the docket
was for."

"And seventeen?"

"I knew the date. I worked a late." He said it evenly. "And eighteen I
found out about five weeks after the fact from the Fifth Hokage in a
drinking house because she reads my establishment record four times a
year."

Tsunade did not laugh.

"That's eight," she said.

"Eight what?"

"Eight birthdays." She was doing the arithmetic on the table with one
finger and not making any attempt to hide it. "Eleven, twelve,
thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen."

"That's correct."

"And you've told me all of that in about nine minutes without stopping,
which is a thing you could not have done a year ago, and I want that
noted." She sat back. "So what happens about the eighteenth?"

"Nothing. It's gone."

"It's three months gone."

"It's three months gone," Sazare agreed, "which as far as I can
establish means the matter is closed. A date is a date. I can't hold
one in the eleventh month by doing something in the second, that isn't
how dates work."

And Tsunade of the Sannin, Fifth Hokage of Konohagakure, looked at him
across a corner table for slightly longer than the sentence required,
and said:

"No. Of course not."

Sazare wrote down, four days later, in a notebook, under a column with
no unit at the head of it, that she had agreed with him unusually
quickly and that he had not thought about it at the time.
