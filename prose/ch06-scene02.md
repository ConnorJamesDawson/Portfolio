# Chapter 6, Scene 2 — Hands

*Act 1, beat 15. Sazare Ruri, age twelve. POV: Sazare. ~2,900 words.*

---

She did not come back in the fortnight.

Iwao said postings ran long. Ren said she'd been made chūnin in the spring and
they worked chūnin like dogs. Hisui said nothing and did eleven frames a week
instead of nine, which nobody mentioned.

Sazare did the Thursday errand for five weeks.

The sixth Thursday there was nothing at the house to collect and the door was
opened by a woman he had never seen, who said there was no Ōno there and never had
been, and shut it.

He stood in the rope-makers' street for a while.

Then he went home and did the small lattices and said nothing to anybody, and that
night he lay in the dark and did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do for six
weeks, which came out as follows.

She had said *there's one on Thursday*.

She had said *if I'm not back it doesn't matter, you just take it*.

She had, at some point between the argument and the morning, decided that she
might not be back, and had not said so, and had instead stood in his doorway and
told him to do seven minutes in the spring and get a house.

---

He went out on the following Tuesday at the eighth hour of the evening, over the
back wall, in his own clothes with a coat over them, and he was more frightened
than he had ever been about anything in his life.

He had six addresses.

That was all he had. Six places he had walked to, over four years, with a package
in his hands and a name in his head: the rope-makers' street; a chandler's on the
west quay; a house behind the tannery with a red gate; the fourth pontoon at the
south basin; a widow above a shop that sold nothing identifiable; and Kuromizu
ford, four miles out, end of the north side, boat in the yard.

He had no idea what any of them had in common and it had never once occurred to
him to wonder.

He started with the tannery because it was nearest.

---

The house with the red gate was empty. Not gone — *empty*, in the ordinary way, with
the shutters up and a bill pasted on the door about arrears. He stood in front of
it for a bit and then went round the back and looked at the step, because he could
not think of anything else to do, and there was nothing on the step.

The widow above the shop was still there and did not know him and was not
interested.

The fourth pontoon at the south basin had four boats on it in the dark and a man
mending a net under a lamp who looked at him once, incuriously, and went back to
the net.

By the eleventh hour he had walked about six miles in a circle and had achieved
nothing whatsoever, and he was cold, and his eyes had gone hot, and he stood on
the walkway above the south basin and thought, with the particular clarity of a
child at the end of a long stupid day, that he was twelve years old and had no
idea what he was doing and that everybody in the world was better at this than he
was.

Then he went to the chandler's on the west quay, because it was the last one, and
because going home meant it was over.

---

The chandler's was shut. Of course it was shut; it was the eleventh hour.

There was a hasp on the door and a lamp still burning inside somewhere at the back
and a rope-peg board along the front wall under the overhang, where the coils hung
in the day, and Sazare stood in front of it with his hands in his sleeves and made
himself look at everything, properly, the way you look at a bowl of water — not
searching, which was the same mistake as pushing. Just looking, and letting the
thing that was different come up out of it.

It came up out of it.

There was a piece of crystal wedged in the joint of the second peg from the left.

It was about the size of a thumbnail, and it was cloudy, and it was worthless, and
it had been pushed into the gap where the peg went into the board so that the flat
of it sat flush and you would have had to be looking at the pegs rather than at the
door to see it at all.

He got it out with a fingernail and turned it over.

There were marks on it.

Four strokes, a hook, a long curve that went three quarters of the way round and
stopped, and a cluster of small marks inside the curve like a handful of thrown
sand.

He knew that hand.

He did not know whose it was. He had never known whose any of them were, and he had
asked, and had been told it was not the game. But he had seen that curve, with that
particular sag in the third stroke where the nib had been going slightly dry, on
the outside of a package he had carried in the second month of the previous year —
a heavy one, in the rain, when he was ten — and he could remember the weight of it
in his hands and the particular puddle he had gone round on the corner and the
exact door he had knocked on at the other end.

Behind the tannery. The house with the red gate.

Which was empty, and had a bill about arrears on it, and which he had looked at
four hours ago from the front.

---

He went back to the tannery at a run.

The gate was still red. The shutters were still up. He went round the back the way
he had gone before and looked at the step the way he had looked at the step, and
there was still nothing on the step, and he stood there breathing hard and made
himself stop and *look*.

The step was stone. The wall beside it was stone. There was a drain, and a gutter
bracket, and above the gutter bracket a hook where a line had once been fixed, and
the hook had rust down it in a stripe, and in the socket of the hook, wedged, flush
—

He had to stand on the bin.

Two pieces this time. Both cloudy. Two different hands: one he had never seen in
his life, and one he had taken to a widow above a shop that sold nothing
identifiable, seven streets north, in the ninth month of the year he turned nine.

He was not certain about the second one. He was fairly certain. He had a feeling
about the tail of the fourth stroke that he could not have justified to anybody.

He went seven streets north.

---

It took him until nearly the second hour of the morning and it went: the widow's
back wall, in the crack under the sill; then a hand he did not know at all, which
cost him twenty minutes of standing in an alley failing to think of anything; then
the same unknown hand, again, on a boat cleat at the fourth pontoon, which he only
found because he had already been there once and knew what the pontoon looked like
when it was ordinary.

And then a hand he knew immediately and completely, because it was the one from
Kuromizu — from Bekku, and the boat in the yard, and the ford in the rain — and it
was pressed into the tar of a bollard at the top of the south basin steps.

Which was not four miles out on the coast road.

Which was here.

He stood at the top of the steps with a bit of worthless crystal in his fist and
understood, all at once, with his ears ringing, what he had been doing since he was
seven.

---

The presents.

Sen and the anise. The man who said *right* and shut the door. Ōno with the bad
ear. Forty-odd packages over four years, wrapped in waxed paper and tied with
string, that did not rattle.

The chips in the cloth roll — *find me two cousins* — and *anybody can learn what a
thing means, hardly anyone can look at a page of scribble and go, those two, those
two are the same hand.*

*It's completely useless. That's why it's a game.*

He sat down on the top step of the south basin at two in the morning in the eleventh
month and put his head on his knees.

It did not feel like betrayal. That was the thing he would try and fail to explain
to himself for years afterwards. He sat there in the cold and went through every
one of those evenings on the step — the twelve chips, then the twenty ugly ones,
then the thirty-four when he had got bored again — and what he felt was not that
he had been used.

What he felt was that his sister had spent four years teaching him something
difficult, patiently, badly, in the evenings, when she was tired, and had never
once told him it was worth anything, and had said *yeah* in the same tone she used
for *no*.

And that she had been extremely careful, every single time, to send him to people
who would take the thing off him at the door and shut it in his face.

He wiped his face on his sleeve.

Then he got up, because the bollard was pointing somewhere and he could read it.

---

The trail ran four more stops and it took him the rest of the night, and by the end
of it he was in a part of the village he had never been to, up past the old
saltworks on the north side where the streets stopped having names and started
having numbers, and where nobody lived except people who could not afford to live
anywhere else.

The last piece was not hidden.

It was sitting on top of a mooring post at the end of a jetty in plain sight, and
it was not cloudy and it was not worthless. It was a clean blue lozenge about the
size of a thumb joint, four hours' work, his mother's grade — and the marks on it
went right round twice and were in a hand he had never seen and which was better
than any hand he had ever seen, tighter and smaller and completely even, and he
stood there looking at it and thought, with a kind of despair, that there was a
whole world of this and he could see about a hundredth of it.

Beyond the jetty there was a boat shed with the doors shut and a light on inside.

Sazare walked down the jetty in the dark with the crystal in his hand.

He was two months past twelve years old. He had eaten nothing since the previous
afternoon. He had walked, by his own later reckoning, something over eleven miles
since the eighth hour of the evening, most of it doubled back on itself, and he had
not once considered going home.

He knocked on the door of the boat shed.
