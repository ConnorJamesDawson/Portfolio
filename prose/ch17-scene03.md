# Chapter 17, Scene 3 — Sweet Water

*Act 2, part D. Kasado, the strait. POV: Ine, chandler, age fifty-two.
~2,150 words.*

---

The third hour, and blowing a little from the north-east, which was the
wrong quarter but not badly wrong, and Ine came down the shingle in her
oilskin with the lamp shut and three people behind her walking the way
people walk when they have been told twice not to talk.

The skiff was the small one, which she did not like and had chosen
anyway. The big one had been up on the hard at the second boatyard for
eleven days having a garboard put back in, and the garboard was going to
cost her eight ryō she did not have, and the man doing it had stopped
asking when she meant to pay him, which was worse than being asked.

She got them in. A woman of about forty. A boy of nine or ten, wrapped in
what was clearly the woman's other coat. And a man of sixty-odd who had
been a schoolmaster somewhere and had said so twice on the walk down, in
the voice people use when they are trying to establish that they used to
be somebody.

"Sit where I put you and don't shift," said Ine. "If you're sick, be sick
down there, not over the side. Nobody stands up. If the boy needs to go,
he goes in the bailer and I'll not have a word said about it."

"Is it far?" said the woman.

"It's nine hundred yards."

"That's not far."

"It's the widest nine hundred yards on this coast," said Ine, "and I've
done it four hundred times, so sit down."

---

She pushed off at the top of the slack and let the shingle go by under
her and took the sculls, and forty feet out she put her left hand over
the side into the water.

She did this every crossing and had done it every crossing since she was
eleven, and nobody had ever asked her about it, because a boatwoman with
her hand in the water is a boatwoman with her hand in the water.

There: the cold thread.

The strait ran salt, obviously; it was the sea. But there was a river
four miles up the north coast that put its whole winter down this channel
in a sweet cold ribbon that lay under the tide and moved when the tide
moved, and Ine could feel it the way you feel a draught under a door —
where it ran, how deep it lay, which way it was sliding, and therefore,
because the ribbon went where the set of the current went, exactly what
the water was going to do to her boat for the next three minutes in
total darkness with no lamp and no stars.

That was the whole of it. That was the entire inheritance.

Her grandmother had it. Her grandmother had used it for cockles. Her
mother had it and had used it to know which well on the hill would go
dry in August, which had been worth a certain amount of respect in a
village of two hundred people and no money whatever.

When the fog came down over the water and the lists went out, the lists
had not cared what it was for.

Ine had been on the third list, in the second year, in a village of two
hundred people, and had gone out of a back window at the age of
forty-nine with her mother's ring in her mouth and had never seen one of
her neighbours again; and had been at Kasado ever since, on the wrong
side of the strait from all of it, doing the one thing on this earth that
a woman who can feel sweet water under salt is actually good for.

---

The crossing took nineteen minutes and was a bad one.

The wind put a short sea on top of the ebb about a third of the way over
and the boy was sick, into the bailer, with enormous dignity, and the
schoolmaster began to say something reassuring and Ine told him to shut
up in a voice that surprised all four of them.

She had the ribbon under her the whole way. It slid west earlier than she
wanted — it always did on a north-easter — and she went with it rather
than across it and lost four minutes and about eighty yards and gained
the entire boat, which is a trade a person makes without thinking about
it after four hundred times.

She put them ashore in the gut below the pines where there is no beach
and no path and a great deal of bramble, because a landing anybody would
choose is a landing anybody can watch.

The man who met them came down through the pines with no lamp. He was
called Bunzō and he was a charcoal burner and he had a cart and a
tolerable reason to be on that road at that hour every day of his life,
and he had been doing this for a year and a half and had never once asked
her a single question about anything.

"Three."

"Three." Ine handed up the woman's bundle. "The old fellow's got a
brother at Ōgi and thinks it's arranged. It isn't arranged. Don't let him
go up there on his own thinking it is."

"Right."

"The boy's not been eating."

"Right."

The woman of forty turned round at the top of the shingle and came back
down two steps and stood there in the dark trying to find something to
say, and Ine, who had had this happen perhaps three hundred times and had
never once been any good at it, said:

"Go on. He's waiting. Go on."

And they went up through the pines, and Ine pushed off and took the
sculls and did the nine hundred yards back the other way with an empty
boat and the ebb behind her, in eleven minutes, in the dark, with her
left hand over the side.

---

She did the marks on the way up from the slip because it was on her road
and because she had never in her life gone to bed with a mark undone.

The chip came off the second peg of the rope-board at the third boatyard
and went into her pocket, which meant *nobody in there now, don't knock*,
and would go back up on the peg tomorrow evening when the next lot came
down. It was a chip of cloudy quartz off the beach at the point and there
were nine more like it in a jar under her counter and it had cost her
nothing, and the whole system had cost her nothing, and that was the
beauty of it and always had been.

She had learned it at twenty-two from a woman at Nagihama who had learned
it from somebody else, and had changed nothing about it in thirty years,
because there was nothing to change. You cannot improve on a stone.

Then she went up the north quay in the dark and let herself into the
chandlery and sat down behind the counter without lighting anything and
was too tired to go up the stairs.

---

The accounts were the accounts and she went through them anyway, in her
head, in the dark, because they were not going to change by being
avoided.

The garboard: eight ryō. The chandlery: sound, more or less; the trade
was real and it paid, and it had paid for everything for twelve years.
The line: not sound. The line had never been sound. Four crossings this
week and the four crossings had brought in, between the lot of them,
about eleven ryō and a wedding ring she was not going to sell, and a
crossing cost her a night, a boat, and a share to Bunzō, who took it and
did not want it and had a family.

And then the other item, which had appeared in the summer and had not
gone away.

They had come to the counter twice. Two different men, both of them
perfectly civil, and the second one had bought forty feet of three-strand
he did not need and had paid for it and had said, on the way out, that it
was a shame about the gulls at the point going after the small boats'
painters, and that a boat off its painter in that current was a boat gone
altogether, and that she'd want to watch that.

The following Tuesday the small skiff had gone off its painter, at the
top of the flood, and had been three miles down the coast by daylight and
had cost her a week and a man's wages to get back.

She was fifty-two and she was not a fool.

She had done the arithmetic more than once, in this chair, in the dark:
that the ferry syndicate at Kasado turned over something like four
hundred ryō a month; that at the head of it were three men who lived at
Kasado, drank at Kasado, and were going to have to live and drink at
Kasado afterwards; that she was costing them perhaps a tenth of that and
had been costing them more every season for two years; and that the
distance between a skiff off its painter and a woman off a shingle spit
is not a great distance, in the border country, in this particular year
of the world.

Her son wrote from the Land of Frost about twice a season. He had a wife
and a business and a spare room and he had stopped putting *come and
live with us* at the end and had started putting it at the beginning,
which was his way of leaning on it.

She answered the letters. She never answered that part.

It was not courage and she would have hit anybody who called it that.
It was that there were, this week, thirty-one people in the two inns on
the south quay who had paid the ferry syndicate forty ryō a head out of
everything they had left in the world, and about a third of them were
going to be told, at the last minute, that the price had gone up; and
they would come to the north quay, because everybody comes to the north
quay eventually, and stand in front of her counter not knowing how to
ask.

You cannot hand that to somebody else. There is nobody to hand it to. She
had looked.

---

At about the fifth hour, with the light coming grey over the toll shed
and the first cart going down to the yards, Ine got up off the stool and
put the kettle on and remembered the block-pulley.

It had been binding for a month. It was the big one over the door, the
one she got the tar barrels up with, and it wanted taking down and taking
apart, and the smith at Kasado was a horseshoe man who would look at it
and shrug.

But there was a new lad in the back of the second boatyard.

She had seen him on the Tuesday and had not thought about it since. A
young one, sixteen or so, with a leg that did not work properly, sitting
on a bench doing forty tally-frames for the Ihoya smith with the
particular unhurried economy of somebody who has done nine thousand of
them; and she had stood in the yard gateway for a moment with a coil of
rope on her shoulder and watched him take a frame apart, and had thought,
with the specific pleasure of a woman who has spent thirty years buying
tools:

*That one's proper.*

She would send the pulley over. It would be a few coppers and it was
worth it, and it would give the boy a bit of work off his own bat rather
than the smith's, and — this was the part she would have denied if
anybody had put it to her — a lad on his own in a strange town with a
bad leg is a lad on his own in a strange town with a bad leg.

She'd send the pulley over on the Friday, and see what he made of it, and
if he was any good she'd put his name about a bit, because a mender who
can actually mend is worth four of a smith on a coast like this, and
because it costs nothing to put a name about.

Then she banked the stove and went up the stairs at last, at ten past
five, to get two hours before the counter opened; and in the morning at
low water she would walk the tideline east under the toll shed and round
to the shingle at the point, alone, the way she did six mornings out of
seven, because it was the only forty minutes in the day that belonged to
her.
