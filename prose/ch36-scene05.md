# Chapter 36, Scene 5 — Sober, In Daylight

*Act 4, part C. Konoha, the quartermaster's shed. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,258 words.*

---

He told Kanzaki in the shed behind the yard on a Thursday, at the
eleventh hour, in daylight, with the doors open and a bucket of
aggregate between them and nothing whatsoever to lower any threshold.

It was, he decided afterwards, the correct order. He had said it drunk
to somebody who had no professional interest in his output. He now
said it sober to the one person in the world whose entire relationship
with him was his output, which was the harder version by a
considerable margin and was the only one that settled anything.

It came out of a technical conversation, which was the only way
anything ever came out of him.

"Thirty kilos of surplus," Kanzaki said, when he had explained it.
"Over and above quota, over sixteen months, alongside an eleven per
cent increment I have already told them is optimistic."

"Yes."

"You'll need the evenings."

"I'm not giving up the evenings."

"Then you'll need better yield, because you cannot make thirty kilos
out of an unchanged rate and a fixed number of hours by wanting it."
She was already reaching for the ledger. "Right. Your reject rate's
been running at about twenty-two per cent for a year, which is not bad
and is not good, and about two thirds of your rejects fail on the flat
rather than on the body—"

"It's not the yield."

Kanzaki's hand stopped on the ledger.

"Say that again."

"It's not the yield." Sazare put both hands on his knees. "You're going
to spend forty minutes finding me four per cent, and you'll find it,
and it'll be good work and I'll take it. And the reason I can do thirty
kilos in sixteen months is not four per cent. It's that I already work
four nights a week at a bench in my own room and I have never once
counted those hours against anything."

"The bench nights are the crystal doctrine work."

"Some of them."

"How many?"

"About one in three." He heard himself and kept going. "The other two
are jewellery."

---

Kanzaki Uta took her glasses off her forehead, put them on her nose,
and looked at him.

"Say more."

"There isn't much. I have a bench I built out of scrap in nine evenings
in my second year here. I have my mother's tools, which is four of
them, because four is what I got out of the country. I have a
jeweller's roll I made at fifteen for entirely different reasons and I
still use it." He turned the aggregate bucket a quarter turn without
noticing. "Two nights a week I sit at it and make things nobody wants
and nobody weighs. Settings, mostly. Stone mounts. I've been working on
a closed bezel for about seven months because there's a thing in it I
can't do yet and the man who could have shown me is dead."

"What do you do with them?"

"Nothing. They go in a tin." He stopped. "One went to Ōtaki Sue. She
doesn't know it was me."

"Ruri." Kanzaki set the pen down flat and squared it, which he had
watched her do about four hundred times and which meant she had decided
to give a thing her whole attention. "I have weighed everything you
have made for three years. Everything. There is a ruled line in that
book for four-tenths of a gram of failed substrate."

"I know."

"And you have been running a second bench for two of those years and
have never once mentioned it."

"It's not fabrication. It's not on the quota. There's nothing to
audit."

"That is a completely correct answer and it is not why you didn't
mention it," said Kanzaki, "and I would like the real one, and you may
refuse, and if you refuse I will not ask again."

---

He looked at the doors.

The yard was doing what the yard did at the eleventh hour, which was
carts and shouting and a man rolling a barrel badly.

"I wanted to be a jeweller," said Sazare.

The check ran. He felt it run — the quarter-second, the *what does this
tell them* — and it produced, this time, an entirely accurate
assessment, which was that telling the quartermaster of a shinobi
village that you had never wanted any of this was a professionally
foolish thing to do.

He said it anyway, which was new.

"That's what I was going to be. My mother had the fine bench and I was
going to have it after her, and I was doing stock lists at seven and
settings at nine and I was going to have that bench by twenty. I went
to the Academy because Ruri went to the Academy — four years, a file
with the state, and then you came home and made earrings. I never
wanted to be a shinobi. Not one day. Not one day in my life."

Kanzaki did not say anything.

"I've got a support classification and a ward and a four-year contract
and I'd do all of it again tomorrow, and I'm good at it, and eleven
people on that floor would come and get me if I stopped turning up."
He kept his eyes on the doors. "And two nights a week I sit in a rented
room and make a bezel nobody will ever wear, badly, and it is the only
thing in my week that I do not have a single other reason for."

"And you told me."

"You've weighed everything I've made since I was sixteen." He looked
at her. "You're the person it costs the most to tell. I've worked out
that that's how you know it's true."

---

Kanzaki Uta sat with a ledger under her hand for what he counted at
eleven seconds.

"Right," she said. "Two things, and then we're going to find your four
per cent, because I meant it about the rate."

"Go on."

"First. I have known there was a second bench for about fourteen
months."

Sazare turned his head.

"You collect small-gauge wire off my scrap tray," said Kanzaki. "You
have done it for fourteen months. You have never taken more than about
nine inches at a time, you have never taken anything that was on an
issue sheet, and you have entered it in the visitors' book every single
time as *scrap, sub-issue, nil value*, which is correct and which
nobody in this yard has done in eleven years." She almost smiled. "You
cannot buy small-gauge silver wire in this village on a technician's
pay. I worked it out in the spring of last year and I have been waiting
ever since for you to say something."

"You never asked."

"No. And I'm not going to explain why, because you already know, and
because I would rather you had told me than been asked." She opened the
ledger. "Second thing. Bring me one."

"One what?"

"One of the things in the tin. Whichever one you think is worst — not
the best, the *worst*, I want to see where the fault is." She found her
page. "I have looked at three years of your fabrication and I have
never once seen you make anything that was for anybody. I would like
to."

"They're not very good."

"You have been telling me your work isn't very good since you were
sixteen years old," said Kanzaki Uta, "and you have been wrong every
single time, and I have got it in writing on about four hundred ruled
lines. Bring me one."
