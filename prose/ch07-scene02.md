# Chapter 7, Scene 2 — Eleven Ryō

*Act 1, beat 18. Sazare Ruri, age twelve. POV: Sazare. ~2,600 words.*

---

The boat shed had been empty for three weeks.

He got up there on the second night, at the hour he had gone before, and the
doors were open and there was nothing inside but the plank across two crates and
a broom and a smell of cold stove. The floor had leaves on it. Somebody had taken
the charts.

He went out to the end of the jetty and looked at the mooring post and there was
nothing on it and nothing in the joint of it and nothing anywhere, and he went
along the whole jetty on his hands and knees in the dark like an idiot, feeling
every board, and found a nail.

He went back to all six addresses over the next four days.

The chandler's on the west quay had a new man in it who sold him rope. The
rope-makers' street had the woman who had never heard of Ōno. Behind the tannery,
the red gate had been repainted green.

The fourth pontoon at the south basin still had four boats on it and a man mending
a net, possibly the same man, who looked at him twice this time.

He did not go to Kuromizu. It was four miles and he had eleven ryō.

---

Jirō's mother stood in her own doorway and cried while she said no, which Sazare
had not expected and did not know what to do with.

"I can't, love. I can't. I've got the four of them and Jirō's brother's just
posted and there's—"

"I don't need anything."

"You need *everything*, that's what you need—"

"I just need — a couple of nights. Just till I—"

"Sazare."

He stopped.

Jirō's mother was a wide, loud, kind woman who had fed him about four hundred
times in eight years and had once smacked the back of his head for something he
had genuinely deserved, and she was holding the door frame with both hands.

"They came round the street," she said. "Tuesday. Two of them, with a list. Asking
who'd been in and out of your gate."

Sazare's whole scalp went cold.

"What did you say?"

"I said you and Jirō went up and down to the Academy together and I'd fed you and
that was the whole of it, and I said it straight, and they wrote it down and went
next door." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "And they'll come back.
That's what they do, that's what everyone says, they come back after a fortnight
and they ask you the same questions and they see if you say it different."

"I'll go," said Sazare.

"If you were *here*—"

"I'll go. I'm going. It's all right."

"It's *not* all right—"

"It's all right," he said, and it came out flat and adult and he heard himself do
it and it frightened him more than anything that had happened that week.

---

Jirō caught him at the end of the street.

He had come out the back and round and he was in his indoor things with no coat
and he was carrying, in both hands, a fish-cloth with something in it and his own
belt-purse.

"Take it."

"Jirō—"

"*Take it.*" He was crying, furiously, in the way of a boy who has decided not to
and is losing. "It's eight ryō. It's got — there's the rest of the rice in the
cloth and there's a — she does a thing with the—"

"I can't take your—"

"You're not *taking* it, I'm *giving* it to you, that's a completely different
thing, and if you make me stand here arguing about it my mum's going to come out
and it'll be worse." He shoved the whole lot into Sazare's chest. "Take it."

Sazare took it.

They stood in the road.

"Where are you going to go?" said Jirō.

"I don't know."

"Right." Jirō wiped his face on his sleeve, which achieved nothing. "Right, so —
listen. Don't be in the market in the morning, they're on the market in the
mornings now. And there's the sheds behind the salt yard, my brother says nobody
goes in the second one because the roof's — it's just, nobody goes in it. And
don't sleep at the *harbour*, whatever you do, don't sleep at the harbour."

"All right."

"And keep your head down," said Jirō, and his voice cracked all the way through
it. "That's all. That's the whole — that's all you have to do. Just keep your head
down and it'll — they'll stop, they always stop, they stopped after the Kanō for
about eight months, my brother says it goes in — it goes in *waves*, and you just
have to be — you have to not be anything, and then it goes past."

"Yeah."

"You're going to keep your head down."

"Yeah."

"*Say it.*"

"I'm going to keep my head down," said Sazare.

Jirō nodded about six times, fast.

Then he hugged him, hard, for about two seconds, and let go and went back down the
street at a run without looking round.

Sazare did not see him again for eleven years, and when he did, Jirō was wearing a
mask.

---

The eleven ryō and the eight ryō lasted twenty-three days, which he thought at the
time was quite good and was in fact appalling.

He was very bad at it. That was the thing nobody had ever told him and which he
had to find out one stupid mistake at a time. He was bad at it because he had
never in his life had to think about where food came from, or where you went when
it rained, or what happened to your hands after four nights in a shed with no
fire.

He learned. He learned the way you learn a thing at twelve, which is by getting it
wrong.

He learned that you did not buy a whole meal from a stall because a boy alone
buying a whole meal was a boy with money. He learned that the second shed behind
the salt yard was warm and had a roof and was known to about nine other people,
five of whom were adults, and that this was fine on the nights when it was fine.
He learned that a public bath cost four sen and was worth eight, because a clean
child is invisible and a filthy one is a question.

He learned that Ozaki the fishmonger — *young Ruri, son, young Ruri* — would leave
a wrap of the small ones on the end of the slab at the end of the day if you came
past at the right time, and would not look at you while you took it, and would not
ever say anything about it, not once, not in four months.

He learned that he could not sell the offcuts, because there was nowhere to sell
them and they were worthless, and that he was going to carry them anyway.

---

He did not make anything.

He had, in the second week, in the salt shed, with nothing else to do and his
hands shaking with cold, put them flat on the boards in front of him out of pure
habit and started to find the place where it was moving.

He got about four seconds in.

Then he took his hands away and sat on them, and did not try again — not that
night, not that year, not for a long time after that, and he could not have told
anybody why. It was not that it hurt. It was that the whole of it, the sitting
still and the not interfering and the four hours and the *there, that's a good
shape* — all of it belonged to a room with nine frames in it and a woman with her
sleeves pinned, and doing it in a shed behind a salt yard felt like using
somebody's name after they had died.

Which nobody had. He had no reason to think anybody had.

He worked at that, over and over, the way you work at a loose tooth.

---

Twice in the first month he went back to the compound.

The first time the gate was still barred and the yard was the same and the bowl was
still on the step with the skin gone hard in it and his frame was still beside it,
and the sand was still all over the floor of the north room with the same
footprints in it, and nothing had changed at all, and that was somehow worse than
if it had.

The second time, six weeks later, there was a paper on the gate.

It was a notice, on notice paper, in the same hand as the notice about mooring
fees, and it said that the premises were subject to sequestration pending
assessment, and it gave a reference number, and it said that enquiries should be
made at the district office.

Sazare stood in front of it for a long while.

Then he went to the district office, because he was twelve, and because it said
enquiries should be made at the district office.

---

There was a queue. He stood in it for two hours.

The man behind the counter was about thirty and had four ledgers and a great many
people to get through, and when Sazare gave him the reference number he wrote it
down and went away and came back with a card.

"Right," he said. "That's held pending assessment. Nothing I can do."

"It's my house."

"It's held pending—"

"Where are they?" said Sazare. "There were five people in it. My mum and my dad
and my uncle and—"

"I've got a reference and a status," said the man. "That's all that's on the card.
Held pending assessment."

"Then who's got the rest of it?"

The man looked at him.

He was not unkind. That was the thing. He was a tired man at the end of a long
counter with sixty people behind a twelve-year-old, and what he did was put his
pen down for a second, which cost him something, and say:

"Son. Listen to me. Don't come back here and ask that."

"But—"

"Don't come back here and ask that," he said. "Next."
