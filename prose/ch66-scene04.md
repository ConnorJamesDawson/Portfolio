# Chapter 66, Scene 4 — The Button

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the Hokage's tower and the quartermaster's
yard. Tenzō, age twenty-seven. POV: Tenzō. ~1940 words.*

---

He read the file on Sunday night, all of it, because Monday deserved a
man who had done the reading, and because he had learned at six years
old that the difference between a posting and an ambush is how much of
the paper you saw first.

It took five hours. It should have taken two. He kept stopping.

Four papers with the Corps' stamp, method sections open to the world,
written in a hand so level it read like print. A reject table four
hundred rows long — *four hundred rows* — every failure numbered,
dated, weighed, and blamed on nobody. Eleven pages of splint method
that three hospitals outside the walls were apparently now using. A
sword's monthly log, filed by the sword's own maker, showing sixteen
days of continuous draw and a fourth reading the clerks had queried
twice because they did not believe an article's order could go *up*.

And the governor. Eleven clauses, signed into a Corps ledger in the
subject's own hand, restricting what the subject was permitted to do
with himself. He read clause eleven four times.

*Including if somebody takes it off my body.*

Tenzō had spent nine years in a shop where they wrote files about
weapons, and he had been the subject of several, and he sat in his bare
room with the lamp down low and thought: *I have read weapon files all
my life. This is the first one the weapon wrote.*

Then the last page, the incident report, her hand.

*No procedure existed and none exists.*

He put the file down and looked at the wall for a while, and what he
thought about was not the fight, which he had now read four accounts
of, or the article in the boy's leg, which he had read one account of
and did not intend to read again tonight.

What he thought about was a shut tea house in the summer, and a scale,
and a boy who had asked him — first of anybody, first of *everybody*,
in twenty-five years of growing timber out of his own hands — where
the wood comes from.

He had made a rose that afternoon, because it was small and he was
being watched by somebody it was restful to be watched by. The boy had
weighed the rose. Then he had weighed Tenzō. And what the scale said,
either time, was between the three of them — him, the boy, and the
notebook — but it had kept Tenzō up three nights, and at the end of the
third night he had understood that he was going to spend the rest of
his life inside that question whether he took a posting near it or not.

Kakashi-senpai had come on Friday and built him a door anyway, because
that was the arrangement older than any of this: the exit before the
question, every time, for eighteen years.

It was a good door. He had admired the joinery and left it shut.

---

Monday, six sharp, the Hokage's office, and there was real tea in the
room, which told him something about how she wanted this to go before
a word was said.

The boy arrived at five fifty-eight. Sword on him, open — Tenzō felt
it come through the door a half-second before its owner, a low
ordered *busyness* at the edge of sense, like standing near a beehive
in good weather — and nobody in the room reacted, because there was a
written instrument eleven clauses long that made the sword's presence
in this office not merely legal but *filed*.

The Hokage read the order out loud, all of it, formal, which took
under a minute because she had written it herself and it was three
lines and a name.

"Questions," she said.

"May I read it," said the boy.

She passed it over. He read it in about ninety seconds, and Tenzō
watched him do it and understood that the boy was not reading the
words, or not only — he was reading the *document*: the hand, the
spacing, the pressure, where the pen had stopped and thought. Reading
it the way Tenzō would read a treeline.

"Approved," said the boy. "And I would like the minutes to carry that
I asked for this on the twenty-fourth of last month, so that the file
shows the collar was requested before it shows the collar was fitted.
That ordering matters. Not to me. To whoever reads this in thirty
years."

"Minuted," said Shizune, writing.

"I have one objection," said the boy, "and it is not to the order."

The Hokage's chin came up a degree. Tenzō, who read rooms the way
other people breathed, saw her put down whatever she had been braced
for, and get interested instead.

"This posting hangs a lamp on him." The boy did not look at Tenzō; he
said it to her, straight, the way a man reads a defect into the
record. "His name is four letters and an arrangement that the four
letters are all anybody gets, and the arrangement is eighteen years
old, and it has held because nothing official has ever pointed at him
for longer than a mission requires. Monday the posting sheet goes up
on a board. Boards are read. I am — by the file's own account — the
most watched fabricator in the Land of Fire." He paused, exactly long
enough to be certain of the sentence, and finished it. "I have spent
two years learning what it costs to be read off a wall at forty feet.
I will not be the reason it is done to a man who chose the opposite
life and built it properly."

There was a silence with some respect in it.

"Answer him," said the Hokage, to Tenzō, and sat back.

Tenzō turned in his chair. The boy met his eyes, and it was the same
level grey attention as the tea house, and it had a fight and an
incident report and ninety seconds of something nobody could explain
behind it now, and it was pointed, entire, at the problem of *Tenzō's
safety*, and Tenzō noted — mildly, the way he noted everything, in the
inventory that never shut — that he could not remember the last time
anything had been pointed at that.

"On Friday," he said, "Kakashi-senpai came to my room and offered me a
door. A full exit. A posting in the east, distance, deniability, and
the file showing I was never asked. He builds good doors. He has been
building them for me for eighteen years, and I have used about forty
of them, and I looked at this one for a while, because it was a
particularly good one." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the
saucer, which was as much ceremony as he had ever owned. "I didn't use
it. You asked me a question in the summer that nobody had asked me in
twenty-five years, and I have been standing inside it since, and this
posting is the seat with the view of the answer. The lamp is real. I
priced it. I am telling you, on the record, in front of the Hokage of
the Land of Fire: the lamp costs less than the seat is worth."

The boy looked at him for three full seconds.

"Then I withdraw the objection," he said, "and I would like the
minutes to carry that it was answered, not overruled."

"Minuted," said Shizune, and if her pen had a smile in it, that was
between her and the paper.

"And now the thing I do not like," said the boy.

"There it is," said the Hokage, to nobody, with the satisfaction of a
woman watching weather arrive on schedule.

"Clause four. Presence at all live working." He said it without heat,
which somehow gave it more weight rather than less. "I will comply,
fully, from today. And the record should carry this: nobody has stood
at my bench while I worked since Hot Water. Not from secrecy. It is
simply a thing that has been mine — the one arrangement in my life
that predates every arrangement in this room. I do not like giving it
up. I will give it up. And I want that on paper, dated the first day,
so that later — when I have stopped minding, which I estimate at
eleven weeks — nobody, including me, can say I was managed into it."

"Minuted," said Shizune, "with the estimate."

"Eleven weeks," said the Hokage, straight-faced. "We'll diary it."

---

They walked down to the yard together, the new chain and its post, and
Tenzō spent the walk doing what he was for, which was seeing.

The sword rode at the boy's back in a soft case with the mouth open —
open on purpose, open *administratively*, a fact with a form behind
it — and people on the tower stairs had stopped noticing it, which
told him the misdirection had finished its work some weeks ago and was
now simply true.

The yard was swept, racked, and labelled to a standard Tenzō had
previously seen only in armouries run by the terrifying. The scale sat
under a cloth. The reject bin was full of numbered failure.

"Clause four says presence," said the boy, at the bench. "It does not
say distance. You will want to establish your own. Kakashi-san stands
at eleven feet. Katsuyu-sama prefers three. The Hokage—" and here
there was a pause of a single grain, a pause Tenzō banked without
spending— "stands where she likes, which is a property of Hokage. I
recommend eight feet for the first fortnight. At eight feet you can
see the hands and the work, and I cannot pretend you are furniture,
which for the purposes of the eleven weeks is the point."

"Eight feet," said Tenzō, and took it up, and found that from eight
feet you could indeed see everything: the tool roll opening like a
book being greeted, the two tins, the lamp lit with a spill from the
brazier though the morning did not strictly need it, a working order
as old and load-bearing as liturgy.

*He has built himself a monastery with a population of one,* thought
Tenzō, who had been built in a facility with a population of sixty and
had come out the only book. *And on Wednesday, the diary says, he
goes on holiday. And in the evenings, the diary says, senjutsu, on the
second floor, at hours I have never in nine years of watching anything
seen a training block occupy.*

He filed that where he filed everything that was a pattern and not yet
a fact: *noted, insufficient data, not mine to interpret, kept.*

Nine years in the shop taught you many things, and the first was the
difference between what you are cleared to see and what you are
assigned to see, and the second was that a man who volunteers
interpretations of the first soon loses access to both.

"Question," said the boy, not looking up from the roll. "The button.
Third down, left side. You still have it."

Tenzō looked down at his own jacket, at the pale wooden disc sitting a
sixteenth proud of five stamped horn siblings, hand-cut in nine passes
by a knife eighteen years ago.

"I still have it."

"Good," said the boy, and took up the first article of the working
day, and that was all — except that Tenzō, walking his eight feet of
new perimeter in a yard like a kept promise, found he had put his
thumb to the button's chamfer, briefly, the way you touch a coin from
a country you are, against all previous information, apparently going
back to.
