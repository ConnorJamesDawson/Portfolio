# Chapter 41, Scene 1 — Three Books and a Bench

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the room over the persimmon tree. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,245 words.*

---

The first thing he found out was how much he had lost.

He had assumed — and had said so out loud to Kanzaki, in a yard, in a
tone he was embarrassed about within a fortnight — that five months
away from the crystal was five months of nothing happening, and that
he would pick it up where he had put it down.

He got the three books home from the library on the Friday and opened
the schoolbook at page eleven, which he had read four times in one
year and could have recited, and found that he could not.

Not the words. The words were there. *Cleavage. The specimen decides
where it breaks; the hammer only decides when.*

What had gone was the thing underneath the words — the live sense of
where that sentence connected to eleven other sentences on eleven other
pages, which was the only reason it had ever been useful. He had had a
structure in his head for two years and he had not visited it in five
months and it had gone quiet, and reading the page again produced
recognition rather than thought.

*This is a skill and it decays.*

He wrote that down at the top of a fresh sheet and underlined it once,
because he had not known it and because it was, on the evidence, the
most expensive thing he had learned that year.

*I have been treating the reading as a thing I had done rather than a
thing I do. Nine hundred hours of it between the fifth month of my
sixteenth year and the ninth month of last year, and I stopped, and I
did not notice stopping, because there was no day on which I decided
to stop.*

*Five months. It took five months to go quiet.*

*I do not know how long it takes to come back.*

---

It took eleven days.

He did it the only way he knew, which was badly and by main force: two
hours a night at the bench with the three books and the notebook, from
the ninth hour to the eleventh, whether or not anything came of it,
which for the first six nights nothing did.

On the seventh night he got a connection between the monograph from the
eastern provinces and a passage on annealing that he had read four
times and had never put together, and sat back from it with the
distinct physical sensation of a door in a house he had lived in
opening onto a room he had forgotten.

On the ninth night he had two more.

On the eleventh night he was working at the speed he had worked at
before he stopped, and he checked that against his own dated notes from
the seventh month of the previous year to make sure he was not
flattering himself, and he was not.

*Eleven days to recover five months.*

*Recorded because I will need this figure. It is a much better ratio
than I expected and I want to note that I spent six of those eleven
nights certain it had gone for good and was wrong, and that if I had
stopped on the sixth night I would have concluded, permanently and
incorrectly, that a man cannot go back.*

---

The bench had changed while he was away from it and he had not
registered that either.

He looked at it properly on the twelfth night — actually looked, the
way he looked at other people's benches — and did the inventory.

*Two nights a week for two years of jewellery. The bezel, seven months
and still not right. Ōtaki Sue's brooch. Four things in a tin nobody
has asked for. Kanzaki has asked to see the worst one and I have not
brought her one, which is now nine months of not doing a thing I said
I would do.*

*One night a week of substrate against quota, which is production and
is not work.*

*And no crystal research at all since the ninth month.*

*The bench has a jeweller's roll on it and a set of gravers and a
lamp at jeweller's height and nothing whatsoever for growing anything,
because I have been growing at the hospital in the fabrication room
where the audit is, and I have not grown one gram of anything in this
room in five months.*

He sat with that.

*I have spent two years building a bench for the trade I wanted at nine
and one year using it, and the crystal — which is the only thing about
me that anybody in four countries has ever wanted, and which is the
reason I am alive and housed and employed and in a treaty — has been
happening in a government building on a government tray with a
government clerk weighing the failures.*

*That is not an accident and it is not a complaint. It is the audit and
I agreed to it and I would agree to it again.*

*But I have noticed something and I want it written down.*

*There is no room in this village where I make crystal for no reason.*

---

He worked on that for four evenings and got nowhere with it as a
practical matter, because the answer was obvious and unhelpful: every
gram he grew was audited, the audit was the whole basis of his position,
and an unaudited bench in a rented room was precisely the thing that
would end everything he had.

Kanzaki had told him about the non-quota clause in the fourteenth
month and he had used it exactly once, for a four-year-old's birthday
present, with a chit and a weight and a line in a ruled book.

*Which is the answer and it is not a satisfying one.*

*I can grow whatever I like. I have to write it down first, weigh it
afterwards, and hand the failures to a woman in a leather apron on a
Thursday.*

*I have been treating that as a restriction for three years.*

He stopped, mid-sentence, with the pencil down.

*It is not a restriction. It is a laboratory.*

*Kanzaki Uta has weighed and dated every single thing I have made for
three years, in her hand and mine, with the failures kept in a tin. I
have three hundred ruled lines of longitudinal data on my own output —
mass, reject rate, medium, method, date — collected continuously, by a
disinterested party, to a standard I could not possibly have maintained
on my own.*

*Nobody in the history of this bloodline has ever had that. My family
kept an order book.*

He got up and stood in the middle of the room for a while.

Then he sat back down and wrote, on a fresh sheet, at the top:

*QUESTIONS FOR THE THURSDAY LEDGER*

and underneath it, the first one:

*Does the medium matter?*

*I have three years of entries. Every one records what I grew and what
it weighed. Do any of them record what I grew it* from?

He knew the answer before he finished writing the question, because he
had signed every one of those sheets.

They did.

Kanzaki had put a column for source medium on the form in the second
month of his first year, unprompted, because she was a quartermaster
and quartermasters record where things come from, and he had filled it
in three hundred times without ever once looking down the column.
