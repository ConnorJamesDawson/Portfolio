# Chapter 43, Scene 1 — Eleven People

*Act 4, part D. Konoha General, the committee room. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,778 words.*

---

The first properly warm day of the year fell on the ninth, and the
committee room faced south, and nobody had thought about that when the
room was assigned in the winter.

It smelled of floor polish and of the cut irises somebody put in the
alcove every Monday out of their own money, and by the second hour of
the afternoon it smelled of eleven people in a room with the windows
shut, because the windows faced the delivery yard and the delivery yard
had a cart on it. Somebody's fan went the whole way through. Chairs on
a floor that had been polished the same way for forty years and had
gone pale in two arcs where the doors swung.

Sazare had prepared for nine days.

He had eleven copies of a protocol, collated and clipped, with the
tables at the back rather than in the body because Kanzaki had told him
that a table in the body is a table an angry man can stop on. He had
Yagi's countersignature. He had Emiko's ward figures for fourteen
months. He had the four media and the reject rates and he had rehearsed
the whole of it twice at a bench with nobody in the room, and it had
run eighteen minutes both times.

He got four minutes into it and lost the room.

---

He lost it because he led with the finding.

He knew it was a mistake at about the ninth word and could not get out
of the sentence he was in, and by the time he had finished it, the
thing standing in front of eleven people was not a study design. It was
a claim about how healing worked, made by a man of eighteen with two
years in the building, and every single person in that room had between
nine and forty years in it and had been taught something else.

"Stop," said Ōkubo, from the far end.

Ōkubo Hidetsugu ran general surgery and had run it for eleven years. A
big man gone soft at the middle in the way of somebody who had been
strong and was now mostly tired, in a coat pressed by a person who
loved him and cuffs frayed at the button because the pressing and the
mending were different jobs and only one of them was being done. Reading
glasses pushed up. Hands broad, scrubbed raw across the backs, a
surgeon's nails, and a tremor in the left that was not yet anything and
would be within four years.

"You're telling this room," Ōkubo said, "that when my patients do
badly, it's because the patients did badly."

"No."

"That's what I heard."

"That's what I said." Sazare put the sheet down. "It isn't what I
meant, and the difference between those is my fault and not yours."

Somebody at the middle of the table made a small noise.

"I led with the conclusion because the conclusion is the interesting
part to me," he said, "and I have just found out in front of eleven
people that the interesting part and the part you can hear are not the
same part, and that leading with one costs you the other."

"That's very candid," said Ōkubo, "and it doesn't get the sentence
back."

"No. It doesn't."

---

He got the protocol out and ran it properly from the front, and it
went better and not well, and at the eleventh minute he made the second
mistake, which was worse and entirely his own.

Ōkubo had a figure wrong.

It was in the objection rather than in anything that mattered — the
board's own historical outcome data for clean-margin grafts, quoted
from memory, and the man had said *sixty-two per cent* and the number
in the book was fifty-eight, and the four points did not change his
argument in any direction at all.

Sazare heard it and corrected it.

He corrected it flatly and immediately and out loud, the way he had
corrected the gate barrier in Natsumi's diagram and the camber on a
street plan and about nine hundred other things, because a wrong number
sitting where he could see it had been physically uncomfortable to him
since he was seven years old and he had never once managed to leave one
standing.

The room went very quiet.

He heard the whole shape of it afterwards, in order: the correction, a
chair, the fan, and eleven people deciding in about two seconds what
kind of young man this was.

"Fifty-eight," said Ōkubo.

"Yes."

"You're sure."

"Page four hundred and eleven. It's fifty-eight point one and it's a
five-year rolling figure and it's yours — you published it."

"I did publish it," Ōkubo said. "In the year you were nine."

---

The board went against him.

It went against him seven to four with the chair abstaining, and the
objection that carried was not the one about patients being blamed and
not the one about his age. It was Sister Kaburagi's, delivered in
ninety seconds without a note in front of her, and it was better than
anything he had prepared for.

"Half of your practitioners work in silence," she said. "You've written
the consent form for the patient. I want to know who writes it for the
practitioner. You're asking six of my staff to stand at a bedside and
withhold the only thing they've got that costs the hospital nothing,
and then you're asking them to go home. And you have not put one line
in this document about what that does to them over nine months, because
it did not occur to you, because you have never been the person in the
room."

He did not have an answer.

He had four answers and all of them were about the data and none of
them were about the six people, and he could feel each one of them
being wrong before it reached his mouth, and for once in his life he
managed to say nothing at all.

"I withdraw it," he said, eventually. "Not for redrafting. I don't have
the design. I'd want to bring the six of them into the writing of it
before I bring anything back to this room, and I'd want them to be able
to stop it."

Kaburagi looked at him for a moment.

"Noted," she said, and wrote it down.

---

He was at the bottom of the south stair with the eleven unread copies
under his arm when he heard Ōkubo coming down behind him, which took
some time, because Ōkubo came down stairs one at a time now and did not
like to be watched doing it.

Sazare stopped and did not turn round and did not offer.

"Page four hundred and eleven," Ōkubo said, at the bottom.

"Yes."

"Nobody has corrected me in that room in eleven years." He got his
glasses down off his head and looked at them and did not put them on.
"I want you to understand that I'm not telling you that as a
grievance. I'm telling you it as a fact about the room. I have been
wrong in that room, out loud, at least forty times, and it has been
allowed to stand at least forty times, and about a third of those went
into somebody's notes and out into the wards, and I have found two of
them myself, years late, and I've no idea what happened to the rest."

Sazare turned round.

"I embarrassed you."

"Yes. Thoroughly, and in front of Kaburagi, who will not forget it and
who will be perfectly pleasant about it, which is worse." Ōkubo folded
the glasses. "And I have been standing at the top of that stair for
four minutes working out which of the two things I am, and the answer
is that I am fifty-six years old and my hands have about four years in
them and there is nobody left in this hospital who will tell me a
number is wrong."

"That's not—"

"Let me finish, I've come down a flight for it." He put the glasses in
his pocket. "Your study is badly built and Kaburagi's right and you
were right to withdraw it. Build it again with the six of them in the
room. And when you have, put me down as one of the six."

Sazare did not say anything for a moment.

"You voted against it."

"I voted against the document. It was a bad document." Ōkubo started
along the corridor, and then stopped, with one hand on the wall in a
way that he clearly believed looked casual. "Also — and I'd take it as
a courtesy if this went no further — I have not been in a study since
I was thirty-one, and every year since then somebody has explained to
me why I'm too senior to be in one, and I have agreed with them every
single time, and I would like to go into a room again where somebody
is allowed to tell me I'm wrong."

---

He wrote it up that evening, both halves, and got to the honest one and
sat with it a long time, because the arithmetic came out in a direction
he did not trust.

*Two mistakes in one afternoon. Both entirely mine. Both avoidable and
both of a kind I have made before and written down before.*

*Mistake one lost me the room in nine words. Mistake two humiliated a
man of fifty-six in front of his colleagues over four percentage points
that changed nothing.*

*And the second one produced the best thing that happened today.*

He looked at that sentence for some while.

*I do not know what to do with that. I have kept fault reports for
eleven years and every single one of them has one column, which is what
went wrong and what I will do instead. There has never been a column
for what the fault produced, because it did not occur to me that a
fault could produce anything except a worse outcome and a lesson.*

*Ōkubo did not follow me down a flight of stairs because of my
protocol. He followed me because I embarrassed him accurately.*

*I would like to be careful here. There is a version of this that
becomes an excuse, and I can see it from where I am standing.*

He ruled a second column anyway, and headed it, and left it empty for
the moment, because he had nothing honest to put in it yet and a
heading with nothing under it was still a truer document than a page
that pretended the day had only cost him.
