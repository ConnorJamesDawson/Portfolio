# Chapter 8, Scene 2 — No

*Act 1, beat 21 continued. Sazare Ruri, age thirteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

She was alive.

That was the only thing in his head for what felt like a very long time and was
probably four seconds. Not *she is on a platform*. Not *they are going to*. Just:
she is alive, she has been alive this entire time, she was alive when I was in the
cutting and she was alive when I was at the counter and she was alive every single
one of the four hundred and thirty nights I have—

She was thin in a way he had never seen a person be thin.

---

He got two rows forward. He did it without any awareness of doing it and he did it
by shoving, which he had never once done in fourteen months of being invisible in
crowds, and a man said something angry above his head and he did not hear it.

Four rows back from the barrier. Perhaps eleven yards from the platform.

His mother was looking at the ground in front of her feet, and her face had
nothing on it at all — not fear, not defiance, nothing; it was the face she wore at
frame six, and he understood at once and with total certainty that she had put it
on deliberately and had been wearing it for some time.

His father was looking at the crowd. He was looking at all of it, everywhere, fast,
the way he asked people three questions without waiting for the answers, and his
mouth was moving very slightly, and Sazare could not tell whether he was praying or
counting.

Iwao had his chin up and was looking at the top of the square, at nothing, at the
sky over the ice-house, and had arranged himself so that he was standing between
Sazare's mother and the nearest guard, which achieved absolutely nothing and which
he had presumably been doing for fourteen months.

And Kohaku was going along the crowd.

---

She found him.

It took her about thirty seconds and it was not luck; she went along the front
rows and then the second and then the third the way you read a page, and Sazare
stood there in the fourth row with his heart going and did not wave and did not
shout, because some part of him that was still working knew better, and waited.

Her eyes came along the row and stopped.

Neither of them did anything for about two seconds.

She had a black eye that was three weeks old and going yellow at the edge and one
side of her mouth had healed wrong. The earring was gone. He looked for it before
he looked at anything else and it was gone, and there was a torn place in the lobe
where it had come out or been taken out, and it had healed, so it had been a long
time ago.

Then his sister shook her head.

Once. Very slightly. Not a plea and not a warning — just the flat small shake she
had given him a hundred times over a cloth roll on a step in the dark, when he had
put two chips together that were not cousins and was about to say so.

*No.*

He knew exactly what it meant. It meant *that's the wrong one*. It meant *put it
back*. It meant *don't*.

Then she looked away from him, deliberately, and went on going along the crowd as
though she had not found anything, and did not look at him again.

---

The officials did something with the folder.

They were checking. That was what it was; two of them were checking the schedule
against the nine people on the platform, and one of them walked along the line and
back, and then they conferred, and the old one nodded, and the man with the folder
made a mark.

It took perhaps three minutes.

Three minutes, in the sun, in front of four hundred people, while somebody counted.

The two women near Sazare had stopped talking about the marriage. The whole square
had gone into a state he had never experienced before and would recognise
instantly for the rest of his life: four hundred people all being extremely
careful about what their faces were doing.

Somebody at the back laughed at something and it went off like a dropped plate and
stopped.

---

He tried to think and could not.

He had, at that moment, the following: nine sen, a cloth roll of thirty-four
worthless offcuts, a coat he had stolen in the second month, and no plan whatsoever
of any kind.

He had never had a plan. He understood that now with a completeness that made him
feel sick. He had come to *look*. That was the whole of it, that had been the whole
of it for eight days, and there had never once been a next part, because he had not
allowed there to be one, because if there was a next part then he had to decide
what it was and he was thirteen and there was nothing he could do.

There was nothing he could do.

He went through it, standing there, in about ninety seconds, the way you go
through a list.

He could shout. Four hundred people, sixty yards of barrier, twenty-odd men, and
he would be face-down on the cobbles before he got to the second word, and it
would change nothing, and it would tell everybody on that platform exactly what
his mother had spent fourteen months making sure nobody knew, which was that there
was a fifth one and he was in the square.

He could run at the barrier. See above.

He could do — nothing. He could stand in the fourth row with four hundred other
people and watch, and go on being invisible, which was what every single adult who
had ever loved him had spent his entire life telling him to do. *Show them your
second best. Tell them you make earrings. Keep your head down. Don't come past the
screen. Don't ask about houses.*

She had shaken her head at him.

She had looked out at four hundred people and found him in about thirty seconds
and used the last thing she was ever going to say to him to tell him to put it
back.

---

The executioner came up the steps at about a quarter past the fourth hour.

He was not what Sazare had spent eight days flinching from. He was about
forty-five, and stocky, and he came up the steps carrying a sword like a man
carrying a tool up a ladder, and he had a cloth through his belt, and when he got
to the top he said something to the nearest official and the official pointed at
the line and said something back, and the executioner nodded and moved two of the
nine along by a pace with his hand flat, the way you move a chair.

He was not cruel. There was nothing in his face at all. He had done this before
and he was going to do it again next month and he was, Sazare realised, thinking
about the geometry — where he was going to stand, which way the light was, what
order made the least mess on a platform they would have to take down afterwards.

He arranged the nine of them.

He put Sazare's mother third.

---

Afterwards, when he had had eleven years to look at it, Sazare could still not
find the moment.

That was what he wanted and never got: the point at which he decided. He wanted
there to have been a second in which he thought *I am going to do this*, because a
second like that would have been something he could have argued with, then or ever.

There wasn't one.

What there was, was this.

The executioner set his feet.

Sazare's mother lifted her head, once, and looked at the top of the square, over
everybody, at the same nothing Iwao had been looking at.

And Sazare's hands came up.

Not as a decision. They came up the way they came up over a frame — the exact
motion, forty thousand mornings of it, both hands out and open with the heels
turned slightly in, which was how you started, which was how everybody in his
family had started for four hundred years.

He had not made anything since he was twelve years old.

He found the place where it was moving.

And for the first time in his life, he interfered.
