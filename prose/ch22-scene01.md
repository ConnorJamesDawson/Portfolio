# Chapter 22, Scene 1 — The Gate

*Act 4, part A. The road, and Konoha. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~2,250 words.*

---

He saw the mountain on the tenth day and did not understand it for about
four seconds.

They had come up out of a river valley all morning through country that
kept getting richer — proper metalled road, milestones, a toll house with
a bored man in it who waved Kakashi through without looking up, and then
farms, and then farms with tile on the roofs instead of thatch — and at
about the eleventh hour the road came round the shoulder of a hill and
the whole of it opened out below them.

A bowl of forest perhaps four miles across, hazed blue with distance and
woodsmoke. A wall running round the near side of it, grey, with a gate in
it that Sazare took at first for a fortification of some kind and then
revised, because it was too big to be a fortification.

And behind the town, standing up out of the far side of the bowl, a cliff
face about four hundred feet high with four human faces cut into it.

He stopped in the road.

They were *enormous*. Each head was sixty feet from chin to hairline. They
had been cut back into the living rock — not built out, cut *in*, which
meant somebody had started with a mountain and removed everything that was
not a face, four times, and had got the eyes right, and the mouths, and
the fall of one man's hair.

"How long did that take?" he said.

Kakashi, thirty feet back, glanced up as though he had not looked at it in
some years.

"The first one? Nobody's sure. Twenty years, maybe. They do the new ones
faster now; there's a technique for it." A pause. "That's the sort of
question I'd keep to yourself for a bit, by the way. Not because it's a
bad question. Because it isn't the one anybody expects."

"What do people ask?"

"Whether it's true they can see out of the eyes," said Kakashi. "It isn't.
Come on."

---

Sazare walked down that hill in the sun and could not stop looking at it,
and did the arithmetic all the way, because he could not not.

Twenty years. On a *cliff*. Which is to say: twenty years of a country
having enough spare men, spare food and spare quiet to put a hundred
people on scaffolding a hundred feet up for two decades to carve the head
of a dead man into a mountain, where it would be seen by everybody, for
ever, on purpose.

He had come from a country that had built exactly one permanent thing in
his lifetime and it had been a wall round a district. The Ruri compound
had gone from a house to a burnt lot in a night and the lot had been sold
inside a year. There was, so far as he knew, nothing at all left anywhere
on this earth to say that his family had existed, except four names on a
schedule in a filing office and a line in a bounty book.

And here was a nation that carved its old men into a hillside.

*They're not soft,* he thought, on that road, at sixteen, with his pack
straps cutting into him. *That's the wrong word entirely and I've had it
in my head for four years because a woman I trusted was given it to say.*

*They're* rich. *They've been safe long enough to be rich, and long enough
to spend the surplus on something that has no use whatever, and that's not
softness, that's what you look like when nobody has been able to get at
you for sixty years.*

---

The gate was a hundred and fifty feet across and about seventy high, and
it stood open.

That was the thing that did for him and he had not seen it coming.

He had spent six days on that road building the arrival in his head:
guards, a challenge, a barrier, papers — and what was actually there, on a
Thursday afternoon in the eleventh month, was two enormous wooden leaves
standing open against the wall with the paint peeling off the bottom of
them, and a stream of ordinary people going in and out under them, and a
gatehouse to one side with a desk in it.

Carts. A woman with four goats and an opinion about them. Two men carrying
a rolled carpet. A boy of about ten going out at a dead run with a
message tube on his hip. A queue of nine people at the desk, moving.

At the desk, two men in their twenties in green flak jackets, and one of
them was eating.

Konoha did not check him at the gate, in the sense he had understood
checking his whole life. There was a book. There was a stamp. The man not
eating looked at Kakashi and said, "Sir," and then looked at Sazare and
said, "Name?" and wrote it down; and the whole of Sazare's careful
planning, the sleeves and the hands and the four days of rehearsal,
arrived in a wooden hut and was received by a man with a rice ball.

Then Kakashi said, quietly, "He's an intake. I need the duty officer,"
and everything changed in about nine seconds.

---

He was in a holding room fourteen minutes later and he did not see Kakashi
again for six days.

That happened at the doorway of the gatehouse annexe and it was the
cleanest thing anybody did to him in Konoha and he was grateful for it
afterwards, in the way you are grateful for a well-set bone.

"Right," said Kakashi. "This is me."

"I know."

"You'll be in there a while and it will be extremely boring and then it
will be extremely unpleasant and then it will be boring again, and none of
it is a signal. It's a process. It happens to everybody and it is not
being done *to* you specifically." He had his hands in his pockets and the
one visible eye was doing the thing it did. "Three pieces of advice and
then I'm going to go and file a report about a bridge.

"One. Answer what you're asked. Don't help. Don't fill silences; they're
deliberate and there's a man on the other side of them being paid to leave
them there.

"Two. When you don't know something, say *I don't know*. They can work with
that. They cannot work with a man who is never uncertain about anything,
and neither can I, and neither can you."

"And three?"

"Three." Kakashi looked at him for a moment. "You've made a habit of
telling people true things that make you look worse than you have to. It
worked on me. It will not work in there, because in there it isn't a
character reference, it's just data, and it goes in a file that stays open
for the rest of your life."

He turned to go.

"Do it anyway," he said, over his shoulder. "But know what it costs before
you spend it, and don't spend it to feel better."

---

The room was eleven feet by nine with a table, two chairs, a bench along
one wall, a barred window at head height showing a rectangle of sky, and a
bucket in the corner behind a screen, and it was clean.

That was the second thing that undid him and it took him three days to
work out why.

He had been in worse rooms. He had slept under a bramble screen in the
rain for two days at Kasado, and in a lime kiln with two broken ribs, and
eleven hours in six inches of run-off with his mouth against the crown of
a pipe. This room had a floor that had been swept in the last day and a
blanket that had been washed and a jug of water that got changed twice
without his asking, and once, on the second afternoon, somebody put a bowl
of something hot through the hatch that had actual meat in it.

Nobody hit him. Nobody threatened him. Nobody said anything at all for two
and a half days except a man at a hatch saying *food* and *jug* and
*bucket*, in that order, three times a day, in a completely neutral voice.

It was the neutrality. That was what he eventually got to, sitting on the
bench with his back to the wall on the third morning: the entire apparatus
of that place was pointed at him and none of it was *interested* in him,
and he had spent four years being the most interesting thing in every room
he entered — the boy with the price, the mender who mustn't be asked, the
foreigner in the doorway — and it turned out that being processed by
people who did not care was a completely new sensation and that he did not
know what to do with his hands.

They took the coat on the first hour and gave him a receipt.

An actual receipt, on a printed form, with a number on it and a
counterfoil, made out to *Ruri, S.*, itemising: **one coat, brown wool,
lining opened by bearer at request; three hundred and sixty thousand ryō
in mixed denominations, counted in bearer's presence, sealed bag no.
4471; one medical roll, contents listed overleaf; one picture frame,
lacquer, empty, damaged, repaired.**

He sat on a bench in a cell in a foreign country for three days with that
piece of paper in his hands and read it about forty times, and the line he
kept coming back to was the last one, because some clerk had looked at an
empty broken picture frame with a larch fillet in the split, and had not
thrown it away, and had not asked, and had written down what it was.

---

On the fourth day the door opened and it stopped being boring.

The man who came in was perhaps forty and was the most physically
frightening human being Sazare had ever been in a room with, and he had
been in a room with Zabuza Momochi.

He was big — not tall; *thick*, through the neck and the shoulders and the
wrists — in a black coat over the green jacket, and he wore a dark bandana
tied down over his skull instead of a headband, and his face was a map of
old surgery. There were two long puckered lines running down it from the
hairline, and burn-scar across one cheek and down into the collar, and one
of his ears had been repaired by somebody good, and none of it had been
done in a fight; Sazare had two years of a country doctor in him and he
knew instantly, and could not stop knowing, that every single mark on that
head had been made slowly by somebody with time.

He sat down opposite and put a folder on the table and did not open it.

"Morino Ibiki," he said. "Torture and Interrogation Force. That's my
actual job title and I say it in the first ten seconds because people
spend the whole hour wondering."

"All right," said Sazare.

"You're not being tortured. Nobody in this building is going to lay a hand
on you, and if anybody does you tell me and I will be extremely
interested." He put his forearms on the table. "I'm going to talk to you
for about six hours a day for as long as it takes. You'll get fed and
you'll get slept and every so often I'll say something calculated to
frighten you and you'll know I'm doing it and I'll know you know, and it
will work anyway, because it always does, and that's not an insult, it's
just the trade."

He tilted his head very slightly.

"Now. Before we start. What are you?"

Sazare had thought that one was going to come later.

"Sixteen. Kirigakure, lower district. Never served. In the book, alive
only. Six killings for money in the border country between the ninth month
of last year and the eleventh, and one before that when I was thirteen
which is the one on the entry."

Ibiki did not move.

"Six," he said.

"Six."

"Kakashi's report says four."

"Kakashi's report says what the coast says," said Sazare, "and the coast
only knows about the four that were talked about, and I told him I'd
answer anything I was asked and I'm answering. There were six. I'll give
you all of them: where, when, who paid, what I was told and what I worked
out afterwards. I can do names for two of them. I made a point of not
knowing the rest and I'll tell you exactly how I arranged that, because
it's the part that will interest you most and it's the part I'm least
proud of."

The room was very quiet.

Ibiki Morino sat back in his chair and looked at a sixteen-year-old with a
bad knee for what felt like a long time, and something moved across the
ruin of that face that was not sympathy and was not triumph, and Sazare
could not read it at all.

"Well," he said at last, and opened the folder. "That's going to save us
both about four days."
