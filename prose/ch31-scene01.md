# Chapter 31, Scene 1 — Two Signatures

*Act 4, part C. Konoha General. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare.
~1,540 words.*

---

The medic Tsunade sent to clear his chakra pathways was a man of about
sixty called Ubukata who arrived on a Tuesday with no file, no
assistant, and an expression of settled indifference that Sazare
eventually understood to be the entire point of him.

He was thin in the way that men get thin in their fifties and stay,
with a long neck and a considerable amount of loose skin under the jaw,
and grey hair cut extremely short all over in a way that suggested he
did it himself with clippers and no mirror. He wore no forehead
protector. His jacket was medical-corps issue, made for a broader man,
and had been taken in at both side seams — not by a tailor; the stitch
run wandered — and the shoulders still sat an inch wide, so that when
he moved his arms the cloth moved a fraction after them.

He walked with his weight back, heels first, unhurried, taking short
steps for a tall man, and did not look at anything on the ward.

"Arms on the table," he said. "Both. Sleeves up past the elbow."

"Should I—"

"No. You should put your arms on the table."

Sazare put his arms on the table.

Ubukata worked for eleven minutes without speaking. His hands were very
cold and moved along the forearms in short passes, wrist to elbow and
back, the diagnostic chakra so faint that Sazare could barely feel it —
which he recognised, after the second pass, as skill rather than
weakness. He had watched Yagi do the same thing with about four times
the output.

"Who was the Hyūga," Ubukata said at last.

"Hyūga Natsumi."

"Her report says one hundred and eight pathways and division after
exit. Is that a figure you agree with?"

"I didn't count. I couldn't."

"Good." He turned the left arm over and went along the inside of it.
"People who agree with the impressive number are usually agreeing with
the number."

He let go and sat back.

"You have abrasion in both arms, more in the left, consistent with a
single very large event followed by four months of a person doing
approximately sixty per cent of what he was told." He said it without
any weight whatsoever, the way a man reads out a delivery docket.
"The chest and throat leakage has resolved. The arms have not. They
are healing and they will continue to heal and I would put the finish
somewhere between four months and never, and I am not going to pretend
to you that I can tell which."

"Never?"

"Some of these don't come all the way back. That's not a prediction
about you. It's the honest range." Ubukata took a form out of his
jacket, unfolded it on the table and wrote on it standing up. "I'm
clearing you conditionally. Medical fabrication, yes. The ward, yes.
Anything in the evaluation, yes, with a ceiling I'm going to write on
here in a number rather than a word, because words get interpreted and
numbers get argued with, and arguing is more honest."

"What number?"

"About a third of what you did on that roof. If you go over it you will
know, because your hands will stop answering, and if that happens
during the evaluation you will stop and say so, and if you don't say
so I will hear about it and I will withdraw this and it will not come
back for a year." He signed it. "Do you understand the difference
between a ceiling and a challenge?"

"Yes."

"Say it."

"A ceiling is a number I don't go over. A challenge is a number I find
out about."

"Correct." Ubukata folded the form and handed it to him rather than
to the desk, which Sazare noticed and did not understand until later.
"Fujita has the knee. I'm told he's already signed."

"He signed on Friday."

"Then that's your two." He stood, and picked up a coat that had been
made for a broader man as well, and put it on. "One more thing, and
this is not medical and you may ignore it. I have read what you did on
that street. I have also read the note where you priced yourself at
about a third of what everyone else in the report thinks you're worth,
and I have been doing this for thirty-four years, and I would like to
say that I found the second one considerably more interesting."

He left before Sazare had assembled a response.

---

The competency examination result came four days later, in a sealed
envelope, hand-delivered by a clerk who waited for a signature and
then left without giving him time to open it in front of anyone.

He opened it on the stairs.

It was two sheets. The first was a grade table, six subjects, marked
out of a hundred, with a line at the bottom that read *this document
is a description and not a permission* in what he was fairly sure was
Tsunade's own hand.

*Medical chakra control — 94.*
*Wound management — 88.*
*Triage and casualty systems — 71.*
*Anatomy — 46.*
*Pharmacology — 38.*
*Infection and contamination — 31.*

He sat down on the stairs.

Somebody came past going up and said something to him that he did not
hear and answered without knowing what he had said.

*Thirty-one.*

The second sheet was the examiner's commentary and it was worse and
better. *Candidate demonstrates exceptional control, exceptional
procedural discipline and an unusually accurate sense of the boundary
of his own competence.* Then, further down: *Candidate's understanding
of contamination is not merely incomplete but actively wrong in three
particulars, each of which he has been operating on for some months.
Candidate believes a clean-looking wound in a clean-looking patient is
a clean wound. Candidate does not distinguish between debridement and
tidying. Candidate has closed at least two wounds that ought to have
been left open, and has been fortunate.*

And at the bottom, underlined once: *None of the above should be read
as a criticism of the candidate. It is a criticism of an education
that did not exist.*

He read the whole thing four times on the stairs.

Then he did something that he could not, afterwards, entirely account
for, which was that he went and found a pencil and wrote *31* on the
inside cover of his notebook, under Fujita's ceiling and under
*Auditor: K.U.*, and then wrote beside it:

*Two wounds. Which two.*

He spent the rest of that day going back through four months of
observation sheets, and found them both by the ninth hour, and neither
of them had gone bad, and both of them could have, and one of them was
bed six who had gone home in the third month with a hand that worked.

---

Handa asked, because Handa always asked.

"Well?"

"Thirty-one in infection."

"Out of?"

"A hundred."

Handa whistled, which took him longer than it used to and was a fact
about the pelvis rather than the whistle.

"That's a proper failure, that is."

"Yes."

"You look pleased."

"I don't think I'm pleased." Sazare turned the sheet over on his knee.
"I've been wrong about contamination for seven months. I've been wrong
about it *specifically*, in three ways, and I've been wrong in the same
three ways every single time because I taught myself out of a book that
didn't cover it and there was nobody to tell me."

"And now there's a piece of paper telling you."

"There's a piece of paper telling *everybody*." He looked at the grade
table again. "That's the part. Kaburagi will read this. Fujita will
read this. If I'm on a floor and somebody hands me a dirty wound,
there's now a document in this building that says don't let him close
it."

"And that's good?"

"It's the first time in my life anybody's written down what I'm bad at."
He folded the sheet along its own crease. "They've written down what
I'm worth four times. Kiri did it twice. This is different and I don't
have anything to compare it to."

Handa was quiet for a while. Out on the ward a trolley went past —
still clicking, still the same bearing, and Sazare had by now been
promoted twice past the level at which he was permitted to simply go
and fix it, and had fixed it anyway on three separate occasions, and
it had come back each time.

"My master used to write his apprentices' faults on the shop wall,"
Handa said eventually. "In chalk. Where the customers could see them."

"That seems cruel."

"It was bloody horrible. And every one of us learned to cut a dovetail
that didn't show, because the alternative was your name on a wall in
front of a woman buying a dresser." He shifted an inch up the pillow.
"He wrote his own up there too, mind. Top of the list. *Rushes the
finish.*"

"Did he?"

"Every time. Forty years. Never fixed it." Handa closed his eyes.
"Knowing it and fixing it aren't the same job, is what I'm telling you.
Don't come back in a fortnight telling me you've read a book about
infection and it's sorted."
