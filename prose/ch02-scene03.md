# Chapter 2, Scene 3 — The Cage

*Act 1, beat 8. Sazare Ruri, age nine. POV: Sazare. ~3,500 words.*

---

It started in the night and it was not, at first, anything.

Sazare woke because the frames had stopped. That was all. Nine frames humming in
the next room was the sound the house made instead of silence, and at some point
in the small hours it had gone, and the absence of it brought him up out of sleep
the way a hand on the shoulder would have.

He lay there.

The frames had stopped because his mother had stopped, and his mother did not stop
in the middle of a night's work, ever, for anything, including the year Kohaku had
come home with her arm in a splint.

He heard the front of the house. Not voices — the door, being opened, and then
not being closed.

He got up.

---

They were all out in the yard: his mother with her sleeves still pinned, his
father with a coat over his nightclothes, Iwao in the gateway with his hand on the
gatepost, all three of them facing the same direction, which was east, towards the
upper districts and the wall.

There was noise coming from over there.

It was not a great deal of noise. That was the thing Sazare would remember about
it afterwards — that it was so much less than you would expect. It was a low
irregular knocking, a long way off, like somebody breaking up a boat with a maul
on the other side of the harbour, and every so often a sound that was not a
knocking and did not have a shape.

The fog took most of it. The fog took everything.

"Go back to bed," said his father, without turning round.

"What is it?"

"Sazare. Bed."

"Is it a *fire*—"

"It's not a fire," said Iwao from the gate. His voice was completely level. "Go
in, boy."

He went in. He did not go to bed. He sat on the step inside the door with his back
against the wall, where he could hear them, and after a while his mother came in
and sat down next to him, which she had never once done, and put her arm round him
and did not say anything at all.

They sat there.

The knocking went on for about two hours. Then it stopped.

---

In the morning the fog had gone and the sky was hard and clear and everything
looked scoured.

His father said nobody was going anywhere, and then went out himself for an hour,
and came back and said something quietly to Sazare's mother in the workshop
doorway, and she said "*all* of them?" and he said something else, and she went
and sat down on the trestle at the end of the row and put her hands on her knees.

"You can go to the Academy," his father said. "It's open. They've said it's
open."

"Ren."

"It's *open*, Hisui. What are we doing if we keep him home."

His mother did not answer that.

Sazare went to the Academy.

---

The route ran down through the fish market and along the cutting and up the far
side, and it was the same route it had been the day before, and everything on it
was different in a way he could not have listed.

The market was busy. That was normal. The noise it made was not: it was lower and
faster and it stopped in patches, so that you would come round a corner into six
people not talking, and then round the next one into everybody talking at once.

There were shinobi on the bridges. There were not usually shinobi on the bridges.
They were not doing anything — they were standing, in pairs, at the bridgeheads,
and they were not stopping anybody, and everybody who went past them adjusted
their pace very slightly on the way through and then adjusted it back.

At the third bridge, the stall was shut.

Sazare had never seen the stall shut. He stood in front of it for slightly longer
than made sense, looking at the boards over the front of it, and then went on.

---

Jirō had the whole thing before the first hour.

Jirō had four older siblings and a mother who worked in the laundry that did the
barracks, and this made him, at nine, the best-informed person Sazare would know
for years to come.

"It was a clan," he said. "It was a whole clan. They came in over the east wall in
the night and they went straight up the hill and they were *trying to get to the
Mizukage*."

"Which clan?"

"Kaguya."

"Never heard of them."

"Nor had I," said Jirō. "Nor's anyone. They live out past the—" he waved a hand
eastwards, "—out that way. They don't come in. They've never come in. And they all
came in at once last night, all of them, everyone, and they went up the hill."

"Why?"

"That's the thing," said Jirō. "There isn't a why."

"There's always a why."

"There isn't." He leaned in. "My mum's been up since four doing the sheets and
they've all been in there talking about it. Nobody's asked them for anything.
Nobody's taken anything off them. They weren't *at war* with anyone. They just came
in and started killing people and they didn't stop and they didn't ask for
anything and they didn't try to get out again." He sat back. "They wanted to."

"Wanted to what?"

"*That's it*," said Jirō. "That's the whole thing. They wanted to."

Sazare thought about the low irregular knocking going on for two hours in the
fog.

"How many?" he said.

"Dunno. Loads."

"How many of *them*?"

"All of them," said Jirō. "Every single one. They killed the lot."

---

Mizuho taught, that day, as though nothing at all had happened.

She did the Second War treaty again, because the year below had come up into the
room for the morning and she had to do it twice a year and this was the second
time; and she did all four men, and she did the long pause, and this time nobody
cried, because the room was not in a state to be moved by anything.

At the end she said, "Bowls out. Six minutes."

Sazare could not make the water still. He could not get anywhere near it. He sat
with his hands either side of the bowl for six minutes watching the surface crawl
and could not find the place where it was moving, because it was moving
everywhere, and at the end of it Mizuho went past his desk and looked and said
nothing at all, which was worse than anything she could have said.

---

He saw it on the way home.

The route back went along the cutting and there were carts coming down the other
way, four of them, going west towards the harbour, moving at the pace of a walking
man. Everybody on the walkway had stopped to let them by. Nobody was saying
anything.

The first three carts had the dead on them under oilcloth, badly covered, because
there had not been enough oilcloth. Sazare looked at the first one and then did not
look at the other two.

The fourth cart had a box on it.

It was about the size of a large dog kennel and it was made of iron bar, and it was
lashed to the bed of the cart with rope at the four corners, and there was a boy
in it.

He was younger than Sazare. Six, or seven — hard to say, because he was sitting
with his knees up and his back against the bars and he was very thin. He had white
hair, cut anyhow, and two red marks on his forehead like a pair of small round
seals, and he was not doing any of the things a person in a cage does. He was not
shouting. He was not holding the bars. He was not crying, or asleep, or hurt in
any way Sazare could see.

He was sitting there with his hands in his lap looking out at the walkway, and as
the cart came past he looked at the row of stopped people, all of them, one after
another, the way you look out of a window on a journey — and his eyes went over
Sazare and did not stop, and went on to the next person, and the cart went by.

A woman near Sazare said, "God."

"That's the one," said a man. "That's the one they got out."

"He's a *child*."

"That's the one they got out," the man said again, as though she had disagreed
with him.

The carts went on down towards the harbour and everybody started walking again.

---

"Why is he in a cage?"

"Sazare."

"He's *smaller than me*—"

"Sazare," said his father, and put both hands flat on the table, which he did
perhaps twice a year. "I don't know. I don't know why he's in a cage. I don't know
who he is. I don't know why any of it happened. Nobody in this house knows and
nobody in this street knows and I'd take it kindly if you'd stop asking, just for
tonight."

Sazare stopped asking.

The meal went on. His mother had not said anything since he had come in. Iwao ate
steadily and looked at nobody.

Then Iwao said, to his bowl: "They'll be tightening things up."

"Don't."

"I'm only saying what's true, Ren."

"You're saying it at the table."

"There's a boy at this table who's going to be a shinobi in two years," said
Iwao, "and he saw a cart today, and I'd rather he heard it from me than off that
friend of his." He put his chopsticks down. "They'll be tightening things up.
There'll be lists. There'll be men coming round asking what's in your house."

"Iwao."

"And it won't be about us," Iwao said, and it was the first time Sazare had ever
heard him raise his voice by even that much, "because it never *is* about us,
because we sell to the quartermaster and the quartermaster likes us. So we'll be
all right. That's what I'm telling him. We'll be all right."

There was a pause.

"Right," said his father. "Well. That's that, then."

Sazare's mother got up and started clearing, although nobody had finished.

---

He lay awake for a long time that night and the frames hummed, all nine of them,
none of them quite in tune, and eventually he got up and went and stood in the
doorway of the workshop.

His mother was at the end of the row with her hands flat on frame six and her
forearms not moving at all.

"Go to bed."

"I can't."

"I know," she said. "Go anyway."

He didn't. He sat down on the step where he always sat.

After a while she took her hands off the frame and shook them out.

"The boy in the box," she said.

"You weren't there."

"Your father told me."

Sazare pulled his knees up.

"He looked at me," he said.

"Did he."

"He looked at everyone. He wasn't — he wasn't upset. He was just *looking*." He
put his chin on his knees. "Why wasn't he upset?"

His mother was quiet for a bit.

"Because he's used to it," she said.

"Used to being in a box?"

"Used to something."

She put her hands back on the frame and the hum shifted very slightly and steadied
again, and Sazare sat on the step and watched her forearms not move, which was the
most reliable thing in the world, and after about half an hour he fell asleep
against the doorframe and his father carried him to bed and he did not wake up.

Nine days later the shut stall on the third bridge opened again, and the woman
was there, and she did the fried things exactly as before, and Sazare bought two
with a coin his mother had given him and ate them on the wall with his heels
knocking, and by the end of the month he had almost entirely stopped thinking
about the cart.
