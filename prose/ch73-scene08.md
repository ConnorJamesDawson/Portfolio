# Chapter 73, Scene 8 — The Glass

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, Tsunade's house. Tsunade, age fifty-three.
POV: Tsunade. ~1480 words.*

---

She woke at nine, which she had not done since the Second War,
in her own house, in her own bed, comprehensively, magnificently
sore, with an eighteen-year-old fabricator asleep face-down
beside her and one of his hands resting flat between her
shoulder blades exactly where it had landed in the garden.

She lay still for a long time and audited nothing at all.

*Charts closed,* she thought, eventually, with enormous
satisfaction. *All of them. Every register, every file, every
column. There is a woman in this bed who has spent three months
writing herself clinical notes about a boy and she is off duty
until further notice.*

The light in the room was wrong — it was morning light, actual
morning, arriving through a window she had washed on Thursday
in a room that had not held a person past dawn since she bought
it, and it was lying across a bare shoulder and a scarred
forearm and about four feet of extremely destroyed bedding, and
she looked at all of it and felt something in her chest that
took her a moment to identify because she had not felt it in
this building, ever.

*The house is awake.*

*Eleven years. I have owned this address for eleven years and
this is the first morning anything in it has ever happened.*

---

He surfaced at half past, badly, in stages, and the stages were
the funniest thing she had witnessed in a decade.

"Don't," she said, watching him try to sit up and immediately
reconsider the entire project.

"I have a training—" He stopped. He looked at the light on the
wall, and then at her, and she watched the calculation land.
"It's Sunday."

"It's Sunday."

"There is no field on Sunday."

"There is no field on Sunday," she agreed, "there is no board,
there is no yard, there is no diary, and Shizune has been given
to understand that the Hokage is unavailable this weekend for
reasons entered as *private*, which is a thing she has been
waiting three years to write and which she wrote, I am told,
with a flourish." She stretched, extensively, and enjoyed the
resulting expression on his face. "Also you're not going
anywhere, because you can't walk."

"That is not—" He moved, experimentally, and stopped. "That is
substantially accurate."

"Eight weeks of Jiraiya and one night with me. Which hurts
more?"

"I decline to answer on the grounds that both parties have
access to my medical file," said Sazare, and she laughed, and
he lay back down looking so profoundly pleased with the world
that she had to put her face in the pillow for a second.

They stayed there for an hour. Nothing happened in it. Nothing
was required to happen in it — that was the discovery of the
morning, a discovery she was apparently making for the first
time at fifty-three: that the hour after is a place people go
and live in, and that she had spent her entire adult life
arranging never to be in a bed at nine in the morning with
somebody who had nowhere else to be.

She learned that he had a scar on his left knee he had never
mentioned. He learned that she snored, marginally, and made
the mistake of saying so, and paid for it. They discussed,
with total seriousness and at length, whether the destroyed
garden constituted a facilities issue for the office or a
private matter, and concluded that it was a private matter,
and that the ridge was staying.

"You're keeping it?"

"I'm keeping all of it," said Tsunade. "The ridge, the
knuckle at the plum, and the nine yards of path you turned
into a mineral ecology. That garden's been dead for eleven
years and now there's four hundred years of Ruri crystal in
it and a hole where I punched your wall. It's the most
interesting thing anybody's ever done to that house." She
considered. "The caretaker's going to have opinions."

"You dismissed the caretaker two years ago."

"...How do you know that?"

"The dust gradient in the hall is nineteen to twenty-four
months old," said Sazare, into the pillow, "and there is a
nail in the kitchen wall where somebody hung a key ring for
years and stopped."

"Off duty, fabricator."

"Off duty," he agreed, and went back to sleep for twenty
minutes.

---

She got up at eleven and made tea in a kitchen that had
never made tea, and found that she owned two cups, which was
a fact about her life she elected to examine on some other
day, and carried them back through the house.

And stopped in the bedroom door.

He was awake, sitting on the edge of the bed in yesterday's
trousers with his hair in a state, and he had the glass in
his hands.

He was not doing anything with it. He was just holding it,
turning it slowly, looking at the dust on the water's
surface the way he looked at a casting that had rung wrong —
and she stood in the door with two cups of tea and watched
the last eleven years of her life get read by somebody who
was going to be right about all of it.

"Say it," she said.

"No," said Sazare.

"You've read it."

"I have read it," he said, "and it is not mine, and I am not
going to hand you an inventory of your own house at eleven in
the morning." He looked up. "But I would like to do something
about it, and it is your glass, so I am asking."

Her throat did something complicated.

"Ask, then."

"May I wash the glass?"

*Oh, you—*

She had to put the cups down. That was the practical
consequence; she had to put both cups down on the empty
shelf, carefully, because her hands had stopped being
reliable, and she stood in the middle of her own bedroom at
eleven o'clock on a Sunday morning in the third month with
her eyes stinging like a girl's.

"It's been there three weeks," she said, which was a lie of
the specific and cowardly kind, and they both knew it, and
she heard herself correct it out loud without deciding to:
"It's been there since I bought the house."

"Yes," said Sazare, gently.

"I put it there the first night. I never drank it. I've
refilled it — twice, maybe three times, in eleven years,
when it evaporated too far to be a glass of water any more —
and I have never once drunk it and I have never once thrown
it out and I have never in my life said that sentence to
another human being."

"I know."

"Wash the glass, love," said the fifth Hokage of the Land of
Fire.

He took it to the kitchen. She heard the tap, and the small
domestic sounds of a man washing one glass properly, the way
he did everything, and he came back and set it on the
nightstand beside the new lamp — clean, full, the water
clear all the way through, the morning coming in the washed
window and going straight through it and landing on the
wall.

It looked, in that room, absurdly like an article.

"There," he said.

She looked at it for a while.

*The house is awake,* she thought again, and this time she
let the whole of it arrive: *the bed is unmade because
somebody slept in it. There are two cups on the shelf. The
garden is wrecked and there is a ridge in it that will
outlive me. The lamp is a week old and the glass is one
minute old and there is a boy in my kitchen looking for
something to eat with the entire wreck of my hair on his
shirt.*

*I have kept this house for eleven years in case.*

*In case of him. I didn't know that when I bought it. I
know it now, and I am not going to say it out loud today,
because he's already read it and he had the grace not to
say so, and because I want one thing left to give him
later.*

She picked the cups back up.

"Tea," she said. "Then breakfast, because there's nothing
in this house and we're going to have to go out into the
village looking exactly like this, and Sazare—" she handed
him his cup and looked at him over the rim of hers, and let
the grin arrive, the one from under the bathwater, "—I want
you to understand that every single person we pass is
going to be able to tell, and that I have decided I do not
care, and that this is the most reckless thing I have ever
done in my life including three wars."

"The record will note," said Sazare Ruri, taking the tea,
"that I asked for the deep end."
