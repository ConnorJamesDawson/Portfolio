# Chapter 40, Scene 3 — Not Courting

*Act 4, part D. Konoha General, the stairwell. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,072 words.*

---

Sakura saw the spine on the Friday and stopped dead on the half-landing
with a tray in both hands.

He had it on top of the other three, face up, on the ledge of the
stairwell window where he had put it down to take a pulse chart out of
his coat, and he did not register what had happened until she had been
standing still for about two seconds, which for Sakura Haruno was an
extremely long time.

"*Ruri-san.*"

"Yes?"

"What is that."

He looked at it.

"It's a book about attraction and courtship. It's from the third bay,
second shelf up, and it was printed forty-four years ago and it's two
hundred and eleven pages and I'm on page nine."

Sakura put the tray down on the sill.

She put it down very carefully, which he understood afterwards to have
been an act of considerable self-control, and then she said, in a voice
that was several notes above where it usually sat:

"*Courtship.*"

"That's what it's called."

"That is *not* what it's called."

"It's on the cover."

"It's on the cover because it was printed forty-four years ago," said
Sakura Haruno, "and I want you to understand that if you say that word
in front of anybody under about forty they will assume you are being
funny, and you are never being funny, and it is going to go extremely
badly for you."

Sazare got the notebook out.

"What's the current term?"

"You're writing it down."

"I write everything down. What's the current term?"

Sakura went the colour of a plum.

"*Dating*," she said. "It's called dating. You — a person asks somebody
to go somewhere with them and it's understood to be *that* and not just
two people going somewhere, and the whole difference is in whether
everybody knows which one it is, and that's why it's a stupid word,
because it doesn't say anything about what it *is*, it only says—"

She stopped.

"Why are you still writing?"

"Because that was the useful part," said Sazare. "The book has nine
pages on the etiquette of the approach and not one line on how either
party establishes which of the two things is happening, and you've just
told me that the entire distinction lives in a shared understanding
that is never stated aloud, which is by some margin the most alarming
thing anybody has told me this year."

---

Sakura sat down on the stairs.

"Right," she said. "I have about four minutes and then I have to take
this up. Ask me things."

"You don't have to—"

"I am *fourteen*, Ruri-san, and I have opinions about this that I am not
allowed to have out loud anywhere else in this building, and you have
just handed me a book that says *courtship*, and this is the best thing
that has happened to me all month. Ask me things."

So he asked her things.

He asked her four, and wrote all four answers down, and about halfway
through the second one he noticed that she had stopped being
embarrassed and had started being extremely precise, and that she was
good at this in the specific way she was good at everything, which was
that she had thought about it more carefully than anybody had ever
asked her to.

"The thing nobody tells you," she said, on the fourth, "is that it's
not one decision. Everyone acts like there's a moment where somebody
decides and then says something. There isn't. It's about four hundred
small things over months and by the time anybody says anything out loud
they've both known for ages and the saying-it-out-loud is
*administrative*."

Sazare stopped writing.

"Say that again."

"Which part?"

"*The saying it out loud is administrative.*"

"...Yes?"

"That's a filing problem," said Sazare. "That's — the entire thing is
accumulated evidence and then a declaration that changes nothing except
the record. That's a *filing problem*. I have spent three years inside
one of those."

Sakura looked at him.

"That is the least romantic sentence I have ever heard in my life," she
said, "and I think you might be right, and I hate it."

---

She got up and took the tray and went up two steps and then turned
round, because she was Sakura and she was never quite finished.

"Can I ask you one?"

"Yes."

"Is this — " She stopped, and visibly reorganised the sentence, and
Sazare watched her do it and did not help. "Is it *general*? The book,
I mean. Is it a general enquiry."

He considered the question honestly, because she had asked it honestly
and because she had earned an honest one.

"Yes," he said.

"Right."

"It's general because I have never run the thing on anybody, ever, and
found that out at eighteen, and I do not know whether that is damage or
whether it is late, and I would like to understand the mechanism." He
put the notebook away. "It's general in the way that a man who cannot
read investigates literacy."

"That's — okay. That's fair." She shifted the tray. "But somebody
asked you a question that made you go and get the book."

Sazare stood in a stairwell.

He did not answer it, and he did not deflect it either, and after
about four seconds Sakura Haruno nodded once, in a way that suggested
she had received an answer, and went up the stairs.

He worked out on the walk home that she had.

---

*Note,* he wrote that night, under the second column:

*Sakura asked whether the enquiry was general. I said yes and then gave
her about forty words of accurate supporting reasoning, all of which
was true.*

*Then she said that somebody had asked me a question that made me go and
get the book, and I said nothing, and she took the nothing as an
answer, and she was right to.*

*Finding: a non-answer is data. I have known that about other people for
eleven years and have been using it professionally since I was fifteen,
and it has never once occurred to me that I emit it.*

*I have been assuming, apparently since childhood, that saying nothing
is the same as giving nothing.*

*It is not. It has never been. Everybody I have ever declined to answer
has walked away holding something, and I have been leaking for eleven
years in the one direction I thought was sealed.*
