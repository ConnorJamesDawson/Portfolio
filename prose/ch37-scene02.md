# Chapter 37, Scene 2 — Chapter Four

*Act 4, part D. Konoha. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare.
~1,839 words.*

---

He did chapter four first, which the book specifically advised against.

*The reader is cautioned,* said the fourth page of it, *that gifts are
the most attractive of these practices and the least effective, being
the only one which may be performed entirely without the presence of
the other party, and are therefore the refuge of the timid.*

Sazare read that sentence, understood it completely, agreed with it,
and did chapter four first anyway, because it was the one he could
*make*.

---

**Handa** produced a problem the book had no provision for.

He got four evenings into designing a marking gauge before he sat back
and understood that he had already done it — that there was a bench
hook in a lean-to three streets off the south-east gate with four
inches of crystal along the stop and nine evenings of the Land of Waves
larch in it, and that he had made it eight months before he had
ever heard of chapter four.

He went back to the book to see what it said about that.

It said nothing about that.

There was no provision anywhere in one hundred and eleven pages for
having performed step four before reading the book, and Sazare sat at
his bench at the eleventh hour with a manual open in front of him and
laughed out loud, alone, for the second time that year.

He made the marking gauge anyway. Handa took it, tried it against a
board, adjusted the fence by about a sixteenth without being asked,
and said, "That's not bad," which Sazare had learned over eleven
months was the top of the available scale.

---

**Ashida** took nine days and was the one he got right.

The problem was that Ashida had already given him the significant
thing, and that the significant thing had been buff card and a rubber
band and ninety-one pieces of newspaper, and that any object Sazare
made in response would be measurably better than the object it was
answering, which the book warned about at length.

*A gift ought not to exceed the recipient's ability to receive it,*
said chapter four. *An extravagance is a demand disguised.*

So he made a folder.

Board, quarter-bound in cloth, with a proper spine and two ties and a
gusset, about eleven inches by fifteen, sized — he measured it in the
liaison office on a pretext — to the same dimensions as the buff one.
It took him three evenings and cost him about nine hundred ryō in
materials, most of which was the cloth, and it was the plainest thing
he had made in a year.

There was no lettering on it. He had thought about that for two of the
three evenings and had decided against, and had then made a small
label of thin card that slid into a pocket on the front, so the folder
could be for anything.

Ashida opened it at the gate and looked at it for a while.

"It's a folder."

"Yes."

"It's a *very good* folder."

"It's the same size as the other one."

Ashida stopped.

"You measured my folder."

"I measured your folder."

"When?"

"The Tuesday you were arguing with the stationery office about the
observation stock. You had it on the desk and I was standing next to it
for eleven minutes."

Ashida Kōhei stood at the hospital gate holding a quarter-bound folder
with an empty label pocket, and did not say anything for long enough
that Sazare began to construct four separate ways of having got it
wrong.

"Right," said Ashida eventually, thickly. "Right. Yes. Thank you.
That's—"

"You don't have to—"

"I'm going to put *WATER* on the label," said Ashida, "and I'm going to
put the clippings back in it, because I've kept doing them, and I never
told you that either."

---

**Emiko** produced a problem of a different kind.

Her cardigan pocket had been re-stitched twice because she carried her
own bandage scissors and the points went through, and Sazare had known
that for two years and had made her a sheath inside four evenings: hard
leather, boiled and formed round the actual scissors, with a spring
clip that would take the weight and a lip so the points could not
reach cloth.

He got as far as the desk with it and stopped.

*A repair is not a gift,* said chapter four, on page sixty-one, in the
middle of a passage he had read twice and had not thought applied to
him. *The person who mends a thing has performed a service, and a
service creates an obligation, and an obligation is the opposite of
what is intended here. Consider whether you are giving or invoicing.*

He stood in the second-floor corridor at the eleventh hour with a
leather sheath in his hand and understood, with total clarity and
about four years too late, what he had been doing to every single
person he had ever liked.

The lantern at Tomari. The lid. The veranda. The stair-nosing, the
mangle, the kettle handle, four hundred and eleven hammer handles at a
causeway head. Ōtaki Sue's window catch, four times, because she opened
it with her foot.

*I have never given anybody anything. I have mended their things and
let them decide what that meant.*

He gave Emiko the sheath, because it was made and because she needed
it, and she said, "Oh, that's *clever*," and had it clipped to her
pocket inside four seconds and used it for eleven years.

And then he went back to the bench and made her something else that
did not fix anything, which was a small brass hook for the back of the
admissions desk to hang a bed state on, which she did not need, which
solved no problem whatsoever, and which was engraved on the underside
where nobody would ever see it with the word *ADMISSIONS* in his
mother's hand.

She found the engraving about a fortnight later and came and found him
on the ward and did not say anything about it at all, and squeezed his
forearm once, hard, and went back to work.

He stood there afterwards for some time.

*Chapter five,* he thought. *That was chapter five and she did it
without reading the book.*

---

**Anko** was the one that cost him.

He had to ask for the coat, which meant telling her he had catalogued
it, which she already knew, and it took him three fortnightly
intercepts at the yard wall to get the sentence out.

"The repair under your left arm."

"What about it?"

"It's thread that doesn't match and it was done in a hurry by somebody
who wasn't going to take the coat off to do it properly, and it's
pulling. There's about four inches of the seam either side of it that's
going to go inside a year, and when it goes it'll take the lining with
it." He made himself finish. "I can do it. Properly. It'd be about six
evenings and you'd have to give me the coat for a fortnight."

Anko looked at him with the senbon halfway to her mouth.

"Six evenings."

"Four to do it. Two because I've never worked wool that old and I'd
want to practise on something first."

"You'd practise."

"It's your mother's coat," said Sazare.

She did not say anything for about eleven seconds. Then she took the
coat off — right there, at the yard wall, in the cold, in a mesh
undershirt, with an entirely unreadable face — and handed it over, and
said:

"If you lose that I will end you and nobody in this village will find
the pieces."

"I know."

"Six evenings."

"Fourteen days."

He took eleven, because the practice piece taught him two things he had
not expected, and when he gave it back the repair was invisible at
four inches and he had also re-anchored the lining at three points
where it had begun to sag and had done nothing else at all, because
chapter four had a paragraph on that too and he had read it eleven
times.

*The mender is not to improve. He is to make the thing what it was.*

Anko put it on in the yard and did not look at the seam.

"You didn't change anything," she said.

"No."

"You could have. The cuffs are wrong, they're let down and you can see
the line, and the collar's — " she stopped.

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because your mother had it made for her arms," said Sazare, "and
somebody let the cuffs down for you, and the line where they did it is
the only part of that coat that has both of you in it."

Anko Mitarashi stood in a quartermaster's yard in the cold with her
hands in the pockets of her mother's coat.

"Right," she said. "That's — right. Yes." She pushed off the wall.
"Same time in a fortnight. And Ruri."

"Yes?"

"Don't do that again for a bit. I've got a limit."

---

**Ōtaki Sue** was the last and the hardest, and the thing that made it
hard was that he had already given her the gift.

It was a small silver stem-and-leaf brooch, about two inches, which he
had made at his bench in the winter over about nine evenings while
stuck on a bezel, and had put through her door in a fold of paper with
nothing written on it, because at the time the alternative had been
writing something on it.

She had worn it every day since.

He had watched her wear it every day for eight months — on the grey
wrapper, always on the left, always straight, which took her about four
attempts each morning with hands that did not do small work any more —
and had said nothing, and had understood that this was the coward's
version and had done it anyway.

Chapter four had a page on anonymity.

*The unsigned gift is a species of hiding. It confers the pleasure of
having given without the risk of being known to have given, and the
recipient is thereby denied the only part of the transaction that
matters to them, which is the knowledge of who thought of them.*

He went down two floors and knocked and took the slate off its hook and
wrote, in his mother's clerk hand, which she had told him eighteen
months earlier was the best she had ever seen:

*IT WAS ME. THE BROOCH. IN THE WINTER.*

*I DID NOT SIGN IT BECAUSE I DID NOT WANT TO BE ASKED WHY I HAD MADE
IT AND THAT WAS COWARDICE AND I HAVE READ A BOOK ABOUT IT.*

Ōtaki Sue read it.

Then she took the chalk out of his hand, and turned the slate over,
and wrote on the back, in the fast square hand:

*I KNEW*

*WHO ELSE WOULD IT BE*

*I HAVE BEEN WAITING 8 MONTHS FOR YOU TO SAY SO AND I ALMOST GAVE UP
IN THE 6TH*
