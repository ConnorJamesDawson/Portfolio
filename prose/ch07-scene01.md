# Chapter 7, Scene 1 — Seven Minutes

*Act 1, beat 17. Sazare Ruri, age twelve. POV: Sazare. ~2,700 words.*

---

The assessments ran over four days in the third month and his was on the second
day, in the afternoon, in a room off the main hall with two examiners and a
clerk.

He did the throwing, which was adequate. He did the wall, which was poor. He did
the substitution forms, twice, and was told to do the second one again and did it
worse.

Then they put a bowl on the table.

"Still water," said the examiner on the left, who was about fifty and had a cold.
"You'll have done this."

"Yes."

"How long do you want?"

"Sorry?"

"How long are you telling me you can do," said the examiner. "Because I'll run the
glass to that and no further. If you say four you get four. It's not a competition
and it's the last thing today and I'd quite like to go home."

Sazare put his hands either side of the bowl.

He thought about Mizuho leaning against a desk with her arms folded saying *your
best is yours, and the day you hand it over you don't get it back*. He thought about
his mother's arm going tighter round his shoulders on the step. He thought — because
he could not help it, because he had thought about it every day for four months —
about a very clean floor in a boat shed and a man called Ryōsuke saying *come back
in four months*, which was eleven days away.

"Seven minutes," he said.

"Seven." The examiner turned the glass. "Off you go."

---

It was the easiest thing he had ever done.

The room was quiet. The clerk's pen went. Somewhere down the corridor a class was
being let out and there was a noise of forty pairs of feet and then nothing. The
water in the bowl found the place where it was moving and Sazare did not do
anything about it and the surface went glassy inside about twenty seconds and
stayed that way.

He held it there for seven minutes and eleven seconds while the sand ran out, and
did not have to work at any of it, and spent most of it thinking about whether the
boat shed would still have the light on.

"Right," said the examiner. "That's your seven."

"Yes."

"Anything left in it?"

Sazare looked at him.

"No," he said.

"Fair enough." The examiner wrote something down and turned the sheet round for
the clerk. "*Support stream — sustained-focus adequate, seven min., no
distinguishing feature.* That do you?"

"Yes," said Sazare.

"Off you go. Results in a fortnight. Send the next one in."

---

Jirō was outside on the wall, having done his the day before, and had been waiting
an hour and a half for no reason except that he had said he would.

"Well?"

"Seven minutes."

"*Seven?*" Jirō came down off the wall. "You can do fourteen."

"Seven's fine."

"You can do *fourteen*, Sazare, I've *seen* you do fourteen, you did fourteen in
the—"

"Seven passes," said Sazare. "That's all it has to do."

Jirō looked at him with an expression that was, for about a second and a half,
completely adult.

"Right," he said. "Yeah. Fair enough."

They went down the hill.

It was the third month and the fog was off and the light had that thin quality it
got, and the market was noisy, and the stall by the third bridge was open and the
woman gave them two of the fried things and took money for one, which she had been
doing for two years and which neither of them had ever mentioned.

They ate them on the wall with their heels knocking.

"Two weeks," said Jirō.

"Two weeks."

"And then it's — what, they give you the band and then there's the assignments,
and my brother says the assignments take another month because they do them all at
once—"

"Your brother says a lot of things."

"My brother," said Jirō with dignity, "is a *reconnaissance chūnin*."

"Your brother is nineteen and he lives in your mum's house."

"He's saving up," said Jirō.

They watched the boats. It was, Sazare thought afterwards, and thought about a
great deal, an extremely ordinary afternoon.

---

He knew from the top of the street.

Not because of anything. The wall was the same and the gate was the same and there
was no smoke and there was nobody standing about, and he knew from the top of the
street, and he never afterwards understood how.

Then he got closer and it was because the gate was open.

The Ruri gate did not stand open. It was shut at dusk and it was shut in the day
if there was nobody in the yard, and it had a bar and the bar had been on it since
before Sazare was born and the single most reliable domestic fact of his entire
life was that his uncle shut that gate.

It was standing open about two feet.

---

He went in.

The yard was empty and completely undisturbed. His mother's rag and the bowl of
lattice-cleaner were on the step, and the bowl had a skin on it, which meant it
had been sitting a while.

The house door was open too.

He went in and there was nobody in the front room, and there was nobody in the
kitchen, and there was a bowl on the kitchen table with food in it, and one on the
side that had been washed, and a pot that had gone cold on the range with the lid
off.

"Mum?"

Nothing.

"*Mum?*"

He went through the whole house. It took less than a minute; it was not a big
house. Nobody in the front room, nobody in the kitchen, nobody in his parents'
room or Iwao's or his own or Kohaku's, which was still empty and still had the
mattress rolled.

Nothing was broken anywhere. Nothing was missing that he could see. There was no
blood.

Then he went into the workshop.

---

They had done the frames.

All nine of them were off the trestles and on the floor, and they had been done
properly — not tipped over, *broken*, each one with the sand out of it and the
frame itself cracked across, which took some doing, because a growing-frame was
four hundred years of hardwood and about as thick as a man's wrist.

The sand was everywhere. That was what he could not stop looking at. Nine trays of
fine grey sand across the whole floor of the north room in a layer, and footprints
through it, and in the sand you could see quite clearly where somebody had walked
in, and walked round, and walked out again, and it had not taken them very long.

The copper lattices had gone. All of them. Even the small ones he cleaned.

His own frame, the tenth, was still on the sill in his room where he had left it,
because it was small enough to sit on a sill, and nobody had touched it at all.

---

He stood in the doorway of the workshop for a long time.

There were no bodies. He kept coming back to that. There were no bodies and there
was no blood and there were no marks on the walls and nothing in the yard, and the
food was still in the bowl on the table, and the frames were broken.

If they had killed them, he thought, they would not have broken the frames.

He was twelve. It was the best reasoning he had and it was, as it turned out,
correct, and it did not help him for one second in the eleven years that followed.

---

He went next door.

He went next door and knocked and the door opened three inches on a chain that had
not been there a year ago, and Mrs Sawaki looked out at him, and Sazare had known
Mrs Sawaki his entire life. She had given him things off her step. She had told
his mother when he had climbed on her roof. She had cried at his grandmother's
funeral so hard she had to be taken out.

"Where are they?"

Mrs Sawaki looked at him through three inches.

"I don't know, love," she said.

"You were *here*—"

"I don't know."

"Did you *see*—"

"Sazare," said Mrs Sawaki, and her voice went, and she pressed her mouth shut and
got it back. "I don't know. I didn't look. I heard the gate and I didn't look. I'm
sorry. I didn't look."

And she shut the door, and he heard her lean against it on the other side, and he
stood there for a while and then went and knocked on the next one along, and
nobody answered it at all.

---

He went back into the yard and sat down on the step next to the bowl with the skin
on it.

The light went.

At some point — and he could not have said how long, and later thought it might
have been three hours — it occurred to him that nobody was going to come out and
tell him what to do, and that this was now, permanently, a thing he was going to
have to decide by himself, and that he had no idea whatsoever how you started.

He went in and got his frame off the sill, and the cloth roll with the thirty-four
offcuts in it, and his coat, and the eleven ryō that were in the jar in the
kitchen because they had always been in the jar in the kitchen.

He did not take the frame in the end. He stood in the yard holding it for a while
and then put it down on the step, carefully, next to the bowl.

Then he pulled the gate to behind him, and put the bar on, because his uncle would
have.
