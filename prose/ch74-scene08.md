# Chapter 74, Scene 8 — Chapter Twelve

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the tower, the residence bath. Tsunade, age
fifty-three. POV: Tsunade. ~2,350 words.*

---

She had been holding the sentence for three weeks and she had always
known she would spend it on a bad day.

That was not what she had told the court. What she had told the court,
from the end of a bed in the second month with the evidence in her lap,
was that she would choose a date without notice, when his composure had
had time to grow back and could be *properly harvested* — and she had
meant every word at the time, and had spent a genuinely enjoyable
fortnight not using it, and had discovered somewhere around the second
Thursday that she was not, in fact, waiting for his composure.

She was waiting for a week that had taken something off him.

*Which is not what a court is for,* she noted, in the register, on the
Wednesday night, with the boy asleep beside her at the wrong depth and
one hand fisted in her sleeve like a man holding a rail. *Chart it
honestly. The sentence was passed for the pleasure of watching him
suffer, and it is going to be executed for the exact opposite reason,
and I am not going to tell him that, ever, because he would work out
what it means and then he would be unbearable about it for a decade.*

*He has had four days. Struck creed, empty books, a boy shouting at
him in a kitchen — he came home on Tuesday and told me all of that
standing up, with his coat still on, because he cannot sit down until
a thing is reported — and then whatever happened in that yard
yesterday, which he has not told me and which I have not asked about,
and which took the last of it.*

*So: tomorrow. It's Thursday. He gets the single most ridiculous hour
available in the Land of Fire and he gets it from me, and if the fifth
Hokage has to conduct it from a bathtub with a piece of pornography in
her hand then that is what the office costs.*

---

He was in the water first, as always, at the far end, thermal
stability, the engineer's opening. Eyes shut. Four days of it lying on
him like weather on a roof.

She came in with the book.

She let him get to the halfway point of a long breath before she said,
in the flat administrative voice, "The court is in session."

The reaction was, she would say afterwards, worth the entire three
weeks.

He came off the end of the tub about four inches. His eyes opened. His
whole face performed something that had no business being available to
a man with no face, and she watched the projection run — the bath, the
book, the page, her — arriving in about a second and a half, and she
sat down on the step with her feet in the water and enjoyed every
frame of it.

"...Now."

"Without notice. It's in the sentence. You were advised to train for
it." She held the book up. Orange. Dog-eared. His own two pages of
faults folded in the back where they lived. "Chapter twelve. Aloud.
To the end of the page. The court reserves the right to heckle and
intends to exercise it."

"Hime, I have had a *week*—"

"I know exactly what week you've had, fabricator," said Tsunade, "and
the court is aware of no mitigating circumstances whatsoever. Take the
book."

He took the book.

She watched him hold it for a moment — hold it the way he held
articles, actually, turning it a quarter, the thumb finding the
dog-ear — and then he found chapter twelve, and she saw him find it,
because the page had a fold in it four attempts deep and it opened
there on its own.

"For the record," said Sazare, "I have never got past the fourth
paragraph."

"The record notes it. Begin."

---

He began.

And it was, for about ninety seconds, absolutely everything she had
been promising herself for three weeks.

The voice was doing its level best. That was the glory of it — he had
gone into the working register, the grading pitch, the one he used for
kiln temperatures and casualty figures, because it was the only voice
he had, and so chapter twelve of the most notorious book in the five
nations arrived in the room as though it were being certified.

"*The steam rose between them like a curtain that neither would draw
aside,*" read Sazare, in the voice of a man reading out a tolerance,
"*and Junko's hair, unbound at last, lay upon the water like—*"

"Like what," said Tsunade.

"I have not got to it."

"Get to it."

"*—lay upon the water like ink poured out of a broken pot.*"

"Right," said Tsunade. "Stop. Hair doesn't do that."

"It does not do that."

"It floats. It fans. It goes to the surface and it *spreads*, and any
man who has ever seen a woman in a bath knows it, and that man has
seen more of them than the census—"

"Fault fifteen," said Sazare, with the pen he had not brought and did
not have, and she was so glad she had to put her face in her hands.
"Continue?"

"Continue."

He continued. He got four more lines, which included the male lead
doing something with a shoulder that was, by any reasonable reading of
the anatomy, impossible from where the author had put him, and Sazare
stopped of his own accord.

"He is behind her."

"He's behind her."

"His right hand cannot arrive there. Not from behind. Not without the
elbow going through the wall of the tub, which I am prepared to
sketch."

"Don't you dare sketch it—"

"I have *sketched* it," said Sazare, "in December, on the fourth
attempt, at about two in the morning, on the back of a fabrication
docket, and the sketch is why I did not get to paragraph five," and
the laugh she produced then took her right under the water, and she
came up with her hair everywhere and the water going over the side and
her ribs hurting, and he sat at the far end with the book open,
pink to the ears, entirely composed, waiting like a man at a lectern.

"Sixteen," she managed. "Sixteen faults. Jiraiya is going to *cry*."

"He is going to bill the hospital's winter fund for it. That was the
sentence. I intend to be extremely thorough — "

"Read."

---

He read.

And somewhere around the third paragraph of the page she stopped
heckling, because something in the room had changed and she was, at
the bottom of everything, a diagnostician, and diagnosticians notice.

He was fine.

Not composed. Not enduring. He was reading a badly-written erotic
scene aloud in a bathtub to the woman it had been about, in a level
voice, with his colour high and his hands entirely steady, in exactly
the way he had once read a border digest — and he had got seven
paragraphs past a place he had never once in four attempts been able
to reach, and he had not noticed that he had.

She let him get to the bottom of the page.

"To the end of the page," said Sazare, and closed it, and looked up.
"Sentence served. May the court—"

"You got through it."

He stopped.

She watched it arrive. It was, she thought, one of the four or five
best things she had ever been present for: the exact moment a man who
had been braced for a wall walked through the place the wall had been
and turned around and found nothing behind him.

"...I got through it."

"Four attempts. Three months. The one page in that whole disreputable
book that could stop you, and it's the reason you nearly took your
own head off in this bathroom in the second month, and you just read
it out loud to me and corrected the *elbow geometry.*"

"I—" He looked at the book. He turned it a quarter, which was what
his hands did when the rest of him had gone somewhere else. "It did
not do anything."

"Say the whole of it," said Tsunade. "You always say the whole of
it."

And she watched him work it out in real time, which was the other
thing she had never expected out of this evening, because he did his
working out on his face and he had no idea.

"The interference pattern is gone," he said slowly. "That was the
mechanism. I could not read it because I had been in this room with
you four times before you ever said *budge up* — four constructed
versions, built out of nothing, running in a man with no data — and
every attempt at the page put the construction and the material into
the same channel and the instrument saturated. It was never the
prose. The prose is *bad*, Hime, it is genuinely bad, I have sixteen
faults—"

"I know."

"—and it never had any power in it at all. The power was mine. It was
my own signal coming back at me off a page." He set the book down on
the step, carefully, the way he set down a finished article. "And
there is no construction any more. There is nothing for it to
interfere with. I have—"

He stopped.

"Say it," said Tsunade, and her own voice had gone somewhere quiet.

"I have the real thing," said Sazare. "So the page is just a page. It
cannot reach me. Nothing in it is as good as Saturday and I do not
have to imagine one single line of it, and I am never going to have
to again."

---

The bath was very quiet.

*Well,* thought the fifth Hokage of the Land of Fire, sitting on the
step of her own bathroom with her feet in the water and her hair
ruined and a piece of celebrated filth face-down on the tiles beside
her. *That went differently.*

*I built an execution and he turned it into a — chart it, doctor, you
have never once flinched from a chart in your life —*

*— into a receipt.*

*Three weeks I've been saving this. I was going to watch him come
apart over six paragraphs and I was going to enjoy it the way I have
enjoyed nothing since I was young, and instead I have just been told,
in the grading voice, by a man who cannot lie and has never once
constructed a compliment in his life, that a scene he could not
survive imagining has been rendered inert by the fact of me.*

*Nobody in fifty-three years has done anything to me on that scale
and most of them were trying.*

She reached over and took the book off the step.

"Court's finding," she said. "Sentence served. Defendant discharged,
and the court would like it minuted that it was comprehensively
outmanoeuvred by a man who was not manoeuvring, which is the only way
anybody has ever beaten me at anything." She turned the book over in
her hands. "Now shut up and get down there, and don't argue, because
this is the appeal and the appeal is mine."

"...The appeal."

"You read me a page. I'm reading you the rest of the chapter." She
found the place with her thumb. "Because I threw this book at a wall
twenty years ago over page sixty-one and I have never once read what
came after chapter twelve, and it turns out I've been carrying my own
fold in it, and I've just watched somebody go through his and I'd
like a go."

And so the fifth Hokage of the Land of Fire read four pages of
*Icha Icha* aloud in her own bathroom, badly, in the flat
administrative voice, doing all the characters in the same voice,
stopping twice to say *oh, that's just lazy* — while an
eighteen-year-old fabricator lay back in the water with his eyes
shut and his head against the cedar and listened to her the way
other people listen to rain.

The count went somewhere in the third page.

She noticed him notice, which was the whole of the joke: she watched
his hand start to move toward a notebook that was two rooms away,
and stop, and settle back on the rim, and she got the exact instant
the ledger lost the thread and did not recover it.

"Fifty-nine?" she said, not looking up.

"I do not know."

"You don't *know*?"

"There were four in a row somewhere around the innkeeper," said
Sazare, from the water, in the voice of a man reporting a
catastrophic and entirely acceptable loss of data, "and one of them
was mine and I could not determine which, and after that the series
is unrecoverable. It is the first break in the record since the
ninth of January."

"Enter it as a break, then."

"I intend to enter it as a *finding*."

"Of course you do." She turned the page. "Go on."

"That the count was designed to detect a shortage," said Sazare, "and
that it has no upper stop, and that it has now failed in the other
direction for the first time — and that a ledger which cannot survive
its own surplus is a ledger with a design fault, and that I am going
to have to build a new one, and that I have never in my life been so
pleased to condemn an instrument."

*And that,* thought Tsunade, *is going in the register at one in the
morning, verbatim, under a heading I am not going to be able to look
at without doing something undignified for the rest of my life.*

"Chapter thirteen," she said, "is about a *boat*, and I have low
expectations. Stay where you are."

She read him the boat chapter. It was terrible. Neither of them
minded at all, and the lamp burned down, and nobody wrote anything
down anywhere, because it was Thursday.
