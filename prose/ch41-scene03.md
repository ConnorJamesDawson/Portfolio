# Chapter 41, Scene 3 — The Unfinished Line

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the room over the persimmon tree. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,246 words.*

---

He got the box down on the twenty-second of the third month and this
time he opened it.

Thirty-one feet across the floor of a twelve-by-nine room, weighted at
the corners with the tin, a cup, the whetstone and a shoe, exactly as
before, and he had to stand in the doorway again to see the whole of
it.

He went straight to the sixth hand.

*On the lattice, and I have put this off for two years because I do not
know how to write it without sounding as though I am accusing my own
grandmother of foolishness.*

*The lattice does not do what we say it does.*

*We say it holds the piece. It does not hold the piece. I have run this
four hundred times and I can now say it plainly: a piece under the
lattice and a piece under no lattice at all fail at the same load if
the growth is fast. The lattice makes no difference whatever to a fast
piece. It only makes a difference to a slow one.*

*Everyone I have asked says this is because slow work is better work,
which is not an explanation, it is the thing to be explained.*

*What I believe is that the lattice does nothing to the piece and that
what it actually does is to the maker — that it obliges us to work
slowly and evenly and to hold a thing for six hours, and that whatever
is really happening is happening inside the stone during those six
hours and has nothing to do with copper at all. We have spent four
hundred years crediting the frame with the work of the*

Four letters and then nine feet of blank paper.

Sazare knelt on the floor of a rented room in the Land of Fire with a
pencil in his hand and did not write on the scroll, because it was
three hundred years old and because whoever the sixth hand had been had
earned better than a boy's handwriting after theirs.

He wrote it on a sheet of hospital requisition paper instead, in his
mother's clerk hand, upright and evenly spaced, and laid the sheet on
the floor at the end of the scroll where the writing stopped.

*— stone.*

---

He sat back on his heels.

*They were right for four hundred years and wrong about why, and those
are not the same failure and I would like to be precise about which one
it was.*

*The lattice works. That is not in dispute and I have four hundred
years of a family's livelihood as evidence, plus my own hands, plus a
copper frame in a workshop in ward eleven that my mother could hold for
six hours and that the trade never called a skill.*

*They found the correct procedure by trial and error over four
centuries and they attributed it to the wrong object, and the wrong
object was the one they could see.*

*Which is not stupidity. It is what everybody does. The frame is
copper and expensive and you can hold it. The stone is a bucket of
aggregate that costs nothing and sits there.*

He looked down the thirty-one feet.

*Nine entries on the lattice. Its dimensions, its gauge, the alloy, the
tension across the frame, the six-hour hold. Nine entries across three
centuries and six hands and every single one of them is about the
copper.*

*There is not one entry anywhere in this scroll about the medium.*

*Sixty-one things I already knew and nineteen I do better and eleven I
did not know, and I catalogued all of that on the ninth night in the
second month of last year and I did not notice the absence, because I
was looking for what was in it.*

*The most important thing in this document is a column that is not
there.*

---

The sixth hand had been about two years from it.

He worked that out over the following hour and it was the part he could
not get past, and he checked it four ways and it held every time.

*They had established that the lattice does nothing to a fast piece.
That is the whole finding. That is the experiment I have just run in a
quartermaster's yard with three years of somebody else's bookkeeping,
and they got there with four hundred repetitions and no ledger at all.*

*They had established that the effect is time-dependent and not
frame-dependent.*

*They had said, in writing, that whatever is really happening is
happening* inside the stone.

*Inside the stone.*

*That is the sentence. That is not a step toward the answer, that is
the answer, four words from the end, and they stopped mid-word.*

He put both hands flat on the floor either side of the scroll.

*Two years. They said they had been putting it off for two years
because they did not know how to write it without accusing their own
grandmother, and then they wrote it, and then they died before the
sentence closed.*

*I do not know their name.*

*I do not know whether they were a man or a woman. I do not know
whether they were forty or seventy or my mother's age. I have their
hand — fast, slanted, no margins, right to the edge of the paper — and
I know they argued with the second hand about tension and with the
fourth about temperature and that they went back and wrote* wrong *in
the margin twice, and that is the entire content of what I have of a
person who was standing exactly where I am standing and got there
first.*

---

He wrote the whole of it up that night and the entry ran to three
pages and he read it back at the fourth hour and changed nothing.

The last part was the part he had been walking around for an hour and
he wrote it in the flat hand:

*I have finished somebody else's sentence.*

*I want to be honest about the size of that, because I have spent all
evening trying to make it into something larger and it is not larger.*

*I did not discover this. I finished it. The work was four hundred
years of a family and four hundred repetitions by one person whose name
I will never have, and what I brought to it was a quartermaster's
source-medium column, a letter from somebody who wants to own me, and
eleven days at a fabrication bench with a chit.*

*Three years ago I wrote in a book that everything I have was invented
twice — once by people who are all dead, and once by me, alone, on
benches, in four countries. I have been carrying that sentence since I
was sixteen and I have been quietly proud of it and it is not true.*

*It was never invented twice. It was invented once, over three hundred
years, by six people, and stopped four letters short, and I have been
walking the last four letters for eleven years without knowing there
was a road under me.*

*I am not the first. I am the seventh.*

He looked at that.

Then he added, because the ledger got the flattering half and the
honest half or it got neither:

*And the seventh is a considerably better thing to be than the first,
and I have needed about four years to be able to write that down.*
