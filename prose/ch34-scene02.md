# Chapter 34, Scene 2 — Four Lines

*Act 4, part C. Konoha, the administration building. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,075 words.*

---

The advance papers came in from the Kiri delegation nine days before
the delegation did, which Kōji said was standard and was also, in his
professional opinion, a message.

"They've sent us their positions in advance. Nobody does that unless
they want the meeting to be short and are confident about how it goes."

Sazare was shown the parts that concerned him in a small room on the
first floor with Kōji on one side of the table and a normalisation
officer called Amagai on the other, and Ashida standing by the door
holding a folder he did not need, in the manner of a man who had asked
to be present and had been told he could be present as long as he was
holding something.

There were three items.

The first was the withdrawal of the retrieval order, offered
unprompted, in a single sentence, with no conditions attached.

"They led with it," said Amagai. She was about fifty and had the flat
cheerfulness of somebody who had spent a career on the losing end of
negotiations and had made peace with it. "Which either means they're
serious about reconciliation or means they knew we'd found it and
wanted to be the ones to say it first. Both, probably. It's usually
both."

The second was the fabrication agreement, which was four pages, and
which Sazare read twice and understood on the first reading and read
again anyway because the second reading was where you found the
clauses that had been written to be read once.

The third was four lines long.

---

*Re: RURI, Sazare. Charge as listed [ref].*

*Review by this office establishes that the charge was entered as a
schedule attachment under [ordinance], for the purpose of constituting
lawful grounds for a retrieval order in respect of a registered
bloodline subject. No investigative file exists. No evidence was
gathered, no witness statement was taken, and no charging officer is
named.*

*The charge is accordingly vacated as improperly constituted. The
subject is not exonerated, having never been lawfully accused.*

*Kirigakure Registry, [date].*

Sazare read it four times.

"Do you want the plain version?" said Amagai.

"I've got the plain version."

"Say it, then, because I want to know we're agreed."

"There was never a charge. There was a form that needed a box filled in
before somebody could put a price on me, and the box got filled in with
a crime, and nobody ever investigated it because there was nothing to
investigate and everybody involved knew that." He set it down. "And
they haven't cleared my name. They've withdrawn the paperwork, because
clearing my name would require somebody to have accused me of something
in the first place, and nobody ever did."

"That's it exactly."

"*The subject is not exonerated, having never been lawfully accused.*"

"It's a good sentence," said Amagai. "It's an extremely good sentence,
technically. Whoever wrote that is a serious lawyer and they have found
the one formulation that is both entirely true and admits nothing."

---

He had expected to feel something.

He had, over four years, constructed a considerable amount of
architecture around the fact of the listing. It had determined eleven
lodgings across two countries. It had determined that he did not walk
into a crowd facing a platform. It had determined the shape of a year
and a half of contract work, the whole of the disposal doctrine, every
single decision he had made about where to sleep between the ages of
thirteen and sixteen, and it had been the specific object of the fear
that had woken him at one-forty in the morning on his first night in a
room over a persimmon tree, reaching for a knife that was not there.

And it turned out to have been a box on a form.

Not a lie about him. Not a false accusation somebody had believed, or
manufactured, or profited from. A **clerical requirement.** Somebody in
an office had needed to make a retrieval order lawful and the ordinance
required a charge, so a charge had gone in, and then everybody had got
on with their afternoon.

"You've gone quiet," said Kōji.

"It didn't matter to anybody."

"No."

"Not one person in that building ever thought I'd done it. There was
nobody to convince. There was no argument I could have won because
there was never an argument." He put both hands flat on the table.
"I've spent four years being careful about a thing that no human being
ever believed."

Amagai looked at Kōji, and Kōji did not look back at her, and Ashida
shifted his folder from one arm to the other by the door.

"Does it help," Amagai said, "that it's gone?"

Sazare considered the question honestly, because she had asked it
honestly.

"No," he said. "It helps that the retrieval order's going. That's a
real thing and it changes where I can stand and how I sleep. But this
one—" he touched the four lines "—this one hasn't given me anything
back. It's told me the thing I was frightened of was never even
pointed at me. It was pointed at my hands, and the charge was the
string they tied round it so the parcel would go in the post."

Nobody in that room said anything for a moment.

"Right," said Amagai eventually, and made a note. "I'm going to write
down that you understood the document, because I have to, and I want
you to know that what I'm writing is not what just happened."

---

Ashida caught him in the corridor afterwards, still holding the folder.

"You all right?"

"Yes."

"Because you look like you did in the third month, when you had that
thing about the—"

"I'm all right, Kōhei."

Ashida stopped, and Sazare realised, from the way he stopped, that he
had used his given name, which he had done perhaps twice in three
years.

"Right," said Ashida. "Well. I'm going to walk with you to the gate,
and I'm not going to say anything, and you're not going to have to
respond to it. That's the arrangement. You can object once, before we
start."

"I don't object."

They walked to the gate, and Ashida did not say anything, and at the
gate he took the folder he had been carrying for no reason all
afternoon, put it under his arm, and said, "Nine days," and went home
to his wife and his two children.
