# Chapter 9, Scene 4 — Her Road

*Act 2, part A. Sazare Ruri, age thirteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,500 words.*

---

The carter's name was Hachirō and he had one subject, which was the road.

Not roads in general. This road — the inland road from Shiranui up over the two
saddles to the spring towns — which he had driven for thirty-one years, and
which he regarded not as a route but as a colleague of long standing, with
moods, and a history, and a number of personal enemies, chief among them the
river, the prefecture, and a bridge-keeper at the second crossing whose
grandfather had wronged Hachirō's grandfather in a matter involving eels.

"Now this stretch," he would say, "this stretch is honest," and then nothing
more for an hour.

Sazare sat beside him on the board, or walked at the tailgate on the climbs to
spare the fat horse, and listened, and found — with the guilt that came
attached to every good thing now — that he liked it. Nobody had talked to him
like this since Kiyo at the weighbridge. Talk that wanted nothing. Talk like
weather, that went on whether you were there or not, that you could stand out
in and be rained on.

The country went up in steps. Terraced fields first, rice and green vegetables
in beds of black volcanic earth; then the forests, enormous, dripping, full of
birds with voices he did not know, hot underfoot in patches where the ground
leaked; then the first saddle, where the trees stood back and the whole coast
lay out behind them, blue and unimportant, and the sea beyond it with no lid on
it anywhere.

On the second day they passed a shrine at a spring where the water came out of
the rock at blood-heat, and Hachirō stopped the cart, and washed his face and
hands in it, and stood for a moment with his cap off.

"For the road?" Sazare asked, when they were moving again.

"For my wife," said Hachirō. "She liked this one. We came up here when we were
courting, and she put her hand in and said, *it's warm*, as though I'd done it
for her." He clicked at the horse. "Forty years back. She's four years dead.
This stretch is honest but the camber's a disgrace."

Sazare looked at the shrine going away behind them, and at the steam rising off
the pool where a woman had once put her hand in the water, and understood that
he had been shown something, and that the showing had cost the old man
nothing, and that this was apparently a thing people could do — carry their
dead lightly, out loud, on a public road, forty years — and he put it away with
the other things he was collecting, and it sat in the collection strangely,
glowing, like a coal.

---

It was on the third day, at the second river crossing, that he understood whose
road he was on.

The crossing was a chain ferry, six carts' worth of platform hauled over on a
cable, and there was a queue, and a board where the fares were chalked, and
under the fares, small, in the corner of the board, somebody had long ago
scratched a mark into the wood itself — not chalk; a knife — and the mark was
four strokes and a hook and a long curve that went three quarters round and
stopped.

Sazare stood in front of it with the queue moving around him.

He knew the curve. He had known it since he was seven years old, off a chip the
size of a fingernail: it was one of the twenty-one families — the seventh, the
one he had found on the fourth evening on the step, the one Kohaku had looked
at and said *yeah* in the tone she used for *no*. He did not know what it said.
He had never known what any of them said.

But he knew, now, standing in a ferry queue in a foreign country, what it *was*.

*Nagihama first, then over to the Land of Hot Water. There's a road.*

He had heard it through a door at eleven, sitting on the step in the dark, and
it had gone into the collection with everything else, and he had carried it
across the strait without once taking it out and looking at it. There's a road.
Not *a* road. *The* road — surveyed, marked, maintained; a road with postboxes
scratched into fare-boards; a road her people had walked, and her packages had
travelled, and down which, if one argument in a workshop had gone differently,
eight people would have come in the night with everything they could carry.

She had stood in this queue. There was no way to know that and he knew it
anyway, with a certainty that bypassed everything he had been taught about
evidence: his sister had stood on these boards with wet boots and a bag of
worthless chips, twenty-three days off her next meal, sixteen years old,
holding the whole system of it in her head, and had looked at that mark, and
checked it against the twenty-one families, and gone on.

"You'll miss the boarding," said Hachirō, from the cart.

Sazare put his good hand flat on the fare-board for a second — on the wood, over
the mark, the way you touch a trestle — and went and got on the ferry.

---

He asked Hachirō about the war that evening, obliquely, the way he asked
everything now: not *what do you hear from the Land of Water* but *do you carry
much off the boats*.

"Less than I did," said Hachirō. He fed the fire they were not cooking on;
carters, Sazare had learned, kept a fire the way other men kept a dog. "Herring's
herring. But the dried stuff, the trade goods, the lacquer — that's half what it
was two years back. Nothing wrong with the boats. There's something wrong with
the sellers." He looked into the fire. "You'd know more about that than me, I
expect."

"Why would I?"

"Because you came off one of Anzu's boats," said Hachirō, without any weight at
all, "and Anzu's boats have been busier every year for four years, and the
cargo walks ashore." He fed the fire again. "I've carried a fair bit of it up
this road. Cargo that walks. It talks less than you, some of it."

"...What do people say? Here. About it."

"They say it's a shame," said Hachirō.

"That's all?"

"That's a lot," said the old man, mildly. "You'll find, when you've gone up and
down a road long enough, that *it's a shame* is most of what a country ever
says about another country, and the ones that say more usually want something.
This lot—" he tipped his head at the dark valley, the sleeping farms, the whole
soft steaming country of it — "they say it's a shame, and they take the cargo
in, and they don't ask it questions, and there's worse neighbours to have. I've
heard of far worse." He banked the fire. "Great powers, they call the others.
Great. Hm. Nobody ever calls a country great for minding its business."

He rolled himself in his blanket, and was asleep with the totality of old men
and children, and Sazare lay on the tail-boards under the foreign stars, in a
country that did not matter, that had taken in four years of walking cargo and
said only that it was a shame, and did his sums.

Mizuho had spent one half-sentence on this place. She had spent whole mornings,
whole *seasons*, on the five that mattered — on honesty that was brutish and
friendliness that was treacherous and pride that was being buried in sand —
and she had done the voices, and the room had loved her, and every word of it
had been the men in the room drawing the room.

He fell asleep somewhere in the middle of the thought, for once without
dreaming, and woke at dawn to the smell of the fat horse and the sound of
Hachirō apologising to it for the camber.

---

Yumoto announced itself an hour before it arrived: the valley narrowed, the
river ran warm — actually warm, trailing skeins of steam in the cold morning
air, with white crusts on the stones like frost that would not melt — and then
the town came up out of the trees in ledges, wooden inns with deep eaves
stacked up both sides of a gorge, bathhouses with their shutters thrown open,
and over the whole of it a permanent standing mist that was not fog.

He watched it come with something turning over in his chest. It was steam. It
rose. It came up out of the ground and the pools and the hundred chimneys of
the town, white and lit and climbing, and where the fog of his whole childhood
had been a lid, this was the opposite of a lid — a country breathing upward,
all day, every day, into a clear sky.

The last house before the town proper stood on its own, past the shrine, where
the road bent to follow the river: a long low wooden building with a wide
porch, a woodpile against the gable that had been stacked by somebody who
squared his logs to the half-inch, and a garden of plants in rows that were not
flowers or food and were therefore medicine.

There was a man on the porch.

He was fifty-odd and built like a door, in a labourer's jacket with the sleeves
pushed up over forearms that did not go with the herb garden, and he watched
the cart pull up at his gate with no expression whatsoever, and came down the
steps unhurried, and stood, and looked at Sazare for a while — top to bottom,
one pass, no expression, and Sazare's whole chest went sideways, because the
last person who had looked at him like that had had her sleeves pinned back in
a cold workshop.

"Hachirō," said the man.

"Tōan. The camber's a disgrace."

"You say that every month." The man came round the cart. He did not offer to
help Sazare down, and did not watch him manage it one-handed, in the way of a
man who has already learned everything he needed from watching him not be
helped. "You're the arm."

"I'm—" Sazare stood in the road. Everything he owned was in one hand. "Yes.
I'm the arm."

"Anzu's bird came Tuesday. It said fourteen months old, three exits, tendon
gone at the wrist, set against a wall by an idiot." Tōan took hold of the sling
— not the arm, the cloth — with two fingers, and looked at the strapping, and
grunted. "The idiot?"

"That was me."

"At least it was a free idiot." He let the sling go, turned, and went back up
the steps. "Woodpile's low, fingers or no fingers. Kitchen's through. You'll
eat before I touch you — a month at least. There's rice on now."

He stopped in the doorway, without turning round, at the exact moment Sazare
had decided the interview was over.

"Second thing you'll learn here," he said. "First thing's the rice. A body
mends from the middle out. Nobody ever grew anything back on an empty—" the
pause was almost nothing, a half-breath, a man rehousing a word he had decided
against — "on an empty stomach. In you come."

The steam went up out of the whole valley into the morning.

Sazare Ruri, thirteen years and six months old, picked his bundle out of the
road, and went up the steps of the last house before Yumoto, and in.
