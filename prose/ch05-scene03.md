# Chapter 5, Scene 3 — On the Books

*Act 1, beat 13. Sazare Ruri, age eleven. POV: Sazare. ~2,600 words.*

---

They came for the Amagai in the second month, at about the fourth hour of the
morning, and Sazare slept through all of it.

He found out at the Academy, from the empty desk.

Nao Amagai sat in the third row by the window and had done for four years. She was
small and extremely serious and had once, in an argument about whether the
continent had an edge, produced a piece of reasoning so completely unanswerable
that Mizuho had stopped the lesson and made her say it again. She was the second
best in the year at the bowls and she was the only person who had ever beaten
Sazare at anything to do with sitting still, which she had done once, in the
eighth month, by eleven seconds, and had been unbearable about for a fortnight.

Her desk was empty and the room was quiet in a way Sazare had never heard a room
be quiet.

Mizuho came in and took the register and did not read out Amagai, and nobody said
anything about that either.

---

"They came in the night," said Jirō, at the break, in the corridor, with his back
to the wall and his voice at nothing. "My mum says four of them. Not the ordinary
ones. The other ones."

"What other ones?"

"The ones with the—" Jirō put a hand up to his face, and then took it down again
quickly, as though the gesture itself were a thing you could be caught doing.
"They didn't knock. They knocked, but they didn't wait."

"Where've they taken them?"

"I don't know."

"But your mum—"

"My mum does the *laundry*, Sazare," said Jirō, and his voice cracked on it, which
had begun happening that year and which he hated. "She washes sheets. She doesn't
know where they take people."

They stood in the corridor.

"They'll come back," said Sazare. "Won't they. It'll be like a — they'll ask them
things and then they'll come back."

Jirō looked at the floor.

"Nao's got a brother," he said. "He's nine. He's in the year below. He's not in
today either."

---

The Amagai were fifth on a list that Sazare had never seen and did not know
existed, and by the fourth month he could have recited it without ever having been
told a single name on purpose.

The Kanō, two years ago, four of them left by the end.

The Yuki, out in the hills, which nobody had known about at the time and everybody
knew about now.

The Momoi, off the east road, who had not had a bloodline at all.

The Nakashima, two doors down, about whom nothing had ever been established.

The Amagai.

Then the Chiwatari, in the fifth month, who were three streets over and who had
kept a stall on the cutting; and the Ōtaki, in the sixth, who were rivermen; and a
household on the west quay whose name Sazare never learned because by then he had
stopped asking, because asking was the one thing everybody agreed you did not do.

Nobody was told anything. There were no announcements and no notices on the board
outside the district office and nothing whatever in the way of an explanation, and
this turned out not to matter in the slightest, because a thing does not need to be
announced to be understood. It went round the market in a day. It went round the
Academy in an hour. By the sixth month there was a whole grammar for it that
everybody in the lower district had learned without anybody teaching it — you did
not say *taken*, you said *gone*; you did not say *why*, you said nothing; and when
a family went you did not go round, and you did not knock, and you did not pick
the washing up out of the gutter.

---

The Ruri were not touched.

Nothing came near them. There was no visit, no letter, no second form. In the ninth
month the intake register was collected again and it was a different clerk this
time, a younger man in a hurry who did it in eleven minutes and did not ask to see
anything, and Iwao complained for a week afterwards that the fellow had not even
been *interested*.

And in the seventh month, at dinner, on an evening that was in no other respect
distinguishable from any other evening, Sazare's father said the thing out loud.

---

It came out of nothing. Iwao had been on about the Ōtaki, who he had known slightly,
and he had been going round the same three sentences for about ten minutes the way
he did — *rivermen, that's all they were, I've had a boat off them* — and Ren had
been agreeing with him at intervals, and then Ren put his cup down and said:

"They're not going to touch us."

Nobody answered.

"I'm saying it because it wants saying," said Ren. "It's been sat in this room for
a year and a half and everybody's tiptoeing round it and I'd rather it was out."
He looked round the table. "They are not going to touch us. We're on the
quartermaster's books. We've been on the quartermaster's books for sixty years.
There's not a squad in this village that hasn't got something of ours on it — half
the corps is wearing us, Iwao. You can't take a household off the books in a night
and have the whole barracks come up short by the spring. It doesn't work. Somebody
has to sign it and nobody's going to sign it."

There was a pause.

"Well," said Iwao. "Yes."

"So we're all right," said Ren.

"We're all right," Iwao agreed.

And the two of them sat there at the table looking enormously, unbearably relieved,
and Sazare — who was eleven, and who had spent four months watching a desk in the
third row by the window — felt something happen in his chest that he could not have
named and did not like.

"Nao Amagai's dad made *nets*," he said.

Ren turned to him. "What?"

"He made nets. For the whole of the east quay. He did the deep-water ones, the big
ones, nobody else does the big ones." Sazare had his hands flat on the table on
either side of his bowl, which he had never done in his life and which he had got
from Iwao without knowing it. "So that's *the books* as well, isn't it. That's on
somebody's books. And they took them anyway, so it doesn't—"

"That's not the same."

"Why isn't it the same?"

"Because nets aren't—" Ren stopped.

He did stop. His father, who was the kindest man in that house and who had never in
his life left a sentence unfinished out of cowardice, got exactly that far and
stopped, because he had heard where it was going.

"Because there's four net-makers on the east quay," said Iwao, quietly, from the
other end of the table, "and there's one of us."

Sazare looked at his uncle.

"That's *worse*," he said.

"It's not worse, boy, it's—"

"It's *worse*. That's — that means they took them because they *could*, that means
if there were four of us they'd have—"

"Sazare," said his mother.

She had not spoken for the entire meal. She had not spoken much for a month.

"Eat your food," she said.

---

Afterwards he found her on the step, doing nothing, which she never did.

He sat down. She moved over.

"Is dad right?"

"About the books?"

"About us being all right."

His mother looked out at the yard for a long time. It was the seventh month and the
light went on until late and there was a gull on the Nakashimas' roof, one, standing
about on the tiles of an empty house with nothing to argue with.

"Your father is right that nobody has signed anything," she said.

"That's not the same as—"

"No," said Hisui. "It isn't."

They sat there.

"Nao was better than me at the bowls," said Sazare.

"Was she."

"Once. By eleven seconds. She wouldn't stop going on about it." His voice did
something and he stopped it. "She's got a brother. He's nine."

His mother put her arm round him, which she had done twice in his life, and he
sat there under it and was too old for it and did not move.

"Listen to me," she said. "Because I'm only going to say this once and then we're
not going to talk about it again."

"All right."

"If anybody ever asks you what you can do," said Hisui, "you tell them you make
earrings."

"That's what Mizuho said."

His mother's arm went very slightly tighter.

"Did she," she said.

"She said show them your second best."

"Then Mizuho is a better woman than I've given her credit for," said Hisui, "and
you'll do what she told you, and you'll do it every time anybody asks, for the
rest of your life, and you'll never once tell them how long you can hold the water
or what you did over two nights in the ninth month."

"But—"

"*Sazare.*"

"All right."

"Say it."

"I make earrings," he said.

"Good," said his mother. "That's good. That's exactly right."

They sat on the step until the light went, and neither of them said anything else,
and in the next room the frames hummed, all nine of them, none of them quite in
tune.
