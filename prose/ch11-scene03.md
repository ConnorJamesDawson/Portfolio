# Chapter 11, Scene 3 — Two Nights

*Act 2, part C. Sazare Ruri, age fifteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,300 words.*

---

He went to the tray in the dead of winter, on a night with snow on the
steam, and he did not decide to do it then either — that was twice now the
important doors of his life had opened without a hand on them — but this
time there was at least a shape to the not-deciding, and the shape was
Tose's wake, and a sentence of Tōan's that had been working in him since
the timber camp like grit in a lap.

*The body knows how. It has only to be reminded.*

He had been treating the growing as a door with something terrible behind
it. Sitting before the sand at midsummer he had gone to it the way you go
to a thing that must be forced — braced, breath held, everything in him
gathered to push — and the ice had come down the arm like a portcullis,
and fair enough. His body had watched the last opening of that door from
eleven yards. It knew exactly what lived behind it. It was not wrong to
bar it; it was doing the thing bodies do, in a tearing hurry, with scar.

But he was an assistant in a treatment house now, and he had held the
lamp while a man's panicking flesh was talked down out of its own
emergency a thread at a time, and somewhere between the crushed hand and
the lamps on the water the question had quietly changed shape. Not: *how
do I get through the door.* Rather: *what is the door afraid of, and what
would have to be true, and for how long, before it stood easier.*

Know your door, Tōan had said, about rooms.

So he did not sit down that first night to grow anything. He put that
away from the start, formally, out loud in the empty kitchen, feeling a
fool: "Nothing tonight. We're only sitting." And he sat before the tray
with his hands in his lap — not raised, not offered, in his *lap* — for
the length of one stick of incense, in the cold, with the snow ticking on
the shutters, doing the bowl and nothing else. Finding where the room
moved. Letting it.

The arm stayed warm.

He did that, and only that, every third night for a month. Hands in his
lap. Nothing offered, nothing asked. If ice came — twice it came, at
nothing, at a settle of the stove — he sat with it until it left, the way
you sit with a frightened patient, without argument, because Tōan had
taught him that fear in a body is information and you do not debate with
information, you wait for the rest of it.

In the second month he raised his hands.

Not over the tray. Over his knees — the start position, heels turned in,
a hand's width above his own legs, held for a count of sixty, then done
with. The next week, over the bench. The week after, over the cold stove.
Absurd, patient, dull as the stones and the knots and the spring-band —
he was rehabilitating a limb; he knew exactly how this went; the
programme was the programme whether the seam was in tendon or somewhere
the thread of light had never once found anything to touch.

The first time he held the start position over the sand itself, the ice
came like a wave and he sat on his hands on the bench, breathing around
the shut place, an hour.

The second time, less.

The ninth time, on a mild wet night at the front edge of spring, with the
thaw loud in the gorge and the tray levelled and damp under his palms —
nothing came at all.

The road lay open, with no traffic on it, and he sat at the top of it in
the lamplight for a long while, hands a hand's width off the sand, feeling
the medium below him the way you feel a pool before you enter it, and then
he said — out loud, in the empty kitchen, keeping terms with a body that
had kept its own terms honestly for two years — "Only a little. And slow."

And fed it.

---

There was no lattice. He had thought about the lattice for a month and in
the end had made one himself from stove wire, a single strand, bent and
bound to a scrap frame with waxed thread — a lattice the way a whittled
stick is a spoon — because the alternative was asking, and this was not a
thing that could be asked for, and anyway there was a rightness to it he
did not examine: everything on this bench was scrap. The tray was a
seed-flat. The sand had been sieved through cheesecloth. The heir of four
hundred years of the finest growing-benches in the Land of Water crouched
over an arrangement a tinker would have apologised for, and fed it the
first deliberate chakra of his new life one grain at a time.

It was the hardest thing he had ever done with his hands, and the whole
difficulty was in how little of it there was to do.

The crystal wanted to come. That was the thing no one had ever said plainly
in the north room, because in the north room it had never needed saying —
he understood his mother's forearms now, understood them in his own,
understood what the four hours had been *made of*: the medium answered like
a struck bell, eager, reaching, and every fibre of the trade was in the
refusal. Not yet. Not that fast. Not all of you. He held it to a trickle,
a grain's worth, a breath's worth, and when his pulse rose at the sheer
nearness of the thing — and once, horribly, at the flat memory of how it
had felt to simply *open the tap*, the ease of it, the relief of it, in a
square, in the sun — he stopped entirely, hands in his lap, and sat with
that too until it had said its piece and gone.

He worked perhaps two hours. Then his hands began to tell him lies about
their own steadiness, which he knew now to believe the opposite of, and he
stopped, and levelled a cloth over the tray, and went to bed.

The second night he worked three hours more, and stopped when it was
done.

Fourteen hours had been the boast at ten, over two nights, against every
rule of the craft. This was five, over two nights, because five was what
it needed and stopping mid-grow held no terrors for a boy who had once
been written up in a district annex for it. The join, where the first
night met the second, was there if you held it sideways to the lamp: a
fine seam of light, like the scar on his arm, like the line in the first
piece he had ever been proud of.

It was an earring. It was the length of a thumbnail, and it leaned, and
there was a grey hollowness at one end where his nerve had flickered on
the first night, and past that it ran clear to the tip.

A bad piece, better than the last bad piece — which had been a spike,
eleven yards, through a man.

He sat looking at it in the palm of his good hand, in the lamplight, in
the steam-country winter's-end night, five years and a world from the
sill where its sisters had grown, and he did not cry, particularly. What
he felt was narrower than that, and older, and it took him a while to
find its name, because he had not met it in so long: it was the plain
workman's satisfaction of the house he was raised in, his mother turning
a flawed thing twice under the light and finding the true sentence about
it.

*The lean's better than last time. You held it steadier at the start.*

"That's a good shape," he said, to the empty kitchen, for both of them.

---

In the morning the tray was re-levelled and the cloth folded and there
was nothing to see, and he told Tōan nothing, and the house's laws held.

But that evening, on the compounding bench, unremarked, between the good
scales and the jar of dried hops, there was a small coil of fine-drawn
copper wire — jeweller's gauge, bright as the day the boats stopped
bringing it, a year's careful lattices' worth — and no invoice ever
appeared for it in any book of the house that Sazare kept, and neither of
them said one word about it, that year or ever.
