# Chapter 71, Scene 4 — What Hinata Saw

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the road from the tower. Hyūga Hinata,
age nineteen. POV: Hinata. ~1100 words.*

---

Hinata made it out of the tower, down the stairs, across the
forecourt, and most of the way to the first street corner at a
completely ordinary walking pace, which she counted afterwards
as one of the more considerable achievements of her year.

Then she stopped at the corner, put one hand flat against the
wall the way you do when a stone has worked into your sandal,
and stood there breathing carefully while the thing she had
seen finished arriving.

*Oh,* she thought, with great precision, which was the whole
of her first analysis.

She had not meant to see it. That was the first thing she
established with herself, standing at the wall, because the
distinction mattered in her family the way water mattered in
a desert: she had not looked. The Byakugan had been dormant
the entire meeting — one did not attend a Hokage's tower with
the veins raised; it would be a declaration of war or at
minimum of terrible manners — and what she had seen, she had
seen the way any Hyūga past their sixth birthday saw
everything: because the clan's other inheritance, older than
the eye and harder-trained, was *attention*. You were drilled
in reading stance and weight and breath before you were
drilled in anything else. You learned that a person is a
sentence being spoken continuously, and that most of the
village went about functionally deaf, and that you did not
get to be deaf, ever, anywhere, because the eye's whole
discipline began with the habit of seeing.

So she had seen. Three words and a hand on a shoulder.

And the meeting had been — before that — already the most
quietly astonishing hour of her month, because Sazare-san had
turned out to be nothing the rumours knew what to do with. The
compound's version was a sort of story-shaped thing: the
crystal man, the incident on the training ground, the
underwear enterprise the branch-house kunoichi discussed in
the baths with an enthusiasm that had made her ears burn for
a week and given her the courage for the errand in the first
place. The person was: a young man who listened with his
whole body, wrote down her hardship clause *first*, before
the commercial terms, as if it were obviously the load-
bearing article — and never once, not once in an hour, looked
at her the way she had braced her whole walk over to be
looked at. He had looked at her the way her instructors
looked at a kata. It had been so restful she had nearly
cried, which she filed, at the wall, under things to examine
never.

And then the Hokage's hand had landed on his shoulder, and
the Hokage had said three words in a voice that thought it
was wearing its office —

— and Hinata, who had loved one person in complete silence
for approximately eleven years, who was the standing
five-nations authority on the exact art of containing a
feeling so that nothing of it reached a face, had heard the
containment fail at one-quarter volume and recognised the
note the way a musician's child recognises her own
household's instrument through a wall.

*They are—* she had thought, in the meeting, and stopped the
thought before it finished, out of courtesy, in her own
head.

At the wall, on the corner, she let it finish.

*They are in love. Deeply. Settled-in, lived-in love — not
the beginning kind; the kind with furniture in it. He built
his voice a room for her and doesn't know the door shows.
She has a hand that has landed on that shoulder so many
times it has stopped asking permission. And it is a secret —
of course it is a secret; the whole shape of it was a
secret's shape, the formality worn over the top like a coat
that has stopped fitting — and they are getting careless in
the specific way of people who have been safe too long, and
neither of them felt me see it, and he of all people should
have, because that man reads rooms the way we do, I watched
him do it to me for an hour.*

*Which means they are lucky it was me.*

She straightened up from the wall. She stood at the corner
in the noon traffic of an ordinary Monday, the heir of a
clan whose entire civilisation was built on seeing and on
the iron governance of what one did with the seen, and did
the accounting her whole life had trained her for, and it
took four seconds, because it was not actually difficult.

*It is not mine. I did not earn it, I was not given it, it
is not the clan's, and it is nobody's business including —
especially — the compound's, where information about the
Hokage's private life would be currency by nightfall and
policy by spring. What is seen and not offered is HELD.
Father says the eye is a responsibility before it is a
weapon. He is usually talking about war. It has never once
been more true than on this street corner.*

*I will hold it.*

*And—* and here she began walking again, because the last
part of the thought needed the cover of motion, being the
part about herself— *and I will go back on Wednesday, to
conclude the licensing schedule, and if there is a moment —
if there is any moment at all — I want to ask him. Not
about her. Never about her. About the other thing. Because
I have now watched a man who blushes to his ears say a
whole loving sentence in an open room with his voice level
and his hands still, and I have loved N— I have loved
someone for eleven years and never once finished his name
in my own head without my throat closing, and whatever it
is that man has built inside himself that lets the truth
OUT—*

*—I want to know how. I want to know if it can be learned,
like a kata. And I think — from the way he wrote my clause
down first — I think he might be the one person in this
village who would answer the real question without making
me say all of it.*

She walked home through the ordinary noon, holding a
Hokage's secret with perfect Hyūga stillness and her own,
eleven years older, folded up small beside it where it had
always lived, and if her byakugan-white eyes were a little
bright at the corners the whole way, the village went
about functionally deaf, and for once she was glad of it.
