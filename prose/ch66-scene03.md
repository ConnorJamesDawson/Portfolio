# Chapter 66, Scene 3 — One Name Long

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the Hokage's tower. Tsunade, age fifty-three.
POV: Tsunade. ~1690 words.*

---

The shortlist had been sitting on her desk for nine days pretending to
be a list.

Three names. She had written three because a decision paper with one
name on it is not a decision paper, it is a fait accompli with a
letterhead, and the council could smell those through a wall. So:
three names, two of them real people and one of them real enough, and
she had spent nine days constructing reasons to strike the two she was
always going to strike, so that the file would show a Hokage choosing
rather than a woman confirming what she had known on the twentieth,
standing on a closed training ground, watching a boy talk a growing
thing out of a jinchūriki's leg.

*Kakashi.* Struck. He had Team Seven, he had the weekly conditioning
slot already, and attaching a man to two jobs meant both jobs got the
half of him the other one dropped. Also — she wrote none of this down —
he had known the boy longest, and the whole point of a chain is that it
is not a friendship wearing a badge.

*A rotation of three from the standing ANBU roster.* Struck. Strangers
on a schedule. The boy would learn all three inside a week — read them
off their boots, their pouch flaps, the wear on their gloves — and be
guarding the guards' feelings by the second Friday. A chain he had to
manage was worse than no chain. And she was not putting a rotation of
anybody near the second floor of this tower. That thought filed itself
under operational security, where she let it stay, because it was even
true.

Which left the list at its real length.

One name, four letters, and an arrangement eighteen years old that the
name was all anybody got.

---

Shizune came in with the incident file at half past ten and found her
reading the sentence again. She had written it herself, on the
twenty-fourth, and it had not improved.

*A live article entered the subject's limb and was arrested by the
operator. Duration approximately forty seconds. No procedure existed
and none exists.*

"None exists," she said out loud. "I put that in a village record in my
own hand. Do you know what the last Hokage to write *no procedure
exists* about a walking asset did about it?"

"Founded the Corps," said Shizune.

"Founded the Corps." She flattened the draft order on the desk. "And I
am appointing one man with a teapot. Sit down, I want a witness while I
talk myself out of the alternatives one last time."

Shizune sat, took the pig off her lap and put her on the floor, and got
her pen ready, because minutes were minutes even when they were this.

"The requirement first," said Tsunade. "Not a guard. He is not in
danger from anything I can post a guard against, and the village is not
in danger from him in any way a guard could interrupt. Nobody alive can
stop that crystal except the boy. What was missing on the twentieth was
not force. It was—" she reached for it, and had it, because she had
been carrying it since he said it to her across this desk— "somebody
whose job it is to say no. He asked for it himself. Asked me to minute
that he'd asked. So the remit is three lines and I'm writing them
myself, because if the drafting office writes them there'll be eleven
clauses and a schedule of escalation and it'll mean less."

She wrote. It went down the way it had been sitting in her for nine
days, finished before it was started.

*The attachment is not able to prevent. It is able to refuse. Refusal
of any live working is effective immediately, appealable to the Hokage
alone, and carries no penalty for being wrong.*

"No penalty for being wrong," she said, going back over the last line.
"That's the part that makes it work. The day the chain hesitates
because a wrong no costs him something, it isn't a chain, it's a
colleague. Read it back."

Shizune read it back.

"Good. Now the name, and the part you're not going to minute."

Shizune put the pen down with a precision that was its own commentary,
and folded her hands, and waited.

"There is a man in this village," said Tsunade, "who has not asked me a
single question since the twentieth. Everyone else has. The council has
asked in committee, the clans have asked at dinner, the hospital board
asked in writing and I enjoyed declining it. He has asked nothing,
because he does not need to ask, because he knows that in the fullness
of time the personnel postings go up on the board like they always do,
and he can read the answer off a sheet like an honest man." She looked
out the window at the winter village, low sun on the monument, smoke
standing up straight from four hundred chimneys. "So the posting sheet
is the reply. I am attaching, to the most watched fabricator in the
Land of Fire, the one shinobi that man's old shop grew, owned, numbered
— and lost. He'll read four letters on a board and understand me
completely. It is the most courteous threat I have ever made and I have
been making threats since before you were born."

"And if it costs Tenzō?" said Shizune. "The arrangement was the
protection. You're hanging a lamp on him."

"I know exactly what I'm hanging on him," said Tsunade, "which is why
Kakashi went to him yesterday and not an order. I told Kakashi to build
the door properly. Full exit, no questions, a posting in the east if he
wanted the distance, and my word the file would show he was never
asked."

"And?"

"And Kakashi came back grinning underneath the mask, which on him is a
weather event, and reported that Tenzō listened to the whole door — the
whole thing, plans, dates, the eastern posting — thanked him for the
carpentry, and didn't use it." She let herself, briefly, take the
pleasure of it. "He said — and I am quoting my jōnin quoting my ANBU —
*the boy asked me a question half a year's worth of quiet ago, and this is
the seat with the view of the answer.*"

Shizune smiled down at her notepad, wrote nothing, let it pass.

"Effective Monday," said Tsunade. "Sixth. I'll read it to them both in
this office, formal, on the record, because the record is half the
point. And Shizune—" she leaned back, and the chair complained, and she
ignored it as she had for three years— "he will not like it."

"He asked you for a chain."

"He asked me for a chain in the abstract. Nobody dislikes a collar in
the abstract. Monday he gets a specific man, at his specific bench, on
specific hours, and that bench has been his since he was a boy in Hot
Water with one lamp. He'll comply. He'll be flawless about it. And
somewhere in the first ten minutes he'll say the thing he doesn't like,
politely, for the minutes, because he pays every toll in exact change —
and I want to see which thing it is."

"You're betting on which," said Shizune, in the flat voice she used for
official disapproval of things she was about to participate in.

"The bench clause. Sight unseen. If it's anything else, the good sake —
the one from the north cellar, not the diplomatic one — goes to you."

Shizune wrote *personnel matter, resolved* in the margin of her pad,
which was how the book was kept for wagers of this class, and then she
did look up, and her face had put its work away.

"For the actual minutes," she said. "Reason for selection. I need one
sentence that isn't the message to — that isn't the message."

Tsunade turned her chair a quarter, to the window, and gave the honest
one, and let it be minuted, because it was true enough to survive any
board that ever read it, and private enough that no board ever would
understand what it was reading.

"He is the only other man in the village," she said, "who is the last
holder of a thing somebody built by breaking children — and the only
one of the two of them who has had eighteen years of practice being a
person anyway. Write that as: *uniquely relevant operational
experience.*"

The pen went for a while.

"There's a last thing," said Shizune, when it stopped. "Friday's
report from the yard. Katsuyu's session." She said it carefully,
because they had rules now, she and the desk, about which channel a
thing arrived through. "Three attempts at the state. Three nothings.
He wrote it up himself and routed it through the training file, where
it belongs, so you're reading it as the Hokage."

"I know," said Tsunade, who had read it twice as the Hokage and once,
later, at one in the morning, as nobody in particular, standing at
this window while the second floor slept. "It doesn't come when he
calls it. Ninety seconds on the twentieth and not one second since."
She squared the draft order, signed it, dated it, and pushed it into
the folder with the incident report, where the two of them could argue
with each other in the dark. "Good. If it came when he called it, I'd
have a weapon on the second floor. As it stands I've got a mystery,
and I have spent fifty years learning which of those the village
survives better."

"You don't mean that last part," said Shizune, gathering the folder.

"I mean it four days out of five," said Tsunade. "On the fifth day I
want him to have everything, all of it, now, and the whole world can
learn to duck. Log the four days. Monday, six sharp — put it in the
diary as personnel, and put tea in the room. The real tea. If I'm
collaring the politest man I've ever met with the second politest, the
least this office can do is brew properly."
