# Chapter 47, Scene 5 — The Rose

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, a room over a closed tea house. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,819 words.*

---

He got the balance out of his bag.

It was Kanzaki's small brass one, the one she used for anything under a
hundred grams, signed out on a chit that said *field comparison — S.
Ruri* in her copperplate, and he had carried it across the village in a
folded towel and set it up on the table with the levelling feet before
he had entirely thought about how it would look.

Tenzō watched him do it.

"You brought scales," he said.

"I brought scales."

"To a meeting you were told you'd probably not be having, about a
question you hadn't asked yet, with a man whose name you didn't know."

"Yes." Sazare set the pan. "It's a five-minute test and I'd have been
extremely annoyed with myself if you'd said yes and I'd had to come
back, and I couldn't come back, because one of Kakashi's conditions is
that I don't."

Tenzō made a small sound that was not quite a laugh.

"He said you'd be like this."

"He says that to everybody about me and he's always right and it's
becoming tiresome." Sazare levelled it. "Whenever you're ready. Small.
Genuinely small — a gram would do, this is good to a hundredth."

---

Tenzō sat for a moment with his hands on the table.

Then he made a rose.

He did not stand up and he did not make a seal, and there was a moment
of about a second and a half in which the room changed in a way Sazare
could not have described and which he wrote four attempts at that night
and got none of them, and then there was a rose lying across the man's
open palm.

Stem about seven inches. Four thorns, unevenly spaced, one of them
crooked. Two leaves. The bud just past the tight stage and beginning to
go, in the ordinary way of a rose at that hour, pale — not white,
something under white, the green of the sap coming through the outer
petals near the base the way it does on the cheap old varieties that
grow up walls in yards.

It was alive. That was the thing. The stem was green wood and it had a
bend in it where it had grown towards a light that had not existed, and
one of the leaves had a fault at the margin, a small brown nothing, the
sort of blemish any leaf in the world has.

Sazare looked at it for some time.

*He did not make a plank,* he thought. *I asked a man who can put a
forest across a valley for something that weighs a gram and he gave me
a flower with a bad leaf on it.*

*Four thorns and one of them crooked. He is not doing this for the
first time.*

*He has done this a very great number of times, alone, with nobody
watching, and he has got so good at it that he has stopped taking the
faults out.*

He did not say any of that.

"Thank you," he said. "May I?"

---

*11.14 g.*

He wrote it down.

"Now you," he said. "Stand on nothing, I've got no floor scale — hold
the pan post, no. Here." He got out the second thing in the bag, which
was a spring balance off the requisition store rated to a hundred
kilos, and a webbing strap. "It's crude. It's good to about fifty
grams either way and that's not good enough to catch a gram, but it's
good enough to catch a hundred, and if you'd lost eleven grams making
that we'd be looking for something else anyway. Sit in the loop."

"You want to weigh me on a *sack scale*."

"I want to weigh you on a sack scale twice."

Tenzō sat in the loop.

*Before: 68.9 kg ± 0.05.*

Then Sazare had him make eleven more roses, one after another, until
there were twelve of them lying on the table in a row like a market
stall, a hundred and thirty-odd grams of living matter that had not
existed at two o'clock — and then weighed him again.

*After: 68.9 kg ± 0.05.*

"Nothing," said Tenzō.

"Nothing to fifty grams, which is the limit of this instrument and not
the limit of the finding. I'd want a proper beam and I'd want you not
sweating and I'd want four repeats." Sazare was writing fast. "But
you've just put a hundred and thirty-four grams of wood, leaf and sap
on this table and the only thing in this room that weighs less than it
did is the tea."

"And you've done this on yourself."

"Nine times. Same answer. I don't lose mass. I lose something and it
doesn't weigh anything."

He put the pencil down.

"That's it," he said. "That's the test. It's five minutes' work and
it's the whole of what I came for and it doesn't collapse."

---

Then he picked up the first rose.

He had been going to put it in the folded towel with the balance and he
had it between two fingers and about nine inches off the table when he
stopped.

*What.*

He turned it over.

*What is that.*

It was not a smell and it was not a temperature and it was nothing he
could have put in front of a board, and he stood in a bare room over a
closed tea house holding a flower and went at it the way he went at a
casting that had rung wrong — the same reflex, forty thousand times
since he was seven.

*It's full.*

*That's the word and it's a bad word and I'm going to have to do better
than that eventually and I am not going to do better than that this
afternoon.*

*It is full of something and I know the sensation and I have known it
since I was seven years old and I have never once had it from anything
I did not make myself.*

He sat down slowly.

"Sazare-san?"

"Give me a minute."

He held the rose in his left hand and got out the tin with the other
and took out one grain of seeded stock and put it on the table, and put
a fingertip on it, and did the smallest thing he could do — not a
course, barely a load, the amount that goes into a seat when you check
a seat.

The grain came up.

He held the two of them, one in each hand, for about twenty seconds.

*The same.*

*Not similar. Not analogous. Not "there is an interesting parallel
here" — I have spent two years being careful about that word and this is
not that.*

*A crystal I have loaded and a rose he has grown feel THE SAME, in the
hand, in the identical way, and I could not tell them apart blind, and
there is nothing whatsoever in common between the two substances. One
is a silicate lattice and one is cellulose and water and neither of
them has any business feeling like anything at all.*

*It is not in the material.*

*It is in what has been put into the material, and it is the same
thing, and he got his from a forest in the Land of Fire and I got mine
from a yard in Konoha and neither of us knows where either of them came
from.*

---

He was aware, distantly, that he had been silent for rather a long
time, and that Tenzō was waiting with the patience of a man who had
spent his life in rooms where waiting was the job.

"Can you feel it?" said Sazare.

"Feel what?"

"The rose. In your hand. Is there anything in it — anything at all,
after it's finished, that you can — " He put it down, because he could
hear himself failing. "Sorry. I'll do it properly. When you've made a
thing and it's done and it's lying there, can you tell it apart from an
ordinary one? By touch, with your eyes shut."

Tenzō picked one up.

He held it for a while.

"Yes," he said. "Yes, obviously — I've always been able to. I could
find mine in a hedge." He turned it over. "It's not a *feeling*, it's —
it's like the difference between a room somebody's just left and a room
nobody's been in."

"How long does it last."

"It doesn't go." He said it as though it were the least interesting
thing he had said all afternoon. "I've got one at home from about nine
years ago. It's dead — it dried out and went brown like anything does
— and it's still got it."

Sazare put both hands over his face for a moment, which he had not done
in front of another person in his life.

"Nine years," he said.

"Is that—"

"Mine goes at six days," said Sazare. "The order in it. I've measured
it fifty times out to nine days and I put three days on the label to be
safe and I have spent fourteen months treating that as a fact about the
world."

---

He packed the balance.

Tenzō watched him do it, and then said, "Take them."

"The roses?"

"I can't do anything with twelve roses and I'd have to explain them to
somebody, and they'll dry in about a fortnight and be brown for the next
forty years." He pushed them across. "Take the lot. Weigh them every
week. That's what you want them for and you were working out how to ask
for four of them without seeming greedy, and you'd have taken four and
you want twelve."

Sazare stopped with the towel half folded.

"That's rung three," he said.

"I'm sorry?"

"There's a ladder. Object, object in use, habit, intention, feeling. I
was taught it two years ago and I'm fluent at the bottom and I'm
useless at the top and I've just been done at rung three by somebody
I've known for two hours." He wrapped the roses. "Thank you. Genuinely.
For the afternoon and for the twelve and for the answer, which is worth
more than all of it and which cost you something I don't fully
understand and am not going to ask about."

Tenzō stood up.

"Can I ask you one," he said.

"Yes."

"You said cities are worse for me. Burnt ground." He was doing up the
jacket, and his hand went, without any part of him appearing to notice,
to the third button down on the left. "Is it worse for you too?"

"Forty-four per cent," said Sazare. "Rubble off the invasion streets.
It's the worst medium I've ever worked and I've been calling it damage
for eleven months."

Tenzō looked at the window for a moment.

"Then it isn't the trees," he said.

"No."

"And it isn't the stone."

"No."

"Then what's in the ground?"
