# Chapter 30, Scene 2 — The Stairs

*Act 4, part C. Konoha, the tower. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~1,499 words.*

---

The summons came on the ninth day and it came the way Kakashi had said
it would, which was as a person rather than a form.

The person was a chūnin of about twenty with a runner's armband and no
particular interest in the errand, who arrived on the second floor at
the fourth hour of the afternoon, established that Sazare was Sazare,
and said, "Tomorrow, second hour, Hokage's office, bring the device and
anything it's written down in," and left before Sazare had finished
asking which device.

"There's only one device," Handa said.

"There are six. Five of them failed."

"Then bring all six and let her sort it out, and stop looking for a
reason to be late."

---

He did not sleep well and did not pretend to himself that he had.

He got up at the third hour and worked at the low table until the
window went from black to grey, and what he did in that time was
inventory, which was what he did with every hour he could not sleep
through and had done since he was seven years old and had first learned
that a room could be counted when it could not be left.

Six devices in a lined tin: five failures wrapped separately in waxed
paper with their dates in his own hand, one spent success in a labelled
dish borrowed from Kanzaki, who had told him to bring the dish back and
had not asked what for. The notebook. The trial file's carbon, which
Ashida had made for him without being asked and had not mentioned
making. Urushibara's twelve elements and the thirteenth, and a fourth
sheet with the *seal on the seal* worked out in three versions, two of
them wrong.

The tin with the six grains, because he could not leave the room
without it and had not been able to for four months.

He did not bring the forearm plate. It had come back from evidence
wrapped in untreated cotton in the sixth week and had sat on the shelf
beside the empty picture frame ever since, and there was no version of
that morning in which carrying it into that building improved anything.

At the sixth hour he did the ward round, because he was still on at
six and had not asked to be taken off it, and found that doing it
steadied him more than another hour of counting objects would have. Bed
one's hand had begun to close past the second knuckle. Bed nine
wanted the window open and bed twelve wanted it shut, and Sazare
brokered a settlement involving the top sash and a folded towel that
satisfied neither of them and stopped the argument, which was the
whole of what a ward lead was for.

At half past the first hour he washed, changed into the clean set, and
found Yagi waiting at the bottom of the stairs with her arms folded.

"Knee," she said.

"Thirty-eight degrees."

"Show me."

He showed her. She watched the tracking with her head tilted and said
nothing about the four degrees he had put on it in nine days by doing
more repetitions than the sheet specified, which meant she had already
seen the sheet and had decided to have that conversation on a different
day.

"Two things," she said. "One. You are going to want to explain the
failures before she asks about them, because you'll think getting
there first is honest. It isn't. It's managing her. Answer what she
asks in the order she asks it."

"And the second?"

Yagi considered him for a moment with the particular level attention
she gave a dressing she was deciding whether to disturb.

"She'll be right about things you don't want her to be right about,"
she said. "She's been doing this since before your mother was born,
and she is better at it than anybody currently alive, and you have
taught yourself out of books and off benches in four countries with
nobody to correct you. Some of what she says is going to take the legs
out from under work you're proud of." She unfolded her arms. "You will
want to defend it. Don't. Write it down instead and defend it in a
month, when you've checked whether she was right, which she will have
been."

"That's not reassurance."

"No. It's the thing that's actually useful, which is what you asked me
for the first week you were here, and I have not yet found a reason to
start giving you the other kind."

---

The village had changed in the eleven weeks since he had last walked
its whole length, and he read it the way he read everything, without
deciding to.

Timber props had come down off most of the Nakadōri frontages and been
replaced with real work: new sills, new lintels, a whole shopfront in
raw cedar three doors up from the fish trestles that had not yet
weathered to match anything either side of it. Somebody had re-laid
forty feet of the market row in new setts and had done it badly, with
the falls running to the middle instead of the gutters, which meant
that stretch would hold water every time it rained until somebody took
it up and did it again.

There were more people than there had been, and fewer bandages, and a
memorial board outside the tower with the notices four deep and the
oldest ones gone soft and pale at the corners.

He stopped at the bottom of the tower steps and looked up at it.

The last time he had climbed those stairs he had been item six, and had
stood in a corridor in front of a slate reading *Amnesty application —
Ruri*, and had understood, with genuine relief, that a man who is one
of seven things on a list is a man nobody has bothered to be
frightened of.

He did not know what he was today. That was the whole of the problem
and it had taken him nine days to identify it. He had a name for
almost every kind of fear he owned — the specific one about the bingo
book, the specific one about a door opening at the wrong hour, the
specific one about a mind-walker's hand and twenty-two minutes — and he
did not have a name for this one, because this one was not about being
taken, or priced, or removed.

It was that he had built something on benches, alone, wrong four times
out of five, and in about twenty minutes the best medical mind alive
was going to look at it and know exactly how good it was.

And he wanted to know the answer.

He stood at the bottom of the steps for four seconds longer than the
climb required, and identified that as the actual shape of it, and went
up.

---

The corridor at the top had the same slate on the wall by the door,
chalked in a different hand.

*1. Border returns — NW, NE*
*2. Reconstruction estimates (Public Works)*
*3. Academy intake — emergency numbers*
*4. Medical corps establishment*
*5. Kiri — preliminary*
*6. Any other*

He read it twice.

There was no item with his name on it. He looked for it out of habit,
found nothing, and had begun to construct an entirely reasonable
explanation for why a technician's appointment would be dealt with
under item four when a woman came out of the door carrying a stack of
folders with a black pig asleep on top of them.

She was small, dark-haired, about thirty, in a black kimono with the
sleeves pinned back for work, and she had the specific harried
competence of somebody who had been the only organised person in a
room for several consecutive hours.

"Sazare Ruri?"

"Yes."

"Shizune. I've read your file." She shifted the folders. The pig did
not wake. "All of it, twice, and the second time was because I thought
I'd misread the entry about the coat pocket."

"You hadn't."

"No. That was my conclusion also." She looked at him with an
expression he could not immediately place and later identified,
walking home, as pity of a very specific kind — not for anything that
had happened to him, but for the next hour. "She's got the whole thing
spread over the desk. She's been at it since the sixth hour."

"Since six?"

"She had two Council members at seven and she moved them." Shizune
adjusted the folders again. "I want you to understand that that is not
a small thing and it is not going to be mentioned by anybody in that
room, including me, once we're through the door."

"Why tell me at all?"

"Because you strike me as a person who works out what things cost
afterwards, on his own, at night, and gets it wrong." She put her free
hand on the door. "Ready?"

He was not.

"Yes," he said.
