# Chapter 20, Scene 1 — The Signs

*Act 3, part B. Nami, before dawn. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare.
~1,800 words.*

---

He knew it was that morning at about the ninth hour of the night before,
and he did not know it from anything anybody said.

He knew it from four things, and he had them laid out on the bench in
front of him in his head by midnight the way he had laid out the parts of
a lock, and every one of them was the sort of thing that only means
something to a person who has spent six weeks reading a town.

**The workmen did not draw their tools.**

Tazuna's crew — eight of them, and it had been eight since the summer, and
before that eleven and before that nineteen — kept their gear in a locked
store at the head of the causeway and drew it on the way out, first light,
every working day. On the Thursday evening the store was locked and the
tally-board beside it, which the foreman chalked with who had what, had
been wiped and not rewritten.

He had walked past it twice and the second time he stopped.

**Two of Gatō's men had been in the town at supper time.**

Not doing anything. That was what made it. They had come along the plank
walk at about the fifth hour, unhurried, and had stood at the top of the
walk for perhaps ten minutes looking at nothing in particular, and had
gone away again; and every door on the walk had shut, quietly, in a
sequence, the way they do.

Men who come to a town to break something break it. Men who come to a town
and stand about for ten minutes are men letting the town know that it has
been counted.

**The tide.**

Low water was at a quarter past seven on the Friday morning. That mattered
for exactly one reason and he was possibly the only person in the Land of
Waves other than Tazuna who had thought about it: the last three piers
were the deep ones, and the mainland end of the causeway had a shingle
foreshore under it that was walkable at low water and under six feet of
green sea at high, and anybody who wanted to be *under* that bridge rather
than on it had a window of about four hours, and the window opened at
five.

**And there would be fog.**

He stood at the loft window at the eleventh hour with the shutter back and
his tea gone cold in his hands, and looked at the sky, and there was no
wind at all. The bay lay flat and black and the town's few lamps stood on
it in long unbroken lines. The air on the back of his hand was wet and
still and about two degrees off the water temperature.

Sea fog by four. Thick by six. Gone by ten if the sun got up, and it
would.

*On Friday morning there will be fog on the bridge,* Haku had not said,
because Haku had said nothing whatever about Friday; and Sazare stood at a
window above a mudflat in a foreign country and understood that he had
known the date for three days and had simply declined to write it down.

---

He did not sleep and he did not pretend to try.

He sat on the mat with his back against the wall and the lamp out and the
tide going under the floor, and did the honest accounting, and it came out
in three parts.

**The first part was that there was nothing he could do.**

He went at that from four directions because he always did and it held
from all four. He could not warn Tazuna's household; the man with the
crutch had known for a week and had built the last eight days around it,
and a foreigner arriving at the door at midnight with information he could
not account for would achieve exactly one thing, which was to become the
most interesting person in the Land of Waves to a jōnin.

He could not go to Gatō's people and he did not need to think about that
one for longer than a breath.

He could not fight. That was not modesty and it was not despair; it was
the flattest and most professional judgement he had ever made about
himself. There would be, on that bridge, in about eight hours: a man who
had taken Kubikiribōchō off Kuriarare Kushimaru on a beach; a boy who
could put a needle through a knee at forty feet; and a Konoha jōnin who
had beaten the first of those and who had spent eleven days doing nothing
but getting his system back. Sazare Ruri had four seconds, a bad knee, and
a bag of tools.

He would not last the length of the first exchange and his corpse would
not even be a complication.

**The second part was that he was going anyway.**

He noticed this without having decided it, at about the second hour, when
he found that he had packed the roll and put out the good boots.

**And the third part was the one he sat with until it got light.**

There was one thing available. Exactly one, and it was not on the bridge
and it would not work, and he could give a completely rigorous account of
why it would not work and he was going to do it regardless.

He could go up the shore and stand in front of Haku and ask him not to.

---

He left the loft at the third hour with the fog already coming.

It came in off the flats the way it always did — not rolling, not
dramatic; it simply began to be there, in the low places first, so that
the mud under the plank walk went white while the roofs were still
visible, and the far pines went and then the near ones, and the lamp at
the top of the walk acquired a halo and then a body and then was a smudge
and then was nothing.

By the time he was over the ridge he was walking in it: a close wet white
in every direction, with the world reduced to about eleven feet of wet
bracken and pine root and the sound of his own boots, and everything
beaded on his sleeves and his eyelashes and the ends of his hair.

It smelled of salt and cold stone.

He had grown up in this. That was the thing that came up out of the ground
at him on that path and he had not expected it and had to stop twice. Not
a memory — the *substance*. The lower district was in that fog: the
drying frames going hoarse, and the wall at the top of the market where
the fish smell stopped, and his mother's voice from four feet away saying
*mind the tray* to somebody she could not see, and a woman on a step
saying *no* and *no* and *yeah*, and four hundred people standing in a
half-circle in weather exactly like this, facing one thing.

He walked four miles in the fog of his own childhood and did not once put
his hand out to steady himself, because there was nothing to put it on.

---

He came down onto the north shore at about the fifth hour with the sky
going the colour of a dirty sheet somewhere above the fog, and the house
was lit and both of them were awake, and he had known they would be.

He stopped forty yards short at the top of the shingle, where he could be
seen from the veranda, and stood still with his hands out from his body,
the way he had done in a road for a charcoal burner.

He stood there for about ninety seconds.

Then the door opened and Haku came out onto the boards Sazare had laid,
barefoot, in the white, with his hair down loose over one shoulder and a
grey travelling cloak over the plain kimono; and he stopped on the top
step with his hand on the frame and looked out into the fog at the shape
of him.

He was not surprised. Sazare could see that from forty yards even in that
light and it landed on him like a hand.

"You're up early," said Haku.

"Yes."

"Have you eaten?"

"No."

Haku considered that for a moment, and then said, in the way of somebody
settling a small domestic question:

"Come down to the water. Don't come to the house."

---

They walked out along the tideline together to where the shingle ran out
onto the flat, forty feet apart at first and then, without either of them
arranging it, about six.

The tide was three quarters out and going, and the fog sat on the flats so
thickly that the sea was audible and invisible: it went in and out over
the shingle somewhere off to the left, hissing on the small stones and
then drawing back through them with a sound like a long indrawn breath,
and the whole world was eleven feet of wet grey shingle and a white wall.

Their boots — Haku's bare feet — printed and filled behind them.

Sazare had spent the whole four miles building the argument.

He had it in three parts, in order, with the objections anticipated, and
he had tested each one against the version of Haku he had spent nine days
assembling, and he had thrown two others away entirely because they were
insulting; and he had got, on that walk, as close as he ever got to
believing that a thing could be *solved* — that this was a mechanism with
a fault in it and that he had found the fault and that if he set it out
correctly, in the right order, to a person he had watched listen properly
to a lecture about the casting line on a pot lid —

Haku stopped on the wet shingle and turned round and said, before Sazare
had opened his mouth:

"You're going to try to talk me out of it."

"Yes."

"Good," said Haku. "I'd have thought less of you if you weren't going to,
and I would like to hear it. Properly, I mean — I'm not being polite. I
have never once in my life heard anybody put it, and I have wondered
what it sounds like from outside."

He put his hands in his sleeves against the cold.

"Take as long as you want. We've an hour."
