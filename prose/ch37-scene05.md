# Chapter 37, Scene 5 — Where People Look

*Act 4, part D. Konoha. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare.
~1,435 words.*

---

She asked it at the seventh hour, without any warning at all, in the
middle of the tidying-up part of the evening.

"Can I ask you something rude."

"Yes."

"You don't want to think about it first?"

"If I think about it first I'll construct four defensive positions and
answer from the least useful one." He put his cup down. "Ask it."

Tsunade looked at him for a second, and then did.

"Why don't you ever look at me?"

---

Sazare, who had spent three years being taught by a man in a training
ground that the top rung was not seeing but responding, and who had
now had four seconds in which to do neither, said:

"I look at you constantly. I know what you're wearing. I could draw the
haori."

"That is not what I asked and you know it isn't."

"No."

"Then answer it."

He sat with it.

The place had six tables and four of them were empty. The woman behind
the counter was doing the thing with the cloth that meant an hour left
rather than a quarter of an hour. Somebody outside went past whistling
something badly.

"I don't know how," he said.

"That's not an answer either."

"It's the whole of the answer and it's going to take me about four
minutes to make it into a sentence, and I'd like you not to help,
because if you help I'll take the version you offer."

Tsunade folded her arms and waited.

---

"I've got two systems for a body," said Sazare.

"Go on."

"The first one's medical. I've been putting my hands on people for four
years. I take pulses, I move limbs, I hold shoulders still, I stand a
woman up under both arms, I count respirations off a chest for a minute
at a time with my hand on it. I'm good at it and I don't think about
it and nobody has ever flinched. That's a complete system and it works
and it has got nothing whatsoever to do with what you're asking."

"And the second?"

"The second's the trade. A body's a frame. Cloth hangs off it, and how
it hangs tells you how the cloth was made and who for and whether it's
been altered." He said it flatly, because it was flat. "I could give
you your build to within about six pounds. I do that with everybody —
I lift patients, I've been estimating weight off a doorway for years
and I'm accurate to about four per cent. Your shoulders are broader
than they read because the haori's cut wide at the yoke. You carry your
weight forward, which is a fighter's habit, and you stand with your
feet further apart than a woman your height would if she'd never been
taught anything. The obi's tied high, which is a choice, and it's
tied by you and not by a dresser because the knot's the fast one."

Tsunade did not say anything.

"That's what I see," said Sazare. "All of it. Constantly. I have never
once been able to stop and I have never once wanted to."

"And the third system."

"There isn't one."

---

"I want to be exact," he said, after a moment, "because I've spent four
months finding out that I'm not exact about myself and it's cost me."

"Take as long as you want. The woman with the cloth will throw us out
when she wants us out."

"I'm eighteen. Five weeks ago, apparently." He turned the cup without
lifting it. "I have never kissed anybody. I've never wanted to that I
can identify, and I want to be careful about the word *identify*,
because I have found out this year that I have been running around
inside my own head with about four things badly labelled."

"That's fair."

"When I was reading chapter five — the one about the hand and the
shoulder — there's a paragraph in it about how most people acquire this
language before they can speak. And I sat in that library and worked
out that there's a whole category of thing that everybody in this
village does constantly, all day, in every room, and that I have been
watching them do for six years and have filed under *behaviour I do not
understand the purpose of*."

He stopped.

"I thought it was a lesser activity," he said. "That's the honest
version. For about six years I thought it was something people did
because they weren't concentrating. I have watched men look at you in
a corridor and I have catalogued *where* they looked and *for how
long* and used it to work out who in the room was going to be difficult
and who was going to be easy, and I have never once, in the whole of
that, run the thing they were running."

"On me or on anybody?"

"On anybody. Ever." He made himself say the rest of it. "And I did not
know that was unusual until about the eighth month of this year, and I
have spent five weeks working out whether it's damage."

---

Tsunade sat with that for a considerable time.

He had expected — he was clear about this afterwards and wrote it
down — one of about four things: reassurance, deflection, a joke, or
the medical answer. She gave him none of them, and what she did instead
was ask another question.

"When you were thirteen," she said. "On the boat. And the two years
after that."

"Yes."

"Where were you sleeping?"

"Different places. Eleven lodgings across two countries. Never twice
in the same one if I could—"

"That's the answer," said Tsunade.

"That's not—"

"Ruri. I have been a medic for forty years and about a quarter of that
was field work." She leaned forward. "You cannot do that with the part
of the brain that does the other thing. Not both. It's not a moral
fact and it isn't a fault of character, it's *load*. That part of a
person needs slack — safety, and time, and a repeated situation where
nothing bad is going to happen — and you have not had one single day of
those three at the same time since you were nine years old."

"I've had two years here."

"You've had two years of a hospital, a treaty, a bounty, an invasion,
a mind-walk and a bloodline agreement, and you have been keeping four
hundred people alive and a schedule running through all of it." She
sat back. "I'd be considerably more worried about you if that had
turned up on time. It doesn't. It turns up when there's room, and
there has been no room."

"That's a mechanism."

"It's the mechanism. And I'll tell you the second half, because you'll
go home and try to do something about it otherwise." She said it
completely levelly. "You cannot make it happen. You cannot practise it,
you cannot run it as an exercise, and if you go home tonight and sit at
that bench and *try*, you will get nothing, and you will conclude that
something is wrong with you, and you will be wrong and it will take you
a year to stop believing it."

Sazare said nothing.

"You're going to try anyway," said Tsunade.

"Yes."

"Of course you are." She almost smiled. "Then do it once, badly, get
the nothing, write it down, and come back and tell me on Thursday. I'd
rather you did it under supervision than spent a winter on it."

---

The last thing she said about it, she said standing up, with her coat
half on.

"For what it's worth."

"Go on."

"Everybody looks. That's not — I'm not complaining about it, I stopped
having an opinion about it around the time I stopped being twenty, and
about half of them are perfectly pleasant people who cannot help it
and are embarrassed." She got the second arm in. "You have sat opposite
me four times a month for a year and you have never once done it, and
about eleven weeks in I noticed, and about four months in I decided
that it was going to be a problem eventually, because I could not tell
whether it was manners or indifference and both of those are things
I've had before."

"And now?"

"And now it's neither and it's worse and better than both, and I'm
extremely glad I asked." She put her collar up. "It's why I asked
rudely. If I'd asked politely you'd have given me the manners answer
and we'd have had the wrong conversation for another year."
