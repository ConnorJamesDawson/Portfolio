# Chapter 48, Scene 1 — The Stair

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the Hokage's tower. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen.
POV: Sazare. ~2,165 words.*

---

She came down the outside stair.

That was the thing he had not expected and could not have constructed —
he had built four versions of the next ten minutes on the way up the
hill and every one of them had him going up and being taken through a
door and standing in front of a desk, and instead there was the sound
of somebody coming down fast in the dark and then Tsunade was on the
step above him in a shirt with the sleeves already pushed back and no
haori and her hair not up.

"Show me," she said.

He held it out.

She took his wrist — both hands, one above and one below, the grip a
medic uses and not the grip a person uses — and turned it into the
light from the stair lamp and looked at it for about four seconds
without moving.

He watched her face.

It did nothing at all. It did the thing it did perhaps twice a season,
where the entire working apparatus went still all at once so that what
was left was not an expression but the absence of one, and he had never
in two years been able to read what was underneath it and could not
read it now.

"Petrification," said Tsunade.

"You know what it is."

"I know what it's called." She turned the hand over. "Sensation?"

"None below the line. The line's at the mid-metacarpal on the index and
it's staggered — it's further up on the index and middle and it hasn't
touched the thumb."

"Pain."

"None. At any point."

Her thumb went to the inside of his wrist.

"Pulse eighty-eight," she said, "which is *ridiculous*, and I want that
noted as an observation and not as a compliment. What were you
touching?"

---

He got the rose out of his coat with his right hand.

Tsunade looked at it.

She did not ask what it was and she did not ask where it had come from,
which told him she had already got both, and she stood on a stair at
five past eleven at night holding an eighteen-year-old's wrist in one
hand and looking at a dead rose in the other and said one word, quietly,
to nobody.

"That's a Mokuton article," she said. "Nine days, ten? It's had the
water out of it. All right. You held it and it filled you, and I want
to know for how long and how hard, and then we're going—"

"No."

"...No?"

"I wasn't holding it," said Sazare. "I mean I was holding it. It wasn't
the holding."

He held up his right hand, which had a sheet of clean paper in it with
something wrapped in it, and he had been holding it the entire time and
she had not looked at it once because she had been looking at the other
one.

He opened the paper against his chest.

Nine inches of green stem. Two leaf pairs. Four thorns, the third one
crooked. A bud about a third open and stopped there.

"That came out of my palm at half past ten," he said.

---

Tsunade did not move for four seconds.

He had learned, over two years, what her face did when the working
apparatus went still, and he had never once been able to read
underneath it, and he was not able to read underneath it now — but he
had never before seen the thing that happened next, which was that she
took her hand off his wrist.

She took her hand off a live petrification to look at a piece of wood.

Then she put it back, fast, and said, in a voice with nothing whatever
in it:

"Upstairs."

"You haven't finished the—"

"I have finished on this staircase," said Tsunade. "I am now going to
finish somewhere with a door on it, because you have just walked up a
public hill carrying the single most dangerous object in this village
in a sheet of paper with the *time* written on the outside, like a man
bringing flowers."

"Not the office," she said. "The room at the end, the one with the
table."

"There's a guard on the—"

"There's a guard on the stair who has just watched the Hokage come down
it at a run in her shirt, and by tomorrow morning that is going to be a
story about a man being brought in, and I will deal with that
tomorrow, and I would like you to understand that I have already
decided that dealing with it tomorrow is cheaper than anything I could
do about it tonight." She was already going up. "Move."

---

The room at the end had a scrubbed table, a lamp, a sink and a locked
cupboard, and it was clearly a room where people were occasionally
worked on and equally clearly not a room anybody wanted to be in.

She put him on a stool and the hand flat on the table and did it
properly.

He knew what a proper examination looked like from the inside and this
was the fastest he had ever been on the receiving end of one. Four
minutes. Sensation mapped with a pin along nine lines. Temperature by
the back of her hand at eleven points. Capillary refill above the line,
which was normal, and below the line, which was nothing, because there
was nothing to refill.

Then the green went on.

He had felt her diagnostic twice before — once through a stiff shoulder
in an office in the fifth month of the previous year and once at a
distance in a corridor when she had not known he was watching — and
this was a different animal entirely. It went in above the line and ran
down and he could feel it perfectly well the whole way, and then it got
to the line.

And stopped.

Not blocked. Not resisted.

*It stopped the way a hand stops at the edge of a table,* he thought,
*because there is nothing past the edge to put a hand on.*

Tsunade took it off.

She ran it again from a different angle, and then a third time, slower,
and on the third one he watched her jaw.

"Say it," he said.

"Give me a minute."

"You've run it three times and the third one was slower than the second
and you've stopped looking at my hand and started looking at the
table."

"*Give me a minute*," said Tsunade, "because I am about to say a
sentence I have said perhaps four times in forty years and I would like
to get it right."

He gave her the minute.

---

"There's nothing there," she said.

"There's a hand there."

"There is an object there in the shape of a hand and it has your
fingerprints on it and it is not tissue." She sat down opposite him,
which put them at a level, which she did not do. "Listen, because this
is the part that matters and I am going to be exact.

"Everything I do, I do to a process. That's the whole discipline. I
don't heal anything — I have never healed anything in my life. I find
what the body is already trying to do and I put chakra behind it and I
make it go faster and go straighter and not stop. A cut is closing
before I get there. A bone is knitting. The graft in the rear yard was
trying to hold and needed eight seconds of somebody else's patience."

"The receiver contributes."

"*The receiver contributes*, and you gave me that sentence at four in
the morning in my own office and I have quoted it to two committees."
She put her hand flat next to his. "Stone doesn't contribute. There is
no process. There is nothing in that hand that is trying to be a hand,
and so there is nothing for me to get behind, and I have run three
diagnostics on the best equipment in the world — which is me — and the
answer is that my entire discipline does not apply to this."

The room was very quiet.

"So the one injury you can't touch," said Sazare.

"Is the one where the patient has stopped being a participant. Yes."
Tsunade looked at him. "You worked that out in about two seconds."

"It's the same finding. It's my finding. It's the reject table." He
looked at the thing on the end of his arm. "Site rubble is forty-four
per cent because it's been shocked and cracked and it can't take up an
instruction any more. It's not that I'm worse at it. It's that there's
nothing on the other side."

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't do the thing where you make it interesting," said Tsunade, "and
I say that as somebody who has done it in this exact room at three in
the morning over her own hands, and it works for about forty minutes
and then it doesn't, and the not-working is worse for having had the
forty minutes."

---

She got up and went to the sink and ran the water and did not use it.

"How fast is it moving?" she said, with her back to him.

"It was thirty millimetres in the first nine minutes and eight in the
seven after that." He had it off the page in his head. "I stopped
taking readings at twenty-two forty-one because I got up. It's moved
about four millimetres since — I've been checking against a mark on the
second knuckle."

Tsunade turned round.

"You marked it."

"With a pencil. On the way up the hill." He said it flatly, because it
was flat. "I needed a fixed reference and my own knuckle was the only
one available, and I did it at the corner by the dye-works under a
lamp."

"...Right." She came back. "Then here's where we are and I am going to
give it to you straight because you would find anything else insulting
and because you are, God help me, the correct person to give this to
straight.

"It is decelerating and I do not know what it is decelerating towards.
It could stop at the wrist in four hours. It could stop in twenty
minutes at the base of the thumb. It could be following an asymptote,
in which case it never technically stops and you have a stone hand and
a live arm and that is your life."

"Or?"

"Or the deceleration is a curve I'm reading wrong off two data points
you took while frightened, and it goes to the elbow by morning and the
shoulder by tomorrow night, and then it is not a hand any more, it is
you." She said it without any softening at all. "I have no way to
distinguish those. Nothing in my training addresses this. There are
about four medics alive who have seen it and I am not one of them and
the other three are in Suna and Kumo and dead."

"What are the options."

"There are two and you're not going to like either." Tsunade pulled the
stool round and sat. "One. I take the hand off above the line. Tonight,
in this room, in about forty minutes, and it is the only intervention I
am certain will stop it, and I am certain because you cannot propagate
along tissue that isn't there. That is the safe answer and it is the
answer any competent surgeon in that hospital would give you inside
four minutes and Ōkubo would have the saw out already."

Sazare looked at his hand.

He thought about a graver at seven years old and a chain at thirteen
and eleven thousand people's pulses and a shoulder held still and a
woman supported under both arms while she stood for the first time in
nine days, and about a maker's panel two and a half inches long on the
inside face of a guard that he had left flat without deciding to.

"And two," he said.

"Two is that I go and ask a question I have not asked in forty years,"
said Tsunade, "of somebody who has been standing next to me the entire
time, and I do not know whether she can answer it, and I do not know
whether the answer arrives before your wrist does."

"What's the question?"

Tsunade did not answer immediately.

"Where the natural energy goes when a body can't take it," she said.
"Which is a senjutsu question. Which is the one subject in the world I
have arranged my entire life around not having a conversation about."

She stood up.

"I'm going to need about four minutes and some floor," she said. "Don't
move, don't take readings, and Ruri — when she gets here, don't be
clever. Not once. She is not a person and she has never in her life
been asked anything by anybody who wasn't me."
