# Chapter 46, Scene 6 — Unexplained Illumination

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,903 words.*

---

She came herself, at one in the morning, on the twenty-ninth, and she
came over the wall.

He found that out afterwards. What he knew at the time was that he had
his palm on the seat and the core lit and the note down a tone under
load, and then there was somebody standing at the gate end of the
awning who had not been there four seconds earlier, and he took his
hand off and the yard went dark and quiet at the same instant and he
said, out of pure reflex, "It's an instrument."

"Yes," said Tsunade. "I can see that. Put it down and come here."

---

She did not sit and she did not let him sit.

"*Unexplained illumination, quartermaster's yard, recurring,*" she
said. "Three sightings, second watch, west wall. Logged the eighteenth,
crossed my desk the twenty-sixth with four other things, and I have
spent three days assuming somebody was going to walk into my office
about it."

"I found out on the twenty-eighth."

"You found out on the twenty-eighth *from a man at a gate who thought
it was funny.*" She had her arms folded. "And then you went home."

"Yes."

"Say why. Don't build it, say it."

"Because I worked out four reasons on the walk up the hill and only one
of them was about the village."

"Which one."

"That there's a recurring unexplained light in a village where I am in
a controlled correspondence with a man who reads my post, and that
whether that matters is Ibiki's judgement and not mine, and I had that
inside two seconds standing in a gate shelter with a cup of tea in my
hand." He kept his hands at his sides. "And then I had three more
reasons, and the third one was about how it would look, and the fourth
was about a story, and I let the fourth one win because it was the most
interesting."

Tsunade looked at him for what he estimated later at five seconds.

"That," she said, "is an honest answer and it does not help you at all.
Do you know what the west wall watch does with a recurring light they
can't source?"

"No."

"Nothing, for a month. And then somebody senior notices it's been on
the sheet for a month, and it goes to the Division, and Ibiki puts one
man on it for four nights — which is one man he wanted somewhere else,
during an operation you are the centre of, and the operation is about a
man who is *specifically interested in what you make.*" She let that
land. "You have not endangered the village. You have spent four nights
of a specialist's time and made the Division look at your own yard,
which is the exact place they least need to be looking."

"Yes."

"Don't just say yes."

"I'm not saying yes to end it," said Sazare. "I'm saying it because
I've now got it and I hadn't got it an hour ago, and the part I hadn't
got is that a thing I don't report doesn't stay unreported. It gets
found out by somebody whose job is finding things out, and then the
cost isn't the thing, it's the finding out."

"Now that," said Tsunade, "is worth the walk."

---

She looked at it.

He had expected — he had constructed it four different ways in the nine
seconds after she said *put it down* — an interrogation about output,
about what it could do, about whether it was a weapon, which was the
sequence every single person who had ever asked him about crystal had
run in the same order.

She did none of that.

She picked the rod up off the felt, turned it once, and said: "Draw
it."

He drew it.

She watched the whole of it — the second and a half, the blade out of
the seat like water up a wick, the lamp going into the body of the
thing and coming out somewhere it had no business coming out of — and
then she said, "Now light it up. Properly. As far as you're prepared to
go with me standing here."

He fed it.

The core went white-blue, deepest along the axis, and the four
branches came up inside it like the veins in a leaf held to a window,
and Tsunade did not look at the blade at all.

She looked at the rod.

"That's a vascular tree," she said.

---

"It's a branching conduction network," said Sazare.

"It's a vascular tree. I've been looking at those for forty years." She
took a step closer, entirely without ceremony, and put her face about
nine inches from a lit article being held by a man who could not
guarantee it. "Trunk, four primaries, and it's asymmetric — that one's
taking more than that one, look at the light in it. And you've got a
narrowing at the third division. Do you know you've got a narrowing at
the third division?"

"Second branch," said Sazare. "I heard it start on the eleventh."

"You *heard* it."

"It goes grainy under the fundamental. About a third of the way up from
where I stop being able to hear the second voice cleanly." He was
aware that he had begun talking rather fast. "There are four pitches.
Handa can only hear three, because the top one goes in your fifties,
and I've got that written down as a calibration because it's the first
external reading I've had off anybody."

Tsunade straightened up.

"Say the four again."

"Four voices, one per primary. The two middle ones beat against each
other about twice a second because they're within about a comma. Pitch
falls as a way widens, which means the note is a direct reading of the
gauge, which is the thing I spent a month unable to measure with a
lens."

"And the light?"

"The light's a by-product. It's the crude reading. Anybody can see it."
He heard himself say the next part and did not stop it. "You can't hear
the fourth voice. I've been watching you not react to it for about a
minute and you're a hundred per cent reliable on everything else, so I
think it's above you as well, and you're not fifty."

Tsunade laughed — once, out through the nose, in a wet yard at half
past one in the morning.

"I'm not fifty," she agreed. "Take it off. I want to say something and
I don't want to say it over a light."

---

He took his hand off. The blade stopped being there and the note
stopped with no tail on it.

"Right," she said. "Two things and the first one's short. That's a
better piece of work than anything you've brought me and I include the
four media, and the reason isn't the sword. It's that you built an
instrument to measure a thing nobody had told you was a variable, and
then found out the instrument reports on itself, which is the entire
history of medicine in one bench."

"That's—"

"I'm not finished, and if you do the thing where you argue with the
compliment we'll be here till three." She put her hands in the small of
her back. "Second thing. Something's wrong with you tonight and it
isn't the light. You've had a face on since I came over that wall and
it isn't the telling-off, because you took the telling-off well and
you've been enjoying yourself for about ten minutes since."

He looked at the rod on the felt.

"It doesn't come from anywhere," he said.

---

He told her the whole of it standing up, in about nine minutes, and she
did not interrupt once.

The weighings, before and after, to three places on the sealing
office's balance. The snapped fragment: 7.11 grams that had not existed
an hour previously. The air, the ground, himself, the seed. The
0.31-gram grain that had produced a rod and a hundred and thirty grams
of fragment and was still going.

And Kanzaki, on a stool, saying *eleven hundred and forty kilos.*

When he stopped, Tsunade did not say anything for some time.

Then she sat down on the low wall, which he had never once seen her do,
and pushed her hair back with both hands and left them there.

"Right," she said, to the yard stone. "I'm going to do this out loud,
because you'll hear me doing it and I'd rather you did."

"All right."

"Hokage first. There is a substance of unknown origin in eleven hundred
kilos inside my hospital, my walls and four of my treatment protocols,
and I have no provenance for any of it, and if I am the sort of Hokage
who acts on that this week then I suspend the crystal doctrine
tomorrow morning and four people die in the second month for want of a
chip and I will have made this village measurably worse in order to be
correct."

"Kanzaki said—"

"Kanzaki said stop the line and then said she wouldn't. I know. She got
there in about four minutes and it took me about ninety seconds, which
is not because I'm faster, it's because I've done it before and she
hasn't." She took her hands out of her hair. "Second. Scientist. Do you
know what you've actually said to me tonight?"

"That I don't know where the material comes from."

"That there is a quantity of matter entering the world through a
teenager's hands at a rate of about forty kilos a month and *nobody in
four countries has ever asked about it*, because they all wrote
'bloodline' in the box." She looked up. "I have spent forty years
around kekkei genkai. I have three of the four Hokage in my family
tree. And I have never once, in my entire life, asked where Wood
Release gets the *wood*."

The yard was very quiet.

"Say that again," said Sazare.

"You heard it."

"I did," he said. "I'd like it again anyway. Slowly. I'm not going to
write it down while you're standing there."

---

She went at about two.

At the gate she stopped, which was becoming a habit and which he had
started noticing.

"You're going to have a hypothesis inside a fortnight," she said. "I
know that because I know what your face does. And it will be a big one,
because that is the only size you make."

"Probably."

"Bring it to me before you write it anywhere." She said it without any
particular weight, which was how she said the things she meant most.
"Not after. Not the tidy version. I told you in the third month —
you throw hypotheses like a man throwing seed, and about one in nine is
true and about one in nine is the one that hurts people for a hundred
years, and I cannot tell which is which from the outside and neither
can you."

"That was about a study."

"It was about you." She pulled the gate to. "And Ruri — get the light
under something. Sacking, a screen, I don't care. I'd rather not have
to explain to Ibiki why I've known for a fortnight."

Then she was gone over the wall, at fifty-something, in the dark, in
a way he found himself thinking about for some time afterwards and did
not enter anywhere.
