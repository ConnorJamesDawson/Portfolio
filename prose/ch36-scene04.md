# Chapter 36, Scene 4 — Nobody Asks What It's For

*Act 4, part C. Konoha, the south-east quarter. Sazare Ruri, age
seventeen. POV: Sazare. ~1,304 words.*

---

Handa's shed was not a shed.

It was a lean-to on the side of a house three streets off the
south-east gate, about nine feet by fourteen, with a corrugated roof
that had been salvaged from something and a whole wall of it open to a
yard, and it had a stove and a lamp and a bench that ran the entire
length of the long side at the height of a seated man.

Sazare stood in the entrance the first time with a picture frame under
his arm and did the survey without meaning to and had to stop himself
after about eleven seconds, because it was rude and because he had
already got the whole of it.

The bench was low. Every tool on the wall was hung at a height a seated
man could reach without standing. There was a rail along the front edge
of the bench that had no structural purpose whatsoever and had been put
there so that a man who could not brace with his legs could brace with
his forearms. The floor was swept.

The bench hook was on the left-hand end, in use, with about a year's
worth of saw cuts in the timber and none at all in the four inches of
crystal along the stop.

"You're staring at my hook," said Handa.

"It's held."

"It's held and it'll hold when I'm dead and somebody's grandson will
wonder what the blue bit's for." He was in a chair with arms, at the
bench, with a spokeshave in one hand. "Sit down. You've brought
something."

"You said bring something."

"That was eleven months ago and I'd given up." Handa put the
spokeshave down. "Let's see it, then."

---

Sazare put the frame on the bench.

Cheap lacquer, about ten inches by fourteen, with the lacquer gone
entirely along the bottom rail where a thumb had held it. Two of the
four mitres had been re-cut. There was a larch fillet let into the
back of the top rail where the original had split, and the fillet had
been fitted by somebody with good hands and no clamps.

Handa picked it up and turned it over twice and did not say anything
for about forty seconds.

"Who did the mitres?"

"I did."

"How old were you?"

"Fifteen. In the Land of Waves." Sazare sat down on the stool. "Two of
them were split when I got it. I re-cut those two and left the others."

"You left the others because they were sound."

"They were sound."

"Most people would have done all four so they matched." Handa held it
up to the light from the open wall and sighted along the top rail.
"That's the correct decision and about one joiner in eleven makes it.
You don't cut a good joint to match a bad one, you cut the bad one to
match the good."

He put it down.

"The fillet's wrong, though."

"I know the fillet's wrong."

"Do you know *why* it's wrong or do you know it looks wrong?"

"Grain direction. I ran it long-grain with the rail and it should have
been cross-grain, because the split it's holding is a shear and a
long-grain fillet does nothing about a shear, it just fills the hole
so it stops looking like a hole." Sazare turned the frame round. "It's
held for three years because nothing's put a load on it. If anybody
ever squeezes that frame it'll go in the same place."

"Then why's it still in there?"

"Because taking it out means opening the split and I have never been
able to make myself do it."

"Right," said Handa. "That's the job, then."

---

It took four hours.

Handa did not do any of it. He sat in the chair with the arms and
directed, and about a third of what he said was wrong and he corrected
himself, and about a third was right and Sazare had already thought of
it, and about a third was right and Sazare had not.

They opened the split with a knife and a wedge over about twenty
minutes because the lacquer had gone brittle and would chip out if you
hurried it. They took the old fillet out in three pieces. Handa made
him cut the new one from a scrap of oak off his own pile and made him
cut it four times, because the first was too tight and the second was
too loose and the third had the grain running about eleven degrees off
and Handa spotted it at four feet across a bench with one eye shut.

"Again."

"It's within—"

"It is within nothing. Again."

The fourth one went in dry and stayed by friction, which is what you
want before you commit, and Handa turned the frame over in his hands
with it in and said, "Right, that's it," and Sazare glued it and
cramped it with two of Handa's cramps and a wedge, and they put it on
the end of the bench to go off.

At some point in the middle of it Handa's wife brought tea out and
took one look at the two of them and put it down without saying
anything and went back in.

At some point after that the light started going and Handa lit the
lamp with a taper because he could not reach the shelf where the
matches were and would not have the matches moved.

---

It was on the walk home, somewhere around the fourth street, that
Sazare noticed.

He stopped under the same lamp he had stopped under in the winter, out
of what he later decided was pure habit, and did the thing he did with
any four hours he could not immediately account for, which was to go
back through them and check what they had produced.

Nothing.

He had produced nothing. There was a frame in a lean-to three streets
off the south-east gate with a piece of oak in it, and it was not his
frame any more than it had been that morning, and it had no function,
and it would never hold a picture, and nobody would ever pay for it or
audit it or weigh it or enter it in a ruled column.

And at no point in four hours had anybody asked him what he was for.

Not what he made. Not how much. Not what grade, or what quota, or
whether the fourth quarter increment was achievable, or how many
seconds it held, or whether he could stop it, or what he was worth
alive and undamaged, or whether he was a surviving member of anything.

An old man with a broken pelvis had asked him whether he knew why a
fillet was wrong, and had made him cut it four times, and had been
pleased about the mitres.

*Four hours,* thought Sazare Ruri, under a lamp, with his hands
smelling of hide glue. *I have not audited a single thing since the
second hour.*

*I did not notice I had stopped.*

*I have noticed everything else that has ever happened to me and I did
not notice that.*

---

He went back the following week, and the week after, and it became a
thing that happened on Sundays.

Handa never once asked about the hospital, the treaty, the crystal, the
scroll, the Mizukage, or the box on the shelf. He asked whether the
fillet had gone off properly. He asked, eleven weeks later, whether
Sazare had ever cut a dovetail, and made a face when told no, and spent
the following four months on it.

And he did not, on any occasion, in eleven months, ask what was going
to go in the frame.

Sazare noticed that too, eventually, and understood it correctly, and
never mentioned it either.
