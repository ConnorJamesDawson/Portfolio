# Chapter 27, Scene 5 — The Roster

*Act 4, part B. Konoha, the east side. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen.
POV: Sazare. ~2,309 words.*

---

The headband changed six forms and nothing visible.

Ashida no longer wrote *hosp / lib / home* in box eleven because daily
movement reporting ended on integration. Instead, personnel administration
issued Sazare a duty book in which the hospital wrote his hours, the
rehabilitation room wrote his restrictions and the evaluation office wrote
*pending* on alternate pages.

His monthly quota remained on Kanzaki's ledger. The six unused grains remained
sealed. The forearm plate remained evidence. He still slept in the same room
beneath the persimmon tree and woke twice each night when the repaired knee
failed to produce the pain he expected.

The first night after signing, he woke at one forty and reached for a knife
that was no longer under the pillow. Konoha had removed it on admission four
months earlier. The headband hung from the medical bag by the door and caught
enough moonlight to resemble another metal edge.

He lay awake until two twelve, listening for the monitoring shinobi on the
roof. The roof remained silent. Daily observation had ended on paper; whether
the observer had ended was information the new agreement did not provide.

At two thirteen he got up, put no weight on the left leg, and moved the bag so
the plate faced the wall.

Integration did not make the room feel occupied by less government. It made
the government easier to name.

The village altered around him.

Timber props appeared against damaged walls. Roofs acquired canvas. The
dumpling stall received a new sill, paid through the public works claim after
O-Shige corrected the clerk's estimate in person. Memorial notices went up on
the hospital board and remained until their corners curled. Shinobi with
bandages returned to duty before medical clearance and were returned to the
ward by other shinobi with orders from increasingly angry doctors.

On his first day back at work, Sazare completed observations from the
evacuation chair. Handa's pulse was seventy. Bed five had gone home. The
stonecutter allowed Sazare to change the crushed-hand dressing without looking
away.

Two replacement orderlies had arrived from clinics in the western district.
Neither knew where anything belonged. Sazare spent the first hour directing
them from the chair: folded linen second cupboard, sterile gauze locked,
kidney dishes above the sluice, broken chair marked and not to be used despite
appearing repaired. At seven twenty, one asked whether he was the shinobi
assigned to the floor.

"Hospital worker," Sazare said.

The orderly looked at the headband on the bag. "Right."

There was no answer that kept the categories separate. That was part of what
he had signed.

At eight, a chūnin from another ward asked him to demonstrate the roof
technique for three friends. Sister Kaburagi removed all four from the second
floor and added *no demonstrations* beneath Ruri's name on the temporary
roster. At nine, a civilian patient refused to let Sazare change his dressing
because he had heard the crystal grew inside people.

Sazare sent one of the new orderlies instead.

At ten, the same patient watched him spend six minutes repairing a trolley
latch and asked whether the newspaper number was true.

"Which number?"

"Twenty-seven."

"Substantially attributed."

"What does that mean?"

"They don't know exactly."

The patient allowed him to change the dressing after lunch. Neither mentioned
the crystal.

At the men's-end window, the towel on the sill was wet.

Sazare removed the sash while seated, dried the runner and replaced the gauze
with a narrow strip of waxed linen. Handa watched the entire operation.

"One rain," he said.

"It held through one rain."

"It leaked during it."

"Holding and sealing are different jobs."

"That's what bad joiners say."

"Bad joiners don't differentiate them."

The new packing compressed evenly. He shut the window.

At noon, Nurse Yagi took him to rehabilitation. Fifteen degrees had become
twenty-two. The inner thigh still cramped. He could place thirty pounds
through the left foot with both hands supported and no lateral movement.

"Again," Yagi said.

"That was ten."

"It was nine."

"You were speaking to Fujita during the fourth."

"I can count and speak."

"Not three things."

"What is the third?"

"Being wrong."

She made him do two more.

Afterwards, the evaluation office sent a genin clerk to measure him for
uniform equipment. Sazare declined the uniform because hospital dress
regulations superseded it on duty. The clerk issued sandals, two kunai pouches
and a standard weapons allowance anyway.

"I don't use kunai," Sazare said.

"Everybody gets the allowance."

"Can it purchase tools?"

"Weapons only."

"A hammer is a weapon."

"A shinobi hammer."

"How is that different?"

The clerk looked at the headband tied to the medical bag and wrote *equipment
consultation required*.

In the afternoon, two seal specialists came to the sluice with the cursed-mark
diagram. One was a woman from Intelligence whose sleeves contained more
storage seals than visible cloth. The other was an elderly man in a hospital
bed pushed by an orderly.

He was introduced as Urushibara Gen, retired sealing instructor, current
patient in the respiratory ward, and the only available person who had agreed
with Ruri that the body was part of the inscription rather than merely the
surface beneath it.

"I did not agree," Urushibara said. Oxygen tubing ran beneath his nose. "I
said the distinction was not stupid."

"That is agreement from him," the intelligence specialist said.

"It is triage."

They laid the copied diagram on the sluice table. Urushibara pointed at the
three curved marks.

"Read it."

"I can't."

"Excellent. People who think they can are exhausting. Tell me what you see."

"Three repeated forms sharing a boundary. Unequal depth. The visible curves
are not aligned with the channels beneath."

"Why?"

"Because skin moves. Or because the visible mark is an index rather than the
instruction."

Urushibara looked at the intelligence specialist. "Not stupid."

"You said."

He gave Sazare a sheet containing twelve basic notation elements. None
resembled the cursed mark. Each had a name, direction and stated function. The
page did not teach him how to make a seal. It did something more useful: it
proved the parts had names.

The first element meant containment, except when inverted, when it meant
definition of an exterior boundary. The second redirected flow clockwise
according to the writer's orientation, not the reader's. The third did nothing
alone and altered the tense of an instruction beside it, though Urushibara
objected to the word *tense* for six minutes without supplying another.

"These aren't pictures," Sazare said.

"No."

"They don't resemble what they instruct."

"Does the word *knife* cut you?"

"Written where?"

Urushibara stared at him. The intelligence specialist covered her mouth with
one sleeve.

"That," the old man said, "is unfortunately the correct question."

He turned the cursed-mark diagram so the visible curves faced away from
Sazare. "A seal is not meaning placed on a surface. It is a relationship
between instruction, medium, chakra and condition. Change the medium and
identical notation may fail, distort or become another instruction entirely."

"Like growth in granite and iron."

"Possibly. I know nothing about your crystal."

"Nobody does."

"Then you are both poorly supervised subjects."

Urushibara tapped the paper. "The visible mark is not the whole seal. Your
observation about skin movement is elementary and relevant. Beneath it, the
chakra channels act as continuing strokes. Damaging the surface may leave the
sentence open."

"Can crystal hold the continuing strokes outside a body?"

"That is not a first-day question."

"It is the question."

"Which is why you are not permitted to answer it on the first day. Memorise
the twelve elements."

Sazare looked at the page. Curiosity moved ahead of pain, caution and the
terms he had signed, as it always did.

"All right," he said.

"Memorise those," Urushibara said.

"Why?"

"So tomorrow, when you tell me the third line looks like the first but
backwards, I can explain that it is neither the third nor a line."

"Are you teaching me?"

"No. I am preventing you wasting my remaining lung capacity."

The intelligence specialist left the copied cursed mark under hospital seal.
Urushibara was pushed back to respiratory with an appointment written into
both their duty books.

It was not combat instruction. It was notation.

Sazare spent the rest of the shift learning twelve names.

At four, Ashida met him at the hospital gate out of habit. They stood—or
Ashida stood and Sazare occupied the evacuation chair—where the movement
reports had been completed for four months.

Ashida took out his pencil, remembered there was no form and put it away.

"Where are you tomorrow?" he asked.

"Hospital. Rehabilitation. Urushibara at the respiratory ward."

"Library?"

"If the knee allows."

"Home after?"

"Yes."

Ashida nodded. "Nothing again."

"You don't have to write it."

"I know."

They went separate ways at the gate.

Sazare could not use the northern stairs with the chair, so the porter took
him down the long ramp towards the market. Repairs had narrowed the road. At
the dumpling stall, O-Shige stood behind a temporary counter serving from a
borrowed pot. She handed Sazare a bowl without asking and refused payment on
the grounds that her public works claim had included two days too many lost
trading and the village could buy his lunch.

He ate at the counter with the medical bag against his chair. The headband
plate touched the wooden footrest whenever the bag moved.

Three academy children stopped on the opposite side of the road and looked at
him. One pointed. The other two argued quietly about whether he was the person
from the roof. Sazare turned the bag so the plate faced the chair.

The smallest child crossed the road before the others decided whether to stop
him.

"Are you a genin?" he asked.

"Administratively."

"What's that?"

"The village has written it down, but I haven't passed the evaluation."

"Then you're an academy student."

"No."

"Genin pass an evaluation."

"Usually."

"Can you do the Clone Technique?"

"No."

The child looked back at his friends with the satisfaction of somebody whose
position had been proven.

"Academy student," he said.

O-Shige put another dumpling into Sazare's bowl. "Eight years late, then."

"Approximately."

The child noticed the splint. "Are you going to take the exam when your leg
works?"

"A different evaluation."

"Because you're old?"

"Yes."

This answer satisfied him. He returned to the others and reported that the
person from the roof was an elderly academy student.

"That won't help," O-Shige said.

"No."

"They think you saved the hospital."

"Many people defended it."

"They aren't eating here."

"That doesn't improve the claim."

She put another dumpling in his bowl.

"You staying?" she asked.

The question could have meant the stool, the hospital, the village or the
arrangement whose metal plate rested against his bag.

"Yes," Sazare said.

She accepted the answer without asking which.

That evening, he opened his notebook at the low table beneath the persimmon
tree. The date occupied the top line. Beneath it he wrote Kanzaki's
instruction exactly:

*I don't know exactly what happened.*

Then he listed what he knew.

Nineteen grains. Thirteen used. The first barrier failed at six seconds.
Fracture retained order. Contact growth from fragment immediate. Resonance
destroyed continuous faces and some seeds. Recall established a return route.
Reversing the route carried a simple instruction outward. One hundred and
eight pathways observed by Hyūga Natsumi. Twenty-two confirmed dead in the
final field. Friendly exclusions possible at disproportionate cost. Full
recall after activation incomplete.

He stopped before writing Guren.

The copied intelligence sentence was not in front of him. He remembered it
anyway: distribution list, eight days before the invasion.

Alive was not a location. It was not an offer, an obligation or a route. It
was one fact with nothing yet attached.

Sazare wrote:

*Guren alive as of eight days before attack. Oto trained against her crystal.
Their method works on individual ordered centres. It does not work quickly
enough against distributed seed.*

Then:

*Find out why hers breaks differently.*

The sentence was a want and a task. He could no longer tell where one ended.

He closed the notebook, packed the twelve seal-notation elements into the
medical bag and set the alarm for the sixth hour.

On the hospital roster the next morning, Sister Kaburagi had crossed out the
old line and written a new one beneath it:

*RURI, S. — 2nd flr, 06.00–16.00. REHAB 12.00. SEALS 15.00.*

Below that, in smaller writing:

*No roofs.*

At six fifteen, Handa asked whether the new line meant Ruri was allowed to
give orders.

"No."

"Then what does genin mean?"

"At present, that I am paid differently and more departments can tell me where
to be."

"Promotion."

"Administratively."

Handa considered the word. "Window's leaking again."

It was not. The waxed linen held the runner dry. Sazare checked anyway, moving
the chair close enough to put two fingers against the sill.

Outside, the hospital approach remained without a gate. Public works had set
the replacement posts but not hung the iron. People passed between them
without slowing: staff beginning shifts, relatives carrying food, shinobi with
new bandages, two labourers taking tools downhill. The chalk evidence circles
had washed into pale arcs after the funeral rain.

The route north over the roofs remained visible. It would remain visible from
this window for as long as the surrounding buildings stood.

Sazare put his left foot flat beneath the chair. Twenty-two degrees at noon,
thirty pounds permitted through the joint, six weeks before combat load. He
pressed until the repaired knee accepted the whole thirty and no more.

The headband touched the medical bag beneath his seat.

At the desk, Sister Kaburagi rang the morning bell once.

Sazare released the sill, turned the chair and began with bed one.

The observation sheet required pulse, temperature, respiration and change
since the previous entry. It contained no box for heroism, ownership or
wanting. His handwriting fit the spaces provided.

The hospital day went on.
