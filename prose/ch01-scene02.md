# Chapter 1, Scene 2 — The Delivery

*Act 1, beat 3. Sazare Ruri, age seven. POV: Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

They went out at the ninth hour with the month's work in two boxes, and Sazare
was allowed to carry the small one because it had the buttons in it and buttons
did not matter.

His father carried the other. Ren Ruri was not, strictly, a Ruri at all — he had
married in from a family of net-makers two districts over, and old Iwao had
never entirely forgiven him for it, or for being cheerful, or for being taller
than everybody. He could not grow so much as a grain. What he could do was talk
to people about money without either party wanting to hit him afterwards, which
his wife said was a rarer bloodline than anything the clan had.

"Straight there," he said. "No stopping at the water steps."

"I never stop at the water steps."

"You stopped for a quarter of an hour last time."

"There was an *eel*."

"There is always an eel," said his father. "That's what the water steps are for."

---

The fog had gone off the streets by then but it hadn't gone far; it sat out over
the water in a flat white band, so that the boats going out to the deep grounds
went into it and stopped existing, mast last. Sazare liked watching that. He was
not allowed to stop and watch it.

Kirigakure in the morning smelled of fish and wet stone and, faintly and
everywhere, of the tar they used on the boat hulls. The lower district was built
close, house against house, with the canals running black between them and the
walkways going over on stone arches that were older than anyone could account
for. Water came up the walls to about knee height and stayed there. Everything
below the line was green.

They went up through the fish market, where the gulls that argued on the
Nakashimas' roof had relocated to argue professionally, and out along the
cutting, and up the hill, and the streets got drier and the houses got further
apart, and the guild hall sat at the top of it behind a wall with broken glass
set into the top.

Sazare had asked about the glass once. His father had said it was because
inscribers were paranoid. His mother had said it was because inscribers were
correct.

---

Genzō met them in the front room, which had a long table in it and nothing else,
and no windows on the street side.

He was a narrow, stooped, pleasant man of about fifty with ink to the second
knuckle on both hands and a pair of spectacles pushed up into his hair that he
would spend the next twenty minutes looking for. He and Ren had a routine. It
went: Genzō said the crystal was late, and Ren said the crystal was early, and
Genzō said he supposed it would have to do, and then they both looked at it and
went quiet, because whatever else they said to each other neither of them was
casual about the work.

"Number four's gone cloudy," said Ren.

"So it has." Genzō turned the shelf over under the lamp. "Buttons?"

"Buttons."

"Shame. It's a good thickness." He set it aside and lifted out the next piece,
which was a clean blue lozenge about the size of a thumb joint, and he made a
small approving noise at it that Sazare felt personally, on his mother's behalf.
"Now that. That's four hours."

"Five."

"She's getting quicker."

"She's getting older," said Ren. "She'd say it's the same thing."

Genzō laid the good pieces out along the table in a row, in an order Sazare could
not work out and which he was certain was not the order they had come out of the
box. Then he found his brushes, and lost his spectacles, and found his spectacles,
and sat down.

Sazare stood on tiptoe at the end of the table.

"Elbows off," said his father.

"They're not on."

"They're going on."

The nib went down on the blue lozenge and moved, and left behind it a line of
figures no thicker than a hair. Sazare watched a shape build up out of nothing —
four strokes, a hook, a long curve that went round three quarters of the stone
and stopped, a cluster of small marks inside the curve like a handful of thrown
sand.

He had seen it a hundred times. He had never once been able to see it *happen*,
because it happened at the speed of somebody's hand.

"What does it say?"

"Hm?" Genzō did not look up.

"That one. What does it say?"

"It's a holding mark."

"But what does it *say*?"

Genzō finished the curve, lifted the nib clean, and sat back to let it dry.
"It says hold," he said.

"Hold what?"

"Whatever they put in it."

"But how does it know what—"

"Sazare," said his father.

"I'm only asking."

"He's only asking," Genzō agreed, mildly. He had picked up the next piece
already. "It's a fair question. It's not your side of the job, though, is it."

"Why isn't it?"

"Because you grow it and I write on it," said Genzō, "and that's how it's been
since before either of us, and it works, and men who are good at two things are
usually not very good at either." He set the nib down and looked over the top of
the spectacles he had spent so long finding. "Your mother could write these. She'd
be dreadful at it."

"She wouldn't."

"She would be *dreadful*," said Genzō, with enormous relish, and went back to
work.

Sazare stayed at the end of the table with his elbows not quite on it, and
watched the marks go down, and did not understand a single one of them.

---

The rest of it took until noon, because the guild worked at the speed the guild
worked at and no faster, and because Ren and Genzō had to argue about the copper
wire — the same argument as at breakfast, conducted with an entirely different
man and reaching an entirely identical conclusion.

Then they packed the finished pieces, and paid for the inscription, and left with
the boxes lighter and the money not yet in them, and went back down the hill and
along the cutting and up the other side to the quartermaster's yard.

This was the part Sazare liked.

The yard was a long open shed behind the barracks with racks down both sides, and
it was always busy, and it smelled of oil and leather and the particular hot
smell of a whetstone being used badly by somebody in a hurry. Shinobi came
through it all day. They came in mud to the thigh and went out clean; they came
in with things broken and left them on the counter without explaining; they
argued about kit allowances with a clerk who had heard every argument there was
and had stopped being interested in about the year Sazare was born.

Ren put the boxes on the counter and the clerk went through them against a list,
slowly, touching each piece.

Sazare turned round and put his back against the counter and watched the yard.

A woman was having a shin guard restrapped and was eating an apple while it
happened. Two men were sorting a crate of wire spools and complaining about the
price, which apparently everybody in the world was doing that week. Somebody
somewhere behind the racks was sharpening something and doing it wrong; Sazare
knew it was wrong because his uncle would have said so, at length.

And then the ANBU came through.

He came in from the barracks side and he was not in a hurry and nobody got out of
his way, because it was a quartermaster's yard and the whole point of a
quartermaster's yard is that nobody is anybody in it. He was not tall. He had a
mask pushed up onto the top of his head so that the animal face pointed at the
ceiling, which Sazare found obscurely disappointing, and his own face underneath
it was ordinary and tired and about the age of Sazare's father.

He put a gauntlet on the counter, next to the boxes.

"Split," he said.

The clerk picked it up and looked. "So it has."

"Second time."

"You'll be doing something to it."

"I'll be *wearing* it," said the ANBU, with the flatness of a man who had had
this conversation before too, and the clerk made a note without any sign of
having heard, and put the gauntlet on the shelf behind him.

Sazare had got a good look at it on the way past.

It was plain dark leather, scuffed pale at the knuckles, with a plate over the
back of the hand — and set into the plate, flush, no bigger than a fingernail,
there was a small blue stone with a hair-thin line of figures round three
quarters of it and a cluster of marks inside the curve.

He knew that piece. He did not know that piece specifically; he knew the *kind*
of piece, and the hand it had been written by, and the four hours it had taken to
grow, and the fact that it had come out of his mother's north room on a wooden
tray full of grey sand.

Something enormous and simple went through him.

He turned round and pulled at his father's sleeve. "That's *ours*."

"Mm."

"That's *ours*, that's — he's got one of ours on his *hand*—"

"Keep your voice down."

"But—"

"Sazare." His father did not look round from the counter, but he put one hand on
top of Sazare's head, briefly, which was what he did instead of most things.
"Yes. It's ours."

"Does he know?"

"I doubt he's ever thought about it."

Sazare thought this was possibly the worst thing he had ever heard.

He looked back. The ANBU had gone over to the rack to argue about a replacement,
and the mask on top of his head bobbed while he talked, and he had the sleeves of
a man who had not slept, and he was complaining about the leather.

*I could tell him*, Sazare thought. *I could go over there and tell him.*

He did not go over there and tell him.

---

They walked home with the boxes empty and the money in his father's shirt, down
through the market where the gulls had won something and were being loud about
it, and out along the cutting where the fog had finally lifted off the water and
you could see all the way to the grey line of the far islands.

"Dad."

"Mm."

"Where do they all go?"

"Where does what all go?"

"All of them. Everything we make." Sazare shifted the empty box under his arm.
"There's nine frames. There's nine frames every night. That's — that's a lot,
over a year, that's hundreds of—"

"Thousands."

"So where do they *go*?"

His father walked a few paces.

"They go where they're wanted," he said. "Jewellers, mostly. Half of what comes
off your mother's frames ends up round somebody's neck at a wedding, and that's
the half that pays for the wire." He shifted his own box. "And the rest goes
where you saw it going."

"On people."

"On people who need it not to break," said Ren. "Which is a decent thing to be,
if you think about it. There's a lot of ways to be useful to this village and
most of them are worse."

Sazare thought about it.

"I'd want to know," he said.

"Know what?"

"If I had one. On my hand. I'd want to know who grew it."

His father laughed — properly, out loud, the way he did about twice a week and
always at nothing.

"Then you'd be the only one," he said.

They went up the steps at the water gate, and there was an eel, and Sazare stopped
for a quarter of an hour, and his father let him.
