# Chapter 17, Scene 1 — Normal

*Act 2, part D. The border country. Sazare Ruri, fifteen turning sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~2,200 words.*

---

Between the salt gangway at Kanezaki and the thing at Kasado there were
nine weeks, and in the nine weeks there were three more, and this is the
whole of what he ever put in the ledger about them:

*Second. A bargemaster at Ihoya. Fourth week. Two nights' ground. Rope
and a hatch coaming. Called an accident and was not disputed. No
material.*

*Third. A clerk of the salt monopoly, at the crossing above Nemura.
Seventh week. Nine days. Knife, bought at Nemura market, dropped at
Nemura ferry. Called a robbery. No material.*

*Fourth. A man at Ihoya again, which was careless and he knew it going
in and took it because the money was standing there. Eighth week. Four
days. Nothing of mine anywhere near him and I have been over it
sixty-one times.*

That was the ledger. Four lines, in a hand nobody could read, in the only
book he had ever kept that had no numbers in it, and the reason the
entries look like that is that the entries are all he permitted himself
to have.

He did not lay the parts out. He had said, on a mooring bollard in a
river port, that he was going to have to live the rest of his life with a
tray of unsorted parts in him, and he had turned out to be exactly right
about that, and the discipline had not got harder.

It had got easier.

---

He was better now, and the getting-better had a shape, and the shape was
that all of it was tradecraft and none of it was the craft.

The survey came down from nine days to four. He stopped putting cards in
windows and started taking work off other people's benches — a
sub-contract, a fortnight's overflow from a busy smith, which put him in
a town with a legitimate face and no queue and nobody's opinion of him
travelling. He learned to buy a knife in one town and use it in the next
one over. He learned that a man who is going to have to leave a place at
speed should establish a boring reason to be seen leaving it — a fetching
job, a delivery, a barge he is known to be waiting for — three days
*before* he needs it. He learned to sleep with his boots where he could
reach them without sitting up.

He did not put one grain of crystal into a job after Kanezaki. Not one.
The three fixtures on the salt side had been the last of it and he had
spent sixty-one repetitions of the fourth job satisfying himself that
nothing of his had gone anywhere near a body, and the satisfying had
taken about a week each time, and the week each time was the price of
sleeping.

And meanwhile, on a bench, alone, at night, in four different rented
rooms, he did the work that mattered and got precisely nowhere.

Four seconds.

He had it at four seconds in the ninth week exactly as he had had it at
four seconds in the first, and he had tried it eleven different ways —
slower feed, faster feed, wet stock, dry stock, a piece grown in silence
and a piece grown while he counted aloud to keep his breath even — and
every single one of them came out at four seconds and cracked at its
note, and he had begun, some nights, to sit and look at his own two hands
with something that was not far off hatred.

*It's something time usually gets you,* he had said, in a noodle shop,
with a bead of crystallised tea on the boards. *Something that happens
while you're waiting, that you don't have to do on purpose.*

He had turned that over about four hundred times since and had got no
further with it than he had got in the four seconds it took him to say
it. He could feel the shape of the missing thing the way you feel a
missing tooth. He could not get at it. He was a boy with a bench and a
hammer and thirty-four worthless offcuts and no instruction whatever, and
somewhere in the world there was a person who would have told him in one
sentence, and a woman with a bad temper had refused to say where, and had
been right to.

---

He found out he was sixteen three days after the fact, off a consignment
note.

It was a dated docket at a rope-walk counter, the eleventh of the month
in a clerk's hand, and he read the date the way you read a date, and then
the arithmetic did what arithmetic does when nobody has asked it to.

Born in the eleventh month. The eighth of it.

He stood at a counter in a border town with a docket in his hand and was
sixteen, and had been sixteen since Tuesday, and there was nobody within
two hundred miles who knew it, and nobody within a great deal further
than that who would have cared.

He worked out afterwards that this had happened before — that fifteen had
gone past him in a valley in the Land of Hot Water without a word, and
fourteen in a rented room, and thirteen on a boat — and that the last
time anybody had marked the day out loud, there had been four of them at
a table, and his mother had made the thing with the sweet bean in it that
she was not good at, and Kohaku had given him a chisel she had almost
certainly stolen.

He put the docket down. He collected the parcel he had come for.

That night he ate a good dinner, on purpose, and did not tell the woman
who served it why, and went back and lay on his bed and did not grow
anything, and thought: *right.*

---

The broker at Kasado was called Chōsei and he was nothing like Umeya.

Umeya had been a licensed pawnbroker with a cat and a second ledger and
thirty-one years of clientele and a conscience he had put on a shelf
where he could see it. Chōsei kept a lamp-oil warehouse and had two
sons in the trade and did the other business out of the counting-room
with the door open, which told Sazare more about the state of the border
country that autumn than anything else he saw all season.

He was fat and cheerful and he called Sazare *young man* and he had a
list four deep.

"Now this one," said Chōsei, tapping the fourth entry with a finger like
a sausage, "this one you'll want, and I'll tell you why you'll want it,
and then I'll tell you the catch, because I'd rather you heard it from me
than found it on the ground."

"Say the number first."

"Four hundred thousand."

The room went quite quiet, in the way rooms do when a number lands in
them.

*Four hundred thousand* was two years with a real teacher. It was the
whole specification he had costed at a bench in Tsuzura with a scale-beam
in pieces — two hundred thousand to buy the year, and two hundred thousand to
live on
while he had it, and no need to work through it, and no need to take
another job for eighteen months, and the arithmetic that had been a
closed trap since the day he lost everything in a wall at Tomari simply
came open, all at once, on a fat man's finger.

"And the catch."

"Two of them. It's soon — inside the fortnight, and I'll not take a man
who wants a month. And it's *particular*." Chōsei turned the ledger
round. "The buyers want it to look like anything at all except what it
is. Anything. Robbery, water, a fall, a fever if you can manage a fever,
which nobody can. What they will not have is a body that looks like
somebody was *paid*, because—" and here he shrugged with his whole
upper half, comfortably, a man discussing carriage rates — "because the
moment it looks paid for, everybody up that strait knows there's a party
who pays, and then everybody up that strait starts thinking about who
that party might be, and then it's all no good."

"That's not a catch," said Sazare. "That's the job."

"That's what I said to them." Chōsei beamed at him. "That's exactly what
I said. But it does mean I want somebody careful and it does mean I'm
paying careful money, and every other name on my board wants to do it
with a crossbow off a roof and be in the next country by Thursday."

---

And then he did it, because they always did it, and this time it did not
even cost Sazare an effort to stop him.

"Now the *why* of it," said Chōsei, settling back, "because you'll want
to know that you're not—"

"Don't."

The fat man stopped. He looked at Sazare for a moment with his head very
slightly on one side, the way a man looks at a barrel that has made an
unexpected noise.

"Don't tell me who," said Sazare, "and don't tell me why, and don't tell
me what they're supposed to have done, and if the buyers have sent a
story down with the money then keep the story. I'll want the where, the
when, the description, and the look-of-the-thing they're paying for.
Nothing else."

"You're the second one of you I've met," said Chōsei, after a moment.

"And?"

"And the first one's dead, but not of that." He made a note. "Suit
yourself. It's cheaper for me; I've a very good story down there and I
was going to have to say it with a straight face."

And Sazare sat in a counting-room in a lamp-oil warehouse and noticed —
carefully, honestly, the way he noticed everything, filing it in the tray
without laying it beside anything else — that the refusal had cost him
nothing at all.

At Tsuzura it had cost him something. He remembered the shape of it: an
old man with his spectacles off, saying *you'll want it later, they all
want it later, at three in the morning about the sixth week,* and a boy
with a knee that would not bend saying *because if you tell me I'll take
it apart, that's what I do, it's the only thing I've ever been able to
do.* It had been a true sentence and a hard one and it had come out of
him at some price.

Nine weeks later, in a warmer room, for four hundred thousand, the same word
had come out flat and early and easy, the way *sixty* came out when
somebody asked about a lantern.

He took the papers. He counted the advance, which was two hundred thousand, which
he had never held in one hand before.

At the door Chōsei said, comfortably, "Kasado, then. Pretty place. Mind
the strait — the ferry's a swindle and the crossing's worse."

"I'll walk round."

"There's no round, young man," said Chōsei, and laughed, and went back
to his lamp oil.

---

He was three days on the road north and he spent them doing what he
always did, which was working out what the shape of the thing told him.

*Inside the fortnight* meant the buyers had a date of their own. A
season's contract, a sailing, a court term, a shipment; something that
had to happen after, and could not happen before.

*Anything but what it is* meant they were not frightened of the law.
There is no law worth the name in the border country and everybody knows
it. They were frightened of *reputation* — of a specific set of people
finding out that a specific kind of money had moved. Which meant the
buyers had to go on doing business, afterwards, in that place, with those
people, looking them in the face.

Which meant the buyers were *local*. Which meant they were commercial.
Which meant this was a trade dispute.

He noticed, somewhere on the second day, that he was assembling.

He stopped, on the road, with the pack on his shoulder, in the middle of
a country lane with a hedge on either side, and stood there for about a
minute.

Then he took the whole of it — the fortnight, the look-of-the-thing, the
buyers who had to stay and be looked at — and put the pieces back in the
tray, loose, unsorted, turned different ways up, the way you keep parts
you have not identified; and walked on, and did not touch them again;

and was, for the eight days that followed, entirely successful in that,

right up until the moment he saw a chip of worthless cloudy stone wedged
flush in the second peg of a rope-board on a boat-shed wall at Kasado,
in a hand he did not know, in a grammar he had learned at eight years
old, sitting on a step in the dark, from his sister.
