# Chapter 34, Scene 4 — Non-Monetary

*Act 4, part C. The Hokage's office. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen. POV:
Sazare. ~1,323 words.*

---

Item five was *any other*, and *any other* was the box.

It came up from beside the Kiri delegate's chair, where it had been the
entire meeting, and Sazare had catalogued it at the eleventh minute
along with everything else in the room — a plain paulownia box about
twenty inches by five, tied with a flat woven tape, the kind of box
that in his mother's workshop had meant *this is going somewhere it
will be opened by somebody who matters*.

He had assumed it was a gift for the Hokage. Delegations brought
things.

"Fourth tranche, non-monetary schedule," said the delegate. "This one
isn't on the aggregate list, because there's one item and it has a
name against it."

He put the box on the table.

"Registry has spent two years matching confiscated and looted movable
property against the pre-war district registers. Most of it is
unmatchable and will never be matched. This one was matchable because
it was catalogued when it was sold — it went through three collections
in six years and each of them wrote down what it was, which is the only
reason we have it."

Amagai said, "Provenance?"

"Compound clearance, ward eleven. Documented. It was on a shelf in a
private collection in the Land of Hot Water and the holder surrendered
it without argument when we established what it was." The delegate did
not look at Sazare. He was, Sazare noticed, being extremely careful not
to. "There's no claim requirement. It isn't compensation. It's being
returned because it should not have left the house."

---

"Would you open it, please," said Mei Terumī.

"Here?"

"Here, and I'd rather you did it than I did." She glanced at the
delegate. "Say the rest of it."

The delegate said the rest of it in the voice of a man reading from a
brief he had been given and had not written, which is what he was.

"The Kirigakure administration wishes to record that the return of
clan technical material to a surviving bloodline holder is undertaken
as a gesture of good will. It is our understanding that the material
relates to the applied fabrication techniques of the Ruri line." A
small pause. "It is our hope that its return will be of practical
assistance to the recipient in the exercise of his craft."

Nobody in the room reacted to that at all, and Sazare understood, in
the four seconds it took him to reach for the tape, exactly what he had
just been told.

*You have signed a four-year fabrication agreement with us eleven
minutes ago and this is going to make the product better.*

They were not being cynical about it. That was the thing. The man had
said it as an ordinary courtesy, and the woman who had ordered it had
almost certainly meant it as a kindness, and it was also — plainly,
unavoidably, in the same sentence — an investment in an asset the room
had spent six minutes pricing.

Both true. Neither cancelling.

He untied the tape.

---

It was a scroll and it was smaller than he expected.

That was the first thing and it went through him sideways, because he
had been carrying an idea of this object since he was nine years old
and the idea was enormous. What was in the box was about eighteen
inches of it on a plain wooden roller with the lacquer gone at both
ends from handling, and the paper had darkened to the colour of weak
tea, and there was a water-stain along one edge that had been there
long enough to have its own fade.

The tie was not the original tie. Somebody in a collection had replaced
it with something decorative.

He did not unroll it. He put one hand flat on it in the box, and the
paper was cool, and the roller had a small chip out of the left end
that he recognised.

He recognised it.

He had no memory attached to it, no scene, no moment. He simply knew
the shape of that chip in the way a person knows the sound of a door in
a house they lived in as a child, and it went into him under everything
he had built in six years, and he stood in a round room at the top of
a tower in front of thirteen people and could not, for approximately
four seconds, do anything at all.

"Ruri," said Tsunade, from the head of the table, in a voice pitched
for exactly one person.

"Yes."

"Is it yours?"

"Yes."

"Do you want to say anything to the delegation?"

He looked up.

Six Kiri. A woman of about thirty in a green coat that fitted, with her
hair cut to hold a line. A man with an eyepatch and both hands flat on
the table. A sixty-year-old civil servant reading somebody else's
brief, who had spent two years of his professional life matching
looted objects against dead people's inventories and had found
one.

"Who did the matching?" said Sazare.

The delegate blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"Registry. Two years of cross-referencing collection catalogues against
pre-war district registers. That's not a person, that's a section, but
somebody found this one. Somebody sat down with a sale catalogue from
the Land of Hot Water and a ward eleven inventory and put them next to
each other and got a match." He put the lid back on the box. "I'd like
them told that it arrived."

There was a pause.

"That's — " The delegate stopped, and looked at the Mizukage, and then
back. "That's not usually what people ask."

"I know what people usually ask. I've read the schedule." Sazare picked
the box up. "They'll have matched about four hundred things and been
thanked for none of them, because the people who get them back are
looking at the thing and not at the two years. Tell them it arrived and
tell them the roller's chipped in the same place it was chipped when I
was nine, so they got the right one."

The delegate wrote it down.

Sazare found out, much later, that he had written it down verbatim and
that it had gone back to a registry office in Kirigakure and been read
out, and that it was the only communication of its kind that section
received in four years of operation, and that they had pinned it to a
wall.

---

Kōji closed item five at four minutes past the third hour.

The delegation left at half past, with the fisheries dispute settled
and a fabrication agreement signed and a retrieval order withdrawn and
a charge vacated that had never lawfully existed, and the whole of it
went into three clerks' folders and would be in an archive by Friday.

Sazare stood by the wall with a paulownia box under his arm.

Mei Terumī stopped in front of him on the way out, which was not on
anybody's schedule, and looked at the box rather than at him.

"I don't know what's in it," she said. "I was told it was technical
material and I authorised the return and I have not read it and I'm
not going to."

"I know."

"I hope it's worth something to you." She said it plainly, without any
weight, and then, after a moment, added the only unofficial sentence
she spoke in that building: "And if it isn't, that's not a failure on
your part. Most of what comes back isn't."

She went.

He stood there for a while.

Then he went back to the hospital, because he was on at six the
following morning and there was nothing else in the world he knew how
to do with an afternoon, and he put the box on the shelf in his room
next to an empty picture frame and a forearm plate wrapped in
untreated cotton, and did not open it for nine days.
