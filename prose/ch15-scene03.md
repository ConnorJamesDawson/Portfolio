# Chapter 15, Scene 3 — Eleven Seconds

*Act 2, part D. Tomari. Sazare Ruri, age fifteen. POV: Sazare. ~3,900
words.*

---

He came in off the north path on the fourth morning with the fifth run
closed behind him and his whole life due to be handed over a counter
before noon, and he went to the jetty first, because he always went to
the jetty first.

Shū was on the bollard with his knees up, doing nothing, in the way of a
nine-year-old who has been doing nothing since before dawn in order to
be found doing it.

"You're b-back."

"I'm back."

"There was a m-m—" Shū stopped, and started again, and Tomari, in the
person of a ferryman's son and a boy with a pack, waited, because that
was the arrangement the whole town had come to about Shū years before
Sazare ever got there. "There was a man."

"What man?"

"At the sh-shed. He asked me where the r-r—" It went. He tried it
sideways. "Where the *road* went. And I told him, and it t-took a bit,
and he w-waited." Shū examined this, and found it worth reporting, and
reported it with the particular flat pride of a child telling you
somebody had been nice to him. "He waited the whole way. He didn't do
the f-face."

Sazare stood on the jetty with the tide going out under the boards.

There were four inns in Tomari and two hundred people and every last one
of them had, at some point in nine years, done the face at Shū: the tiny
flinch of the jaw, the eyes going for the door, the helpful guess at the
end of the word that saves the adult four seconds and costs the child
the sentence. Sazare had done it himself in the first week and had
caught himself doing it and had stopped, and stopping had taken
conscious work for a month, and he was the only person in the town who
had ever bothered.

A stranger off the coast road, in a hurry, asking directions, had waited
out a nine-year-old's stammer to the end without moving his face.

Nobody does that by accident. That is a trained man practising the
central skill of his trade on the first available subject, the way a
swordsman turns a stick over in his hand while he talks.

"Was he nice?" said Sazare.

"He was v-very nice," said Shū.

"Good," said Sazare. "That's good. Hold this a minute, would you," and
gave him the pack strap to hold, which took the boy's hands and eyes
somewhere else for four seconds, which was as long as Sazare needed to
be sure his own face had come back under him.

---

He did not go to the chandler's.

He went up the ferry road at an ordinary pace and round the top of the
town the way he went most mornings, because a man who has walked a
circuit forty times can walk it once more without a single person
noticing, and he read Tomari the way he had read it in his first three
days, and it took him about seven minutes to find the second thing.

The lame horse.

There were men at the far inn whose horse had gone lame two days back.
He knew that because the whole town knew it; a lame horse in a
two-hundred-person ferry-stage is an event, discussed at the water queue,
and it had been discussed at the water queue.

Nobody had come to get him.

That was all. That was the entire tell, and it was enormous, and it
would have meant nothing whatever to a man who had not spent six weeks
being the person Tomari sent for. They sent for him about kettles. They
sent for him about a cracked stock-pot and a stuck window and a
sail-hank and a child's broken clog. There was no horse-doctor within
eleven miles and Tomari's answer to anything on four legs was to fetch
the mender and see if he had an opinion, and they had done it twice, and
he had been useless both times, and they had fetched him anyway,
because that is what a town does with the person it has decided is the
one who looks at broken things.

Three men had had a lame horse in that town for two days and had let it
stay lame rather than have a conversation with a stranger.

He stood at the top of the ferry road with his heart going and did not
alter his pace or his face or the swing of his arms, and walked on, and
went down the far side, and thought about the money.

It was in a wash-leather bag in a hole behind a brick in the chandler's
back room, five runs of it, two years of it, everything a boy could
carry out of a country and everything he had earned since — and he was
thirty yards from it, and the woman would count it out in about four
minutes, and the shop had a front door on the ferry road and a back
door on the alley, and both of those doors were rooms he could not see
into.

*You won't be drawn,* said Sakai, on a fence, in the steam. *You won't
be flattered. You won't fight the fight that's offered when you can
fight the one that isn't.*

He thought about what he would be without it: a boy on a foreign coast
in the ninth month with a pack and no money, walking inland into winter,
taking the first work anybody offered him. He knew exactly what the
first work offered to a boy like that was. He had known since Yumoto.
That was the whole reason for the halves and the pegs and the tenth and
the two miserly years — the money was not money, the money was the
distance between him and the only trade that ever came looking for
people with nothing.

He wanted it so badly that his hands had gone cold.

He did not go to the chandler's.

---

But his pack was in the loft, and the loft was over the chandler's, and
there were two things up there he could not walk inland in the ninth
month without, and one of them was a medical roll and the other was a
stick of seasoned oak, cut to the wood's own say-so, worn to the shape
of his hands, wrapped in sacking so that a checkpoint read *labourer's
tool*.

The outside stair went up the alley side. He took it slowly, like a
tired courier home from a run, and got the plank off the gable window
and went in over the sill instead of through the door, which he had done
every night for six weeks and which was the first thing in the whole
morning he did entirely by habit and not by decision.

The loft was as he had left it.

He rolled the blanket, took the pack off the peg, put the good chisel and
the little scale and the medical roll in it and left the rest, and
unhooked the sacking bundle from the rafter where it lived looking like a
bundle of anything, and stood for one second in a room he had been happy
in.

Then he went down the outside stair into the alley, and the alley had a
man in it.

---

*One.*

He counted, because Sakai had made him count, in a yard, for a year,
until counting arrived before fear did. It was the only reason his mind
was in the alley at all.

The man was at the far end, twenty feet, coming, and he had a white face
and no weapon out and he was not hurrying, and Sazare's entire body
understood in the first quarter of a second what four years of every
adult's warning had been about, because it was not like being frightened
of a person. It was like standing in front of weather.

*Two.*

He went back up two stairs and slammed the alley gate at the bottom of
them — not the gate; the gate opened outward and could not be wedged; it
was the *store door* at the stair-head, and it opened inward, and there
was a spur in the rebate a quarter-inch proud that he had grown one night
in his second week for no reason he could have defended to anybody, and
he kicked the wedge into the shoulder of it as he went through, and the
door took the wedge and held, and a hand hit it from the other side hard
enough to bring plaster off the frame.

He had four seconds.

*Three. Four.*

Out the store's far window onto the alley's other wall, and the wall was
sheer render, six feet of it, and he did not think about it at all: his
right foot went out to the side and found a rung the size of two fingers,
blue-grey, mortared into render where no rung had ever been, and pushed
off it, and his hands took the gutter.

He was on the roof before he had any idea how.

*Five.*

Slate, wet from the night, pitched steeper than it looked. His left foot
came down on a ridge no thicker than a coin, under the eave, and did not
slide.

*Six.*

Across the ridge-line and there was another one below in the ferry road,
white-faced, standing perfectly still in the middle of the road, not
looking up, and Sazare understood the shape of the thing all at once and
completely: there were three of them, they had the alley and the shop and
the road, they had had the whole town for two days, and the money had
never been the point — the money had been the *anchor*, and they had not
come to take his money, they had come to take him at the ten minutes in
six weeks when he would be standing still with his hands full.

He had not gone to the chandler's. That was the only card he had played
correctly all morning and he had played it seven minutes ago.

*Seven.*

Down the back pitch, over the gable, and he did the thing he had been
turning over on eleven roofs in two years and had never once had to do:
he went *toward* the shed instead of away from the town. The customs
shed's blind side, the one wall in Tomari with no window facing it and no
sightline from the road, because every position with a view is a position
somebody is standing in.

He hit the shed roof, and the shed roof, being a customs shed, was
rotten, and he went through it to the waist.

*Eight.*

Something came up the alley behind him at a speed that was not a man
running. He got his legs out and dropped off the shed's blind side onto
the flats path, and he was four strides from the culvert mouth under the
ferry road when the third one came round the corner of the shed and took
his knee.

He never saw it. He was aware of the ground arriving.

It was the outside of the left knee, and it was done with a boot heel and
not with anything sharp, and it was, he thought later with the horrible
detachment that came over him afterwards, *aimed to be survivable* —
placed where a joint gives and does not shatter, by a man who had done it
enough times to know the difference to the width of an inch.

*Nine.*

He came down on the shingle on his side with the bundle under him and the
pack strap across his throat, and the man was on him before he
had finished arriving, and Sazare did the only thing there was: he got
his right arm up across his own face.

The bare one. The scarred one. Wrist to elbow, three exit-marks, the arm
that had been in a cloth for two years.

The blow stopped.

It was already coming — he felt the air of it — and it stopped, and
turned in the air, and went past his ear into the shingle, and the man
above him made a short sound of pure professional irritation and took a
fistful of the pack strap instead.

*Damage to hands or forearms voids premium.*

He had read it four times in an inn entry in the rain, and been sick
behind a woodpile, and had never once thought of it as anything but the
number on a tag round his neck. It arrived now as a *rule of the fight*.
There were things this man was not permitted to do. There was a whole
country of him — from the fingertips to the elbows, both sides — that
was off the board, not by mercy but by clerkship, because an office two
years east had written a sentence and the sentence had come down the
coast ahead of the men and was lying here on the shingle at Tomari
holding a professional's hands.

So Sazare put his arms where the blows were.

He did it three times in perhaps two seconds, and it was not skill, it
was arithmetic, it was the crudest thing he had ever done — and each time
the strike aborted or diverted, and each abort cost the man above him a
quarter-second of rebuilding, and Sakai's voice said, quite calmly, from
a fence in Yumoto: *four seconds is a fortune. Wars have been bought with
less.*

*Ten.*

He got the bundle up between them — a stick in sacking, against that; he
knew what it was worth while he was doing it — and levered, and the man
took it off him the way you take a spoon off a child, and Sazare let it
go, because there was nothing else to do with it, and rolled the way
the taking pulled him, and came up on one knee facing the wrong way with
the flats path in front of him and the culvert mouth eight feet along it,
and the whole left side of him not working.

*Eleven.*

Eight feet. He went the eight feet in a sort of falling scramble, and
behind him he heard the man's feet reset — unhurried; a professional
does not lunge at a boy with a dead knee eight feet from a hole, he
walks, because the hole has an end and the far end is on a beach in
daylight — and Sazare went into the culvert mouth on his belly.

And grew a bar across it behind him.

---

He had never done it live. He had done it eleven hundred times on a bench
and in a rented room and on a hillside above a valley with a stopwatch of
his own counting, freehand, off the lattice, along a wire, around a form;
and he had never once done it with his hands shaking and his own blood in
his eye and a man's shadow coming across the mouth of a drain.

It came out wrong.

He knew it was wrong while it was still growing, the way you know a note
is flat while you are still singing it: too fast, unsmoothed, the
still-water gone out of him entirely — and it went across the culvert
mouth anyway, corner to corner, wrist-thick, blue-grey, ugly as a
splint.

The shadow arrived. A hand closed on it and pulled, and it did not come.
Something struck it, twice.

Sazare, eleven feet inside a pipe in the dark, on his elbows in six
inches of cold water, counted the strikes and knew precisely what he was
listening to, because he had spent eight months finding it out on a
bench: everything grown has a note, and everything grown *wrong* has its
note somewhere it should not be, and a wrong-grown piece will shatter at
its note if anybody happens to give it.

The third strike gave it.

The bar went with a sound like a dropped cup, and the sound went down the
pipe past him and out onto the flats, and the whole thing had lasted
four seconds.

Four seconds. He would have laughed if he had been able to breathe.

---

He was thirty feet in and going on his elbows with his left leg dragging
when he understood that he was not going to reach the other end.

The culvert ran under the ferry road and out onto the tide flats and it
was ninety feet long and it took the run-off of half the town, and the
far end was a grating in daylight on an open beach, and the man behind
him had already worked all of that out before Sazare went into it, which
was why he had walked instead of lunged.

There would be somebody on the grating.

He stopped.

There is a place, twenty-two feet in, where the culvert takes a
step-down and the old builders had set a haunch of dressed stone for the
water to fall over, and in the lee of it, under the road, out of the
sightline from either end, there is a pocket of dead air about the size
of a coffin.

He had put a cleat in it in his second week in Tomari. A finger of
blue-grey stone in the joint of the haunch, no bigger than a door hook,
grown on a wet Tuesday with his hand in the dark and water round his
wrist, for a purpose he could not have named to a magistrate and had been
faintly ashamed of before he was out of the drain.

He hooked two fingers of his right hand over it and pulled himself in
under the step and got his shoulders into the dead air and put his mouth
against the crown of the pipe where an inch of it stayed dry, and made
himself into an object.

Then he stopped counting, and went still.

---

*Still water,* said a room full of eight-year-olds, nine years ago, in
the fog. *You are a bowl of water. Nothing is being poured in and nothing
is going out.*

He was very good at it. He had been told at ten and a half never to let
anybody find out how good, by a woman who had made it sound like
household economy, and he had obeyed her, and had done seven minutes in
front of an examiner and been written down as *no distinguishing
feature*; and had once held it three hours in a drainage cutting under a
road with prisoners going past above him; and had never in his life been
told what it was for.

Water went over the step and past his face. Boots came into the mouth of
the pipe and stopped, and there was a long silence of the kind that is a
man listening, and Sazare lay in six inches of the Tomari run-off under
eleven tons of ferry road with his broken side going and did not think
about the boots, because you cannot hold still water while thinking about
a thing.

He thought about the grain in a piece of thwart timber that ran two ways
round a knot where a branch had been.

The boots went out. Somebody said something short at the road end.
Somebody answered from a long way off, out on the flats.

And then, much closer than either — from the mouth of the culvert, from
inside the pipe, from a man who had come in eight feet without a sound
and was crouched there in the dark not four yards from the step-down,
close enough that Sazare could hear the water off him — a voice said:

"Seven's fine."

---

It was flat. It came through porcelain, and porcelain takes everything
off a voice that a voice has, so it was not a voice he knew, and it was
not said the way a person says a thing to another person. It was said the
way you set something down.

For one entire breath Sazare did not understand it at all.

Then he did, and the understanding went through him worse than the knee
had, and for a quarter of a second he came very close to losing the
stillness altogether: a wall in the third month with the fog off and two
pairs of heels knocking against the stone, and a fried thing the woman
gave them two of and took money for one, and a boy coming down off that
wall going *you can do fourteen, Sazare, I've SEEN you do fourteen* —

*Seven passes. That's all it has to do.*

An examiner had had the seven minutes and a clerk had had the sheet, and
neither of them had ever heard the two words in their lives. The two
words had been said once, on a wall, above a market, to one person.

The water went over the step. The man in the dark did not move for three
seconds, and did not come forward, and did not say anything else, and did
not — this was the part Sazare would take apart for the rest of his life
— did not ask him anything. Not *are you there*. Not *come out*. Nothing
that would need an answer, nothing that could be reported as a challenge
refused, nothing that put a single thing on Sazare that he would have to
carry.

Just the two words, set down in the dark, from the only person on earth
who could have said them.

Then the man backed out of the culvert.

Sazare heard him reach the mouth and stand up in the light, and heard him
turn, and heard him say — in the flat porcelain voice, at volume, up the
ferry road, in a tone of clean professional boredom that would have
convinced anybody in the world:

"Nothing under the road. He's away north."

---

He stayed in the dead air for a long time after that.

Long after the boots had gone off the road. Long after the tide turned
and began pushing cold up the pipe from the flats end, an inch and then
three inches, so that he had to work himself up onto the haunch and hold
the cleat with his good hand to keep his mouth in air. Long after his
left leg had stopped hurting and started doing something worse, which was
nothing at all.

At some point in the afternoon he heard the ferry go out, which meant the
ferry had gone out, which meant Tomari had had an ordinary day.

At some point, above him, muffled through eleven tons of road, a small
voice was calling a name across the flats — not his, he had not given
anybody in Tomari his — calling the word the town used for him, twice,
and then further off, and then not at all.

He put his face in the crook of his own scarred arm, in the dark, in the
water, and stayed absolutely still, and was good at it.
