# Chapter 30, Scene 4 — Why Does This Heal

*Act 4, part C. The Hokage's office. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~1,234 words.*

---

She took the spent device out of Kanzaki's dish with two fingers and
held it up to the window light.

Half a gram of colourless grit, discharged, indistinguishable by eye
from the granite it had been grown from. Sazare had looked at it
perhaps forty times and had never once been able to see anything in it
that would have told a stranger what it had been.

"Nohara Daichi," Tsunade said. "Graft edge, lifting under mechanical
load. Yagi's pattern, eight seconds, keyed to the patient. Fired at
range while every medic in the building was occupied elsewhere."

"Yes."

"Three things wrong with it before I say anything else, and I want them
on the record because if I only tell you the interesting part you'll
build nine more before Thursday."

"Go on."

"One. Fixed dose. Yagi loaded that pattern into a man whose blood
pressure was one thing on the day and something else five days later,
and the stone released the same intervention regardless. On a graft
edge that's harmless. On a repaired vessel in a patient who's dropped
two units since you loaded it, the same pulse is a different medicine
and you have no way to know which one you've given him."

"I know. I don't have a solution."

"You don't need one yet. You need it *written on the label*, so the
next person who picks one of these up knows it can't think." She set
the grit down. "Two. Degradation. Urushibara says a week. On what
evidence?"

"...His judgement."

"His judgement is excellent and it is not a measurement. You have one
device, one patient, one discharge at five days. You do not know
whether the pattern degrades gracefully or whether it holds perfectly
for six days and becomes something else on the seventh, and those two
failure modes want completely different rules. Find out which. On the
bench, not on an arm."

"Yes."

"Three, and this is the one that actually frightens me." She put one
finger on the trial file. "A generic version of this closes skin over
contamination. Somebody will build that. Not you — you'd notice — but
somebody will read a summary of this work in two years and make a
stone that seals wounds, and it will seal an infected one, and the
patient will die of something that would have drained if nobody had
been clever." She looked up. "That's not a criticism of what you built.
It's the reason the label matters more than the device."

Sazare wrote all three down.

Then Tsunade picked the spent stone back up, and put a thread of chakra
into it — he felt it happen, a green flicker at the edge of his
attention, precise as a needle and about as subtle as one — and turned
it over once, and asked the question.

"Why does this heal?"

"It doesn't."

The room went quiet in a way it had not been quiet since he came in.

Shizune stopped writing. Somewhere below the window a cart went past
and a man shouted something at a horse.

Tsunade set the stone down and looked at him.

"Say the rest of it."

"It doesn't heal anything. It has no medicine in it. It's a recording
of eight seconds of what Yagi already did with her own hands, stored in
a structure that will hold a pattern without arguing with it, released
when the right key arrives. It can't diagnose, it can't adapt, it can't
decide whether releasing is a good idea. The patient's body does the
healing. The stone keeps one intervention from a person who couldn't
stay in the room."

"Then why does the tissue accept it?"

He opened his mouth to answer and found that he did not have one.

He had the whole of the mechanism — structure, store, condition, the
route a seed carried home, the signature copied onto a new key, the
seal on the seal — and he had a hundred and nine dated lines in a
notebook, and not one of them addressed the question she had just
asked, because he had never once thought to ask it.

*Why does a living body take an instruction from a rock and act on it
at all.*

"I don't know," he said.

Tsunade pulled a chair round from the side table with one foot, sat
down in it opposite him rather than behind the desk, and put both
forearms on her knees.

"No," she said. "Neither do I."

---

Later — much later, at the low table, with the lamp going and the
notebook open and the persimmon black outside the window — he would try
to write down what changed in the room at that moment, and would fail
four times, and would eventually settle for the only version that was
accurate.

*She stopped reading and started asking.*

At the time he had no capacity to observe it, because she had begun to
talk.

She talked about chakra as energy and intention as pattern, and living
tissue as a thing that supplies its own reference — that a medic-nin is
not pouring healing into anybody, but running a continuous conversation
in which the patient's own chakra comes back through the medic's and
tells them what to do next, four hundred times a second, which is why
control matters more than volume and why a strong medic with poor
control is more dangerous than a weak one with good.

And she said, in the middle of it, without slowing down:

"So the reason it works at all is that his body knows the shape of
Yagi's hands. Not the stone's. Hers. She ran that pattern on him six
times before you ever recorded it, and by the sixth the tissue had
already learned what to do when that particular shape arrived. You
didn't teach the stone to heal him. You taught the stone to knock the
way Yagi knocks."

She stopped.

Sazare watched a woman who had been talking at speed for four minutes
arrive at a sentence and stop dead in the middle of it, and understood
that she had not known she was going to say that until she heard
herself say it.

"That's not in your file," Tsunade said slowly.

"No."

"It's not in mine either." She sat back. "Which means either I've just
made it up, or nobody has ever built a thing that had to be answered
by a body in order to work, and so nobody has ever had a reason to
notice that a body can learn to answer."

"Can it be tested?"

"Yes. Badly, and slowly, and with about nine confounders I can name
sitting here." She was already reaching for a blank sheet. "Load an
identical pattern from a medic the patient has never been treated by.
Same wound, same dose, same key. If it takes worse, I'm right. If it
takes the same, I'm a woman who talked herself into something
attractive at two in the afternoon."

She wrote for perhaps ninety seconds, folded the sheet, and held it
out.

"Make me another one," she said.

Sazare took it.

"You've just told me three dangerous flaws in the first one."

"Yes," said Tsunade. "And I want another one. Those are both true, and
if you can't hold two true things at once you're going to be no use to
me at all."
