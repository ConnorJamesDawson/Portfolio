# Chapter 45, Scene 6 — The Fracture Surfaces

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,878 words.*

---

*You can't hold the outside of it. There isn't an outside.*

He worked on that for a day and a half in the wrong direction, which
was as an instruction about the sphere, and got nowhere with it, and on
the second morning he stopped trying to use it and simply looked at it,
and it came apart in his hands into something else entirely.

*She meant: the boundary is not a thing you maintain.*

*I have spent eleven years maintaining a boundary. That is what a course
IS in my model — an instruction laid into a volume, held inside its own
edge, and the edge is the thing that fails. Fails to grit if it was
never seeded. Fails to pieces if it was.*

*I have described every single failure I have ever had as an edge
failure and I have never once looked at the edge.*

He put his cup down.

*I have never looked at the pieces.*

---

Kanzaki weighed everything. She had weighed the spent chip in the
eleventh month of his sixteenth year at under half a gram, colourless,
grit, indistinguishable by eye from the granite it had started as, and
had written *0.5 g, consumed, single use* in a ledger and set it in a
labelled dish.

There were nine hundred and eleven labelled dishes in the back room of
the requisition office.

She kept everything. She had kept everything for thirty-one years,
because a quartermaster who throws away a failed article is a
quartermaster who cannot answer a question in four years' time, and
Sazare had walked past that room perhaps two thousand times and had
gone into it four times and had never once opened a dish.

He asked on the thirtieth.

"You want the failures," said Kanzaki.

"All of them. Mine. Three years."

"Two hundred and nine dishes." She was already reaching for the key.
"Bench three, and you bring every one of them back or I shall be
extremely unpleasant about it. What are you looking for?"

"I don't know yet."

"Good," said Kanzaki. "That's the first honest thing anybody's said in
this yard this week."

---

He laid them out over two days.

Two hundred and nine dishes on the far bench and the awning bench and,
when those ran out, on a plank across two trestles that Handa had left
in the yard in the spring and nobody had moved. Every failed article he
had produced in three years, in order, with Kanzaki's dates on the
labels in her copperplate.

He had Kanzaki's lens, which was a disgrace, and he made do with it for
the first day and then went to the sealing office on the second and
came back with a triple on loan from a clerk who did not know him and
had accepted a signature, and he did not think about why he had not
walked eleven minutes further and asked.

Then he looked at the fracture surfaces.

---

It took him about forty minutes to find it and about eleven seconds to
understand it, and the eleven seconds were the worst and best of the
month.

The grit was not grit.

Under a proper lens, at the sort of magnification a sealwright uses
routinely and a fabricator uses never, the half-gram of colourless
nothing that had been a medical chip was not powder. It was a
collapsed structure. It had *ways* through it, or it had had — fine
branching channels running from the centre outward, all of them
terminating, all of them terminating in the same manner, all of them
widened at the terminus into a bulb and then a break.

He knew that geometry.

He had seen it at thirteen, in Hot Water, in a burnt-out lamp housing
that a man had brought into the workshop wanting the silver salvaged,
where a wire too thin for its job had run hot along its whole length
and had gone at the point where it was thinnest and had left a bead of
melt on either side of the gap.

He sat down on the yard stone with the lens in his hand.

*It did not run out.*

*It burned out.*

---

He tested it, because a thing he had understood in eleven seconds was
a thing he did not yet know.

Two articles, matched as closely as he could manage: same stock, same
mass to within four grains, same figure, same seeding. One grown at
speed — one minute forty, which was the sort of thing he did in a
corridor. One grown across an afternoon and a morning, seven hours and
ten, laid slow, which he had almost never done in his life because
there had never been a reason.

Same course into both. Three deep, fine damping coarse, the ordinary
article.

The fast one went at four seconds.

The slow one did not go at all.

He held it at full course for a count of forty and then stopped
because his own arm had started to shake and the crystal had not
changed by any measure he could take.

He put it down on the felt and looked at it for a while.

Then he broke it, deliberately, with a hammer, which cost him
something, and put the pieces under the lens.

The channels in it were four times the width of the ones in the fast
piece and there were perhaps a third as many, and not one of them was
bulbed, and not one of them was broken.

---

*It is a wire.*

*It has always been a wire. Every article I have ever made has been a
conductor and I have been describing it as a container for eleven
years, and the entire difference between a fast one and a slow one is
not what it holds. It is what it can carry.*

*Time does not make a bigger vessel. Time cuts wider ways.*

*Four seconds is not the duration of a course. Four seconds is how long
that particular article survives me.*

He wrote that and then sat back from it, because the next sentence was
standing there and had been standing there since the eleven seconds on
the yard stone.

*I have been over-driving everything I have ever made.*

*Since I was seven.*

*Every splint plate. Every setting. Every wall in that corridor. The
slab I put on my own forearm in the street in about a second and a
half, which held for one impact and came apart, and which I entered in
a report as evidence that the technique had a limit.*

*It did not have a limit. It had a rating, and I exceeded it, and I
have exceeded it on every article I have ever produced under time
pressure, which is nearly all of them, because I learned this trade in
rooms where somebody was waiting.*

*I have spent eleven years putting a car's worth through a lamp's worth
of wire and writing "medium failed" in the column.*

*Three hundred and eleven entries. Fourteen months. In my own hand.*

---

He went and got the reject table.

It took him nine minutes to re-read it and every one of them was worse
than the last.

*Yard aggregate 22. Granite 31. Site rubble 44. Own prior work 4.*

*I read this in the third month as: what will take up an instruction,
and how fast. I put it in front of the Hokage at four in the morning
and she cleared her desk for it and it was correct.*

*It is also, and I did not see it, a grading table for conductor
stock.*

*Site rubble is scrap wire. It has been shocked, cracked, heated and
dropped by an invasion, and its ways are broken before I start, and it
fails at forty-four per cent because it cannot carry me.*

*My own prior work is four per cent because it has already been drawn.
The ways are cut, they are the right width, and they were cut by me at
my own rating.*

*It was never about memory and it was never about acquaintance. It is
gauge.*

He looked at that for a long time and then wrote the honest half
underneath, because it had to go on the same page.

*And the finding still stands. The material does take up part of the
instruction and the skill is still in the handing over, and I said that
to her and I meant it and it is true.*

*It is true the way "the splint held" is true. It is a correct
observation of an outcome by a man who did not know what was
underneath it, and I have now spent two days finding out that I was
right for a reason I could not have given if anybody had asked.*

*Which is exactly what I said about Yagi's bedside manner in the third
month, and what I said about the sixth hand's line in the scroll, and
what Wakabayashi did in a tent thirty-one years ago with two
categories in a subordinate clause.*

*Everyone is always right for a reason they cannot give. That is what
the reason is FOR.*

---

There was one more thing and he found it on the last afternoon, in the
dishes, and it was the one that mattered.

Not every failure had ways in it.

He sorted them — two hundred and nine dishes, four hours, on his knees
on yard stone with a borrowed lens — and about a fifth of them had no
channelling at any magnification he could reach. Solid through. No
branches, no termini, no beads. They had failed mechanically, cracked
or crushed or gone at a seat, the way a stone fails, and not one of
them had ever carried anything.

He checked the labels.

They were the storage articles. The medical chips. The recorded stones.
Every single object he had ever made whose job was to hold an
instruction and release it once and not to conduct anything at all.

*Two kinds.*

*Not two grades. Two kinds, and I have been making both of them since I
was nine without ever once distinguishing them, because I have never
looked at a fracture surface in my life.*

*One has ways in it and carries. One is solid and stores.*

*And I have been making the choice between them by accident — by how
fast I laid it, by what I laid it into, by whether somebody was waiting
— for eleven years.*

He put the last dish back in the tray.

*The medical stones are the solid kind. That is why they degrade at six
days: there are no ways in them, so the order inside has nothing
holding its shape and it slumps, and I have measured that fifty times
out to nine days and written three days on the label.*

*If I put ways in them they would not degrade.*

*They would also not be storage any more, and I do not yet know what
they would be, and I am not going to find out this month, and I am
writing that sentence down so that in four weeks I can see that I knew
it and check whether I obeyed it.*
