# Chapter 23, Scene 5 — The Roster

*Act 4, part A. Konoha, the third month. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~2,300 words.*

---

By the third month he had a week and the week had a shape, and it was the
first shape any week of his life had ever had.

**Sixth hour to the fourth**, six days, second floor. Dressings, obs,
lifting, the corner bed, and about forty minutes a day of some door or
window or trolley wheel that nobody had asked him to do.

**Tuesday and Thursday evenings**, the sluice, with Nurse Yagi and a
scalpel and his own left forearm, which by the third month had eleven
faint pink lines on it in a row like a tally and had become a joke on the
ward.

**The reading room**, second hour to the tenth, on his day off and on
about half the evenings that were not sluice evenings, at the end of the
furthest table with his back to the wall.

**And the crystal**, at night, at the low table, under the window, with
the persimmon tree coming into leaf.

That was the whole of it and it added up to about eleven hours a day of
work he had chosen, and he had not been so tired since the veranda at
Kasado and had not slept so well since he was twelve.

The quota he met every month and never comfortably. It was set at four
hundred grams of usable substrate and it looked, on the form, like
nothing; and in practice it meant about nine hours a week of sitting
absolutely still with his hands on a tray after a ten-hour shift, because
usable meant Kanzaki's usable, and Kanzaki's usable meant a flat that
would take a graver at the edge without chipping out, and about two thirds
of what he grew went in the failure tin.

She weighed the tin. She weighed it every Thursday. In the third month she
looked at the ratio for a while and then said, without any particular
emphasis:

"Your failures have come down eleven per cent since the first month."

"I've been going slower."

"Mm. Why?"

"Because I've been putting four minutes into a two-inch cut on my own arm
on Tuesdays," said Sazare, "and it turns out that whatever it is I do in
there is the same thing, and it's got better, and I don't entirely
understand why yet."

Kanzaki Uta pushed her glasses up onto her forehead and looked at him.

"Write that down," she said. "Not in the ledger — write it down for
*yourself*, tonight, in the exact words you just used, and date it. In
about nine years you'll want to know what day you noticed."

---

Ashida Kōhei stopped coming to the door in the second month and started
catching him at the hospital gate instead, because it was on his way and
because, as he put it, "you're always in the same three places and I've
run out of ways to write it down."

He had, by then, developed a system: he wrote *hosp / lib / home* in box
eleven and *nothing* in box twelve, and had done so about a hundred and
twenty times, and once — Sazare saw it over his shoulder — had written, in
box twelve, in a fit of despair, *nothing again*.

"They'll pull you up on that."

"They will not read it," said Ashida with enormous certainty. "It goes
upstairs, into a tray, and an old man looks at the top sheet on a
Wednesday and asks somebody a question about somebody else."

He was, as far as it went, entirely right, and he was also entirely wrong,
and neither of them ever found out.

In the third month his wife had a baby, and he was off for eleven days,
and the man who covered him was a chūnin called Sōma who did the job
correctly and said nothing whatever and made Sazare realise, in about four
days, how much of the last four months had been eased by a fat cheerful
man complaining about spelling.

When Ashida came back he was grey to the eyes and enormously pleased with
himself and had a folded piece of paper with a footprint on it in his
jacket, which he showed to Sazare at the hospital gate at six in the
morning without being asked.

"Girl," he said. "Seven pounds and a bit. She's got a chin on her like my
mother, poor kid."

"Congratulations."

"Yeah." He put it away carefully. "Right — box eleven. Where are you
today?"

---

The village started filling up in the second week of the third month and
Sazare noticed it from the hospital before he noticed it anywhere else,
because hospitals fill up first.

Not with casualties. With *paperwork*: foreign nationals presenting with
things, which meant identity checks, which meant Sister Kaburagi at the
desk being short with people, and a laminated card going up by the door
about which forms applied to whom.

"Exams," said Nurse Yagi, when he asked. "Chūnin Exams. They come round
between the villages and it's our turn, and every three years this place
fills up with about two hundred foreign genin and their instructors, and
for about six weeks the whole village is polite and the training grounds
are booked solid and the hotels put their prices up."

"Two hundred."

"Give or take. Suna, Kusa, Ame, Taki this year, and there's a new one I've
not heard of." She shook a thermometer down. "And about four hundred
tourists, and gambling, and every pickpocket between here and the capital.
It's a *nightmare* and the village makes a fortune and we get about
eleven extra a week for the duration. Watch out for the ones with
concussion who won't admit it. That's every year."

So Sazare, who had spent two and a half years walking into towns and
reading them, walked out into a village he had lived in for four months
and found it full of strangers, and did what he had always done, and could
not stop.

He did not go looking. He simply could not turn it off: he went to the
reading room and came back past the west market and got, in twenty
minutes, without asking a single person a single question, about nine
things.

Most of them were nothing. Suna teams travel with their own water and are
funny about it. Kusa genin are older on average than everybody else's and
he could think of three dull reasons for that. The inn on the west road
had put a man on the door who was not an innkeeper's man.

And one of them was odd, and it was odd in the specific way that only a
person raised in a workshop would ever have seen.

---

There were three of them at a stall on the market row, buying dumplings,
and they had forehead protectors on with a symbol he had never seen: a
musical note, cut through with a horizontal bar.

Sazare stopped at the next stall along and bought something he did not
want, and looked at the metal.

He had spent nine years in a family that made small metal-and-stone
articles for a living and had spent four of those years cleaning copper
and sorting fittings, and he had been able to read a stamped plate since
he was about six.

All three plates were new.

Not *clean*. New: unworn at the punch-line where the cord goes through,
which is the first place a plate wears and which is worn on every
protector he had seen in Konoha, including the boy in orange's, which was
worn nearly through on the left; no bloom on the metal; no plate-flex at
the fold; and — he got a good angle at the third one when the boy turned
his head — the same die.

The identical die. All three. Same strike, same depth, same very slight
flaw where the bar met the note on the left-hand side, which meant one
tool, one batch, and recently.

He stood at a market stall in Konoha with a thing he had not wanted in his
hand and turned it over the way he turned over everything.

*A village that has existed for twenty years does not do that. A village
that has existed for twenty years has plates of nine different ages in it,
because they get replaced when they're lost and broken, and you see
old strikes and new strikes and one bloke's grandfather's, and I have
watched about four hundred of these go past me in this village in four
months and no two of them are the same age.*

*Three out of three from one batch means either a brand-new village, or a
brand-new* team *that has been issued together, or—*

And that was as far as it went, because that was as far as the evidence
went.

It could have been a new village. New villages existed; there were about
eleven small hidden villages on the continent and some of them were younger
than he was. It could have been a first-year intake being kitted out
together. It could have been a proud minor country buying a batch of
plates for a delegation because a delegation is a shop window.

It was three headbands.

Sazare Ruri stood on the market row in the third month and did the other
arithmetic, which took him about four seconds and which he was never able
to argue himself out of afterwards.

*I am a probationary foreign national on a combat restriction with a
liaison and a daily report.*

*If I walk into an office and say "I have been examining other foreigners'
equipment in the market and I have conclusions", then I have told this
village three things: that I do that; that I am good at it; and that I
have been doing it since I got here.*

*There is nothing in what I have got. There is no report in it. There is a
sentence about a die-strike and a village nobody has heard of, and any
duty officer in the world would write it in a book and forget it, and the
only durable consequence would be a line in my file that says* subject
conducts unprompted surveillance of foreign nationals.

*And I have exactly one asset in this country and it is that nobody has
ever had to wonder about me twice.*

He put the thing he had not wanted in his coat and walked back to the
north road and did not mention it to Ashida Kōhei, or to Sister Kaburagi,
or to anybody else.

---

The roster went up on the ward wall on the last Friday of the third
month.

It was a piece of card about two feet by three, ruled in pencil, with the
days across the top and the names down the side and the shifts written in
by Sister Kaburagi on a Friday afternoon with a very sharp pencil and a
ruler and no patience whatsoever; and it was pinned to the wall by the
sluice door, at eye height, where forty people a day walked past it.

Sazare came up at the sixth hour on the Saturday and stopped in front of
it, because everybody stopped in front of it, because that is what a
roster is for.

**YAGI. KANNO. ŌTA. HOSHINO. RURI. TERAOKA. Fuji (nights).**

He stood in a hospital corridor with a laundry bag over his shoulder and
looked at his own name in the fifth row in pencil.

*Ruri — Mon 6, Tue 6, Wed off, Thu 6, Fri 6, Sat 6, Sun 6.*

It was the fifth time an institution had written his name down.

There had been men in an office at Kirigakure with a schedule and four
names in block capitals under a heading, and a column for *bloodline* and
a column for *use*.

There had been a fat sweat-swollen book in an oilcloth packet with a
boxed price at the bottom of the entry and a line about the hands.

There had been a lamp-oil warehouse with a list four deep and a fat
cheerful man tapping the fourth one with a finger like a sausage.

There had been a printed sheet in a shed with *NIL* on it four times.

And there was this: a piece of card on a wall by a sluice door, written in
pencil by a woman who did not particularly like him, which said, in
effect, and to about forty people a day, and for the coming week only:

*This one will be here at six.*

Every other ledger in his life had been somebody writing down **what he
was worth**.

This one was somebody writing down **where he would be** — and it was
written in advance, by somebody who was relying on it, and if he did not
turn up on Thursday the whole of that column had a hole in it and Nurse
Yagi would have to do the obs on eleven beds by herself.

Sazare Ruri stood in a corridor that smelled of carbolic and boiled
laundry, at sixteen years and four months old, four hundred miles from
everything, with a bag of dirty linen on his shoulder and a woman shouting
at somebody about a trolley on the floor above.

*A person, a place, or a trade,* a man with very clean hands had said, at
a table, in a room with a barred window. *I don't much care which and
neither will it.*

*Only don't leave it empty.*

He looked at the pencil for a while longer than he needed to.

Then he took the laundry up to the second floor, because it was two
minutes past six and he was on the roster.
