# Chapter 1, Scene 1 — The Frames

*Act 1, beats 1–2. Sazare Ruri, age seven. POV: Sazare. ~2,100 words.*

---

The frames were humming when he woke, which meant his mother had been up a long
time.

They only started once the medium took. You could seed a frame at midnight and
hear nothing until dawn, and then all at once the whole room would come alive
with it — a thin, dry note, like a wet finger going round the rim of a cup, from
every frame at once and none of them quite in tune. Sazare had asked once whether
you could make them agree. His mother had said that if he ever worked out how,
she would like to hear it.

He lay still and counted. Nine frames humming, which was all of them, which meant
a good night.

The compound was cold in the way it always was in the eighth month, when the sea
fog came up over the wall before light and sat in the yard until someone opened a
door and let it out. He could hear his father in the kitchen doing something
unhurried with a pot. He could hear the gulls arguing about the fish drying on
the Nakashimas' roof two houses down, which they did every morning, and which
old Iwao complained about every morning, and which nobody had ever done anything
about.

He got up.

---

The workshop took up the north side of the house because the north side was
coldest and the light was even. Nine frames on trestles, each one a shallow
wooden tray with a bed of grey sand in it and a lattice of copper wire over the
top, and his mother sitting at the end of the row with her sleeves pinned back
and her hands flat on the last frame, not moving.

He knew better than to speak. He sat down on the step where he always sat and
waited, and watched the muscle in her forearm shift very slightly, and after a
while she took her hands away and shook them out and said, "Morning."

"Did it take?"

"They all took." She looked along the row without much expression. "Number four's
gone cloudy. It'll do for buttons."

Sazare went and looked at number four. The crystal had come up through the sand
in a low irregular shelf about the size of two hands, faintly blue, and there was
a milkiness through the middle of it like breath on a window.

"Why does it do that?"

"Because I let it go too fast in the night."

"But *why*." He put a finger near it, not touching. "Why does going fast make it
cloudy? Why doesn't it just make it faster?"

His mother made the small sound she made when she was thinking about something
else. "Because that's what happens."

"But *why* is that what happens?"

"Sazare."

"I'm only asking."

"You're always only asking." She stood up and stretched her back until it
clicked, which he loved, and which she hated. "Your grandfather used to say fast
crystal is a fast man's crystal. He said it like it meant something. Nobody ever
asked him what."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." She picked up the cloudy shelf, tested its weight,
put it down again. "Go and get yours."

---

His own frame was the tenth, and it did not live in the workshop. It lived on the
sill in the room he slept in, because it was small enough to sit on a sill and
because his mother had said, when he was five and had asked for a frame of his
own, that she was not having a child of hers under her elbow every morning while
she worked.

He carried it in with both hands and set it down at the end of the row where
there was space, and stood back.

There was one piece in it. It had come up crooked, leaning off to the left the
way they always did for him, and it was about the length of his thumbnail, and it
was not blue. It was nearly colourless, with a grey cast, and when he picked it
up and held it to the window he could see straight through the middle of it to
the yard outside.

Hollow. Not cloudy — hollow, which was worse. A shell with nothing in it.

His mother took it out of his fingers and turned it over twice and held it up the
way you would hold up something you were seriously considering.

"That's a good shape," she said.

It was not a good shape.

"The lean's better than last time," she went on. "You held it steadier at the
start. That's the hard part and you did it." She turned it again. "It'll take a
polish. What's it for?"

"Kohaku."

"Ah." She handed it back. "Then don't polish it too far. She likes them a bit
rough, she says it looks like you meant it."

He held it in his palm and looked at it. It was not a good shape, and he knew it
was not a good shape, and his mother had looked at it for slightly longer than
she needed to before she said anything. He was seven and not stupid.

"It's hollow," he said.

"Yes."

"You said last time it wouldn't be hollow if I fed it slower."

"I did say that."

"I fed it slower."

"You did." She sat back down at her own frame and pinned her sleeve again. "And
it's less hollow than it was. It's a bad piece, Sazare. It's a bad piece that's
better than the last bad piece, which is the only kind of better there is."

He thought about that.

"How many bad pieces did you make?"

"Hundreds."

"How many did *Kohaku* make?"

His mother's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Ask her," she said.
"And then don't believe her."

---

Breakfast was rice and the fish that had not been good enough to dry, and his
father talking to Iwao about the cost of copper wire, which was a conversation
they had approximately once a fortnight and which never ended with anybody doing
anything.

"It's up again," his father said. "Fourth time this year."

"It's up because there's a war on somewhere," said Iwao. "There's always a war on
somewhere. You'd think a man could buy wire."

"You could use the old lattices."

"The old lattices are *shot*."

"You could straighten them."

"I could straighten *you*," said Iwao, without looking up, and Sazare's father
laughed and got up to fetch more tea, and Sazare put the earring on the table
next to his bowl where he could see it while he ate.

Iwao noticed it. He noticed everything, which Sazare's father said was because he
had nothing else to do.

"That yours?"

"Yes."

Iwao picked it up between finger and thumb — his hands were enormous, and the
piece disappeared into them — and held it at arm's length, because his eyes were
going and he refused to admit it.

"Hollow," he said.

"I know."

"Hm." He put it down. "Your mother's were hollow."

"She said she made hundreds."

"She made *thousands*." Iwao went back to his rice. "First one she made that
wasn't hollow, she cried for an hour. Nobody could work out why. Neither could
she." He pointed his chopsticks at the earring without looking at it. "Keep it.
When you make one that isn't hollow, you'll want to know what the difference
was."

---

Afterwards he went and stood in the small room off the workshop, which was not a
room so much as a cupboard with a window, and which held the account books, and
the tax scrolls, and the box.

The box was black lacquer and about as long as his forearm, and it sat on the
second shelf, which had been chosen at some point in the family's history as the
shelf that was too high for children and low enough for short adults. It had sat
there his whole life. It was not locked. Nobody in the house would have dreamed
of opening it, which his father said was a much better lock than a lock.

Inside it was a scroll, and on the scroll were the clan's combat forms — all of
them, every technique any Ruri had ever worked out and thought worth writing
down, going back four hundred years.

Sazare had never seen it open. His mother had seen it open twice. Kohaku, who
had graduated at nine, had learned what was in it and never once told him a
single word of it, not even when he had bribed her with a whole month of doing
her chores.

He stood on the step stool and looked at the box for a while.

"You're not tall enough," said his mother from the workshop, without turning
round.

"I'm not touching it."

"I know you're not."

"I'm just looking at it."

"I know."

He got down off the stool. Through the doorway he could see her back, and the
long row of frames, and the grey sand, and the light coming in flat and even off
the fog.

"When?" he said.

"When you graduate."

"That's *years*."

"Then it'll be years." She reached out and adjusted the copper lattice on number
six by an amount he could not see from where he was standing. "And when you open
it you'll want to be able to hold a frame steady for four hours, because half of
what's in there is worse than useless if you can't. Which is why you're going to
go and seed your frame again now, instead of standing in a cupboard looking at a
box."

"It's a bad piece."

"It's a better bad piece," said his mother. "Go on."

He went.

Behind him the frames were still humming, all nine of them, none of them quite in
tune, and he thought — not for the first time and not for the last — that there
had to be a reason for that, and that somebody, somewhere, must know what it was.
