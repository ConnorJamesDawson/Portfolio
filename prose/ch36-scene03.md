# Chapter 36, Scene 3 — Whether There Is Anywhere to Stand

*Act 4, part C. Konoha, the intelligence building. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,104 words.*

---

Hazama remembered him.

He had not expected her to. She had read to him for two hours and
eleven minutes in a room with a broken radiator eighteen months
earlier and had filled two glasses of water and drunk neither, and he
had assumed — reasonably, he thought — that he was one of about four
hundred people she had read to.

She looked up from a page when he came in, and put her glasses down on
their cord, and said:

"Ward eleven."

"You remember."

"I submitted a query on it," said Hazama. "On my own account, that
afternoon. It came back nine weeks later with nothing in it and I never
told you, because there was nothing to tell you, and because I have
learned over about twenty years that *I asked and there was nothing*
lands worse on people than silence does." She squared the page. "I have
been fairly sure that was the wrong call for eighteen months. Sit
down."

---

He put the question badly and she let him finish it badly, which he
had begun to notice was a professional skill in that building and not
a courtesy.

"Is there a grave."

"Whose?"

"Any of them. My mother — Ruri Kaede, she's in the district register,
she was the registered holder. Or the compound. There were about
forty of us in nine streets and I don't know what happened to any of
them after the platform and I have never once asked anybody, and I have
just worked out that I could be standing in the Land of Water in
sixteen months and I don't know whether there is anywhere to stand."

Hazama wrote *Ruri Kaede* on a pad without asking him to spell it,
because he had spelled it while saying it, which he had not noticed
doing.

"Right," she said. "I'm going to tell you what the honest range of
answers is before I send anything, because you will construct your own
in the interval and yours will be worse than mine."

"All right."

"Best case. There's a communal ground. In a clearance of that period
the bodies went somewhere, and *somewhere* is usually a municipal
ground on the edge of a ward with a stone on it that says a year and a
number and nothing else. If that's what happened there'll be a burial
register, and the register will have a date and a count, and possibly
names, and you would be able to stand in a specific field."

"And the worst?"

"The worst is not what you think it is." She set the pen down. "The
worst is not *nothing*. The worst is that there are four different
answers in four different offices and none of them agree, and you spend
three years finding that out one letter at a time."

"That's happened before."

"That has happened to about nine people I've corresponded with and it
is considerably crueller than a blank." Hazama pulled a form toward
her. "Which is why I'm going to send four queries and not one. Burial
register, ward clearance records, the reconstruction survey — because a
phase-three deferral means somebody walked that ground with a clipboard
and wrote down what was on it — and the temple registers, which nobody
ever thinks of and which in my experience are the ones that come back
with something."

"Temple registers."

"People say prayers for the dead whether or not the state is keeping
records," said Hazama. "In my experience the state's the least reliable
party in any of this."

---

She wrote the four queries in front of him, which took twenty-five
minutes, and read each one back before she sealed it.

He had expected to feel useful during that and did not. He sat in the
chair and watched a woman he had met twice write four letters about his
family in a hand flattened at the first two joints by thirty years of
holding a pen, and had nothing whatsoever to contribute to any of them,
because everything they needed was a date or a ward number or a
register reference and he had none of it.

The only thing he could give her was the name.

"Anything else?" said Hazama, on the fourth.

"She was left-handed."

Hazama's pen stopped.

"That's not — " Sazare stopped. "That's not useful. I'm aware it's not
useful. I've been sitting here for twenty minutes trying to think of
one fact about her that would help you find where she's buried and
there isn't one, and that's the only thing that came."

"It's not useful," Hazama agreed, and wrote it on the pad anyway, off
to the side, where it was not part of any query. "I'm keeping it
because it's the only line in this file that isn't a number, and if
any of these come back with a list of forty names I would rather have
one human fact in front of me while I read it."

---

"How long?"

"The burial register, three months if it exists and eleven if it's been
moved. Clearance records, four. The survey, quicker — that's a live
department and they answer, because they want to be seen answering."
She counted them off. "Temples, no idea. Could be a fortnight, could be
never, depends entirely on whether there's a priest who cares about
correspondence."

"So somewhere between a fortnight and never."

"Somewhere between a fortnight and never, and I will write to you at
the hospital each time one comes back, including the ones that come
back empty." She looked at him. "That's the bit I got wrong last time
and I'm not doing it twice. If it's empty you get a letter saying it's
empty. You do not get silence and a woman deciding on your behalf that
you'd rather not know."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me for that, it's the minimum and it took me eighteen
months to work it out." She sealed the fourth. "One thing, and then I'm
going to make you leave because I have nine hundred pages of a fishing
dispute to read."

"Go on."

"You have sixteen months. Don't spend them waiting for these." Hazama
put the four envelopes in the out-tray, squared. "I have watched people
do that. It's the commonest thing that happens after somebody sends
letters like these, and it is completely understandable and it wastes
the year, and the letters come when they come and not one day sooner
for being waited on."

"What should I do instead?"

"Thirty kilos of stone, I'm told," said Hazama, and went back to the
fishing dispute.
