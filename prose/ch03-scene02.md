# Chapter 3, Scene 2 — Thin

*Act 1. Kohaku Ruri, fifteen. POV: Kohaku. ~2,400 words.*

---

The man in Kirigakure who knew routes was called Ishibe and worked in a
chandler's on the west quay, and Kohaku had known him for four years and had
never once been inside the shop.

They did it on the quay itself, in the open, at the hour when the boats came in
and there were two hundred people shouting at each other about fish. She would
buy rope she did not need. He would sell it to her. It took ninety seconds and
they had never had a conversation that could not have been overheard by a
harbourmaster.

"Half-inch. Four fathom."

"Four?"

"Four."

Ishibe measured it off along the pegs, arm over arm, without hurrying. He was
about sixty and had a face like something left out in weather.

"Nagihama's slow," he said.

"Nagihama's ninety and she spills the tea."

"Nagihama," said Ishibe, "is the only one of you I'd bet on in a hard month."
He cut the rope. "Shirakawa's four men now."

"I noticed."

"You went through it."

"I had a letter and a bag of rubbish."

"You went *through* it," said Ishebe, and coiled the rope, and did not look up.
"There's a way round it at the Kuromizu ford that adds a day. You'll take it."

"That's a day."

"You'll take it," he said, "because four men on a table is a man with a list,
and a man with a list stops being interested in your bag and starts being
interested in you, and you're fifteen and you're on that road twice a month and
somebody is going to start counting." He handed her the coil. "Eight hundred ryō."

She gave him eight hundred ryō.

"Anything going up?" she said, which was the only question she was allowed and
which she asked every time.

"Same as always. There'll be a form."

"There's always a form."

"There's always a form," Ishibe agreed. "This one's the intake, in the ninth
month, same as last year. It'll have the line on it again." He took the money and
put it in the box. "And this year they're sending someone round to help people
fill it in."

Kohaku stood with four fathoms of half-inch rope she had no use for.

"Help them."

"Assist," said Ishibe. "Word they're using is assist."

"For all households?"

"For households in the trades," he said. "Which is a way of saying for households
where the work is the sort of thing that goes on a form, which is a way of saying
what you think it says." He shut the box. "Nothing's happening. It's a form.
People will fill it in and it'll be fine and I'd be grateful if you'd stop looking
like that on my quay."

"I'm not looking like anything."

"You're looking like a girl who's just heard something," said Ishibe. "Buy some
tar."

She bought some tar.

---

She thought about it all the way home, which was a mistake, and she knew it was a
mistake while she was doing it.

The thing was that none of it was anything.

A line on a form was not anything. Four men at a checkpoint were not anything;
there were four men at the Shirakawa cutting because the Kaguya had walked into
the village six weeks ago and killed sixty people, and any village on earth would
have put four men on a cutting after that, and it would have been strange if they
hadn't. A clerk coming round to *assist* households in the trades was not
anything either, because half the households in the trades had somebody in them
who could not read, and a form that people filled in wrong was no use to anybody.

Every single piece of it had a reason, and the reason was always sensible, and if
you laid the pieces down one at a time and looked at each one on its own you would
have had to be an idiot to be frightened of any of them.

She had been laying them down one at a time for two years.

She had a place in her head where she put them, which she had come to think of as
a shelf, and the shelf had on it: the Yuki, three months back, which had been a
raid and had been reported as a raid and about which nobody had said anything
because the Yuki had been in the hills and nobody had known any of them. The
Kanō, before that. The line on the register. Aoi Momoi and his bar. The word
*manifest*, which was a strange word to use about a person, and which somebody had
chosen. The four categories in the new Academy unit, which her brother had come
home and recited at dinner with his mouth full and no idea what he was saying,
and which she had listened to all the way through with her hands flat on her
knees.

And now: *assist*.

None of it was anything. All of it was the same thing.

---

She did not go straight home. She went up the hill instead, to the guild, and hung
about outside the wall with the broken glass on it until Genzō came out at the end
of the day with his spectacles pushed up into his hair, and fell into step with
him.

"Ah," said Genzō. "It's the impatient one."

"Have you got any offcuts?"

"I have always got offcuts. What do you want offcuts for? Your brother's got a
bag of my offcuts."

"He's got twelve. He's found six families in them."

Genzō stopped walking.

"Six?"

"Six out of twenty-one. He doesn't know it's twenty-one. He thinks he's found six
out of about eight and he's furious about the two he can't get."

"He's nine."

"He's nine," Kohaku agreed.

Genzō started walking again, more slowly.

"Six," he said. "Off twelve scraps."

"Off twelve scraps and about four evenings."

"Hm," said Genzō.

They went down the hill a way.

"You'll want to keep that quiet," he said.

Kohaku had been waiting four years for somebody to say something to her that she
could not immediately account for, and she found that it was not as satisfying as
she had expected.

"Why?"

"Because there are eleven people in this village who can do what I do," said
Genzō, "and we are all over fifty, and the guild has been telling the barracks
for six years that there is no shortage, which there is, and if it ever gets round
that a nine-year-old who isn't guild and isn't apprenticed and isn't *anything*
can sit on a step and sort my hands out of a heap—" He shrugged. "They'd want him.
That's all. They'd want him and they'd be very nice about wanting him and it
would be about eleven years before he saw daylight."

"He can't read them."

"He doesn't need to read them. Reading's the easy half." Genzō pulled his
spectacles down, found he did not need them, and pushed them up again. "Anyway.
He grows crystal. Badly, but he grows it, and that's what a Ruri does, and long
may it continue." He held out a hand. "How many do you want?"

"Twenty."

"Twenty."

"Different hands," said Kohaku. "All twenty different, if you can. And ugly ones.
Ones you'd have thrown out."

Genzō looked at her for a second.

"You're making it harder for him."

"He gets bored."

"Right," said Genzō, and did not ask anything else, which was one of the four or
five reasons Kohaku had liked him her entire life.

---

She lay awake that night doing the arithmetic she did not let herself do on roads.

*Ninth month. Say the eighth, they always run it early. That's five months.*

*Assist means a man at the table. A man at the table means he watches you write
it. So there's no leaving it blank, and there's no not applicable, and Aoi Momoi
is what happens to people who get clever, and everyone in this district watched
that happen and everyone in this district understood it.*

*So we'll write Y.*

*And then we'll write four names, because there are four of us it's manifest in,
whatever manifest means, and mother will write hers and mine and Sazare's and
uncle's, and she'll write them in block capitals because she'll have been told to
be accurate, and she'll be accurate, because she's the most accurate person I've
ever met in my life.*

*And then there'll be a list.*

*And a list is easy.*

She turned over.

*It's a form. It's twenty-one words on a form. It's been twenty-one words on a
form for one year and nothing has happened to anybody who filled it in properly.
Nothing has happened to the Hōzuki. Nothing has happened to us. We sell to the
quartermaster and the quartermaster likes us and Iwao is right about that even
though he's wrong about everything else.*

*Nothing is happening.*

She thought about Ishibe saying *there'll be a form* in the tone of a man
discussing the weather, and about Sen picking two chips out of eleven and saying
*that's Ishibe, he's been doing that since before your mother was born*, and about
her brother sitting on a step in the dark going *no — no, wait — that one and
that one*, and getting it right.

*Two more years.*

She was fifteen. In two years she would be seventeen, and Sazare would be eleven,
and he would be in his last year at the Academy, and there would be two more
intake registers between now and then, and she had already worked out that she
was going to let him carry things through both of them.

She had worked it out about a month ago and had not said it to herself in words
until just now.

*Nobody stops a child in the lower district.*

She lay there with the sea going and the frames humming through the wall, and
found, with the particular clarity that arrives at two in the morning and is gone
by breakfast, that she had become someone who used the phrase *nobody stops a
child* as a reason for doing something rather than as a reason for not doing it.

In the morning it was raining and there was work, and by the time she left she had
put it back on the shelf with the rest.
