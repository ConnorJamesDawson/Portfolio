# Chapter 34, Scene 5 — The Unfinished Line

*Act 4, part C. Konoha, the room over the persimmon tree. Sazare Ruri,
age seventeen. POV: Sazare. ~2,085 words.*

---

He opened it on the ninth night with the lamp lit and the shutter shut
and a clean cloth on the low table, because you did not put
four-hundred-year-old paper down on bare wood, and because he had spent
nine days finding reasons not to and had run out.

It was thirty-one feet long.

He had not expected that either. It came off the roller in sections and
he weighted each one with whatever was to hand — the tin, a cup, the
whetstone, eventually a shoe — and it went across the mat and up
against the wall and back, and when it was fully out it occupied the
entire floor of the room and he had to stand in the doorway to see the
whole of it at once.

Six hands.

That was the first professional observation and it steadied him
enormously, because it was a thing he knew how to do. Six distinct
hands, in three inks, over what he estimated on the first pass at
somewhere between two hundred and eighty and three hundred and forty
years. The earliest was a formal brush hand, upright and evenly
spaced, laid out in columns with ruled margins by somebody who had
been taught calligraphy properly. The latest was a fast slanted hand
in a thinner ink that had faded less, which meant it was newer by a
long way, and which had no margins at all and ran right to the edge of
the paper.

He read the whole thing twice, which took him until the fourth hour of
the morning, and then he sat back against the wall and did not move for
a long time.

---

It was a workshop manual.

Not a technique scroll. Not combat forms, not a kata sequence, not the
thing an eleven-year-old in a classroom in the fog had spent four years
imagining. Thirty-one feet of *how the Ruri made things*, accumulated
by six people over three centuries, each of whom had added what they
knew and corrected what they could.

And he knew almost all of it.

He worked through it methodically because working through it
methodically was the only thing keeping him upright, and he made three
columns on a fresh sheet and put every substantive item into one of
them.

**Column one: things I already do.** Sixty-one entries. Mineral
selection and where to find it. Preparation of media. The wetting of a
face before growth. Working temperature and why cold work fails. Nine
separate entries on the copper lattice — its dimensions, its gauge, the
alloy, the six-hour hold, the tension across the frame. The whole of
the fixture-and-frame tradition that his mother had taught him with her
hands before he was seven.

**Column two: things I do better.** Nineteen entries.

He was careful with that column. He checked each one twice and threw
four of them out on the second pass, because being pleased with himself
at four in the morning over his own dead family's paper was a thing he
was determined not to do carelessly.

Nineteen survived. The seeding. The courses. Recall. The grading of a
face by touch rather than by eye, which the scroll gave four lines and
which he had four years of measurements on. Speed of formation, on
which the scroll was frankly and repeatedly wrong, because six
generations of Ruri had worked at a bench with unlimited time and had
concluded that fast growth was simply bad work, and had never once
needed to find out what happened when a man had four seconds.

**Column three: things in here I did not know.** Eleven entries.

He wrote the eleven out and looked at them.

Two were about a mineral he had never worked with because it did not
occur in any of the four countries he had lived in. Three were
commercial — pricing conventions, a guild practice, a way of marking
work that had stopped being used before he was born. Four were
refinements to methods he already had, and all four were *slower* than
what he did, and he understood after some thought that this was not
because the Ruri were worse but because the Ruri had been protected,
and had had time, and had never in four hundred years had to make
anything under a load.

That left two.

One was a note on the storage of prepared media that was genuinely
useful and would save him about four per cent of a monthly quota.

And one was the last thing on the scroll.

---

The fast slanted hand was the sixth and it occupied the last eleven
feet and it was the only one that argued with anybody.

It argued constantly. It argued with the second hand about tension
across the frame and with the fourth about temperature, and it went
back and annotated earlier passages in the margin, twice with the word
that Sazare had to look at for some time before he accepted it was the
word: *wrong.*

Somebody in his family had taken this scroll down off a shelf and
written *wrong* on it.

The last entry was three-quarters of the way down a column and it
stopped.

*On the lattice, and I have put this off for two years because I do not
know how to write it without sounding as though I am accusing my own
grandmother of foolishness.*

*The lattice does not do what we say it does.*

*We say it holds the piece. It does not hold the piece. I have run this
four hundred times and I can now say it plainly: a piece under the
lattice and a piece under no lattice at all fail at the same load if
the growth is fast. The lattice makes no difference whatever to a fast
piece. It only makes a difference to a slow one.*

*Everyone I have asked says this is because slow work is better work,
which is not an explanation, it is the thing to be explained.*

*What I believe is that the lattice does nothing to the piece and that
what it actually does is to the maker — that it obliges us to work
slowly and evenly and to hold a thing for six hours, and that whatever
is really happening is happening inside the stone during those six
hours and has nothing to do with copper at all. We have spent four
hundred years crediting the frame with the work of the*

It stopped there.

Mid-sentence. Three-quarters down a column, in the middle of a word
that had got as far as four letters, with about nine feet of blank
paper after it.

The ink at the break was the same as the ink above it. The pen had not
run dry. The hand had not wavered.

Somebody had been writing that sentence and had stopped writing it, and
had not come back.

---

Sazare Ruri sat on the floor of a rented room in the Land of Fire at
four in the morning and put both hands over his mouth.

*Somebody got there.*

*Somebody in my family stood at a bench and ran it four hundred times
and worked out that the lattice was the wrong object, and wrote it
down, and was about a tenth of the way to the courses when they
stopped.*

*Two years, they said. They'd been putting it off for two years. So
they knew for two years before they wrote a word of it, which means
they were sitting on it at the same bench, in the same house, in the
same nine streets—*

He got up and could not find anything to do standing up and sat down
again.

He had spent nine years believing that everything he had was invented
twice: once by people who were all dead, and once by him, alone, on
benches in four countries. He had written that sentence in a notebook
in the fifth month of his sixteenth year and had thought it was the
whole of his inheritance.

It was not true.

His family had not been finished. They had not been a completed
tradition that he had reconstructed from scraps. They had been *in the
middle of it* — arguing with each other on the same piece of paper
across three centuries, getting things wrong and marking each other's
work *wrong*, and one of them had been standing on the exact ground he
had spent nine years crawling toward and had been perhaps two years
from the courses.

And had stopped, mid-word, and had not come back, and there were nine
feet of blank paper after it because whoever it was had expected to
have time.

He did not know which of them it was.

That was the part that would not stop. There was no name on it. The
hand was not his mother's — he knew his mother's hand the way he knew
his own, and it was not that. An uncle. A great-uncle. A cousin. Some
Ruri he had passed in a street nine hundred times and never spoken to,
or had never met at all, or had been four years old with.

*I could have asked them.*

*If I had been eleven instead of nine. If it had been three years
later. If any single one of a hundred things had gone differently, I
would have walked into a room and somebody would have said, come and
look at this, I think the lattice is wrong, and I would have spent my
entire life not alone at a bench.*

---

He sat with that until the window went grey.

At some point he had begun, without noticing, to do the thing he did
with objects, which was to take the whole of it apart and put it back
together looking for the fault, and at some point after that he had
found it, and it was not in the scroll.

*Kiri hoped this would be of practical assistance to me in the exercise
of my craft.*

*It contains nineteen things I do better than my own family, eleven I
did not know, two of those worth anything, and one unfinished sentence.*

*It is worth about four per cent of a monthly quota.*

*I am the only person alive who knows that. Six people in a room
handed me an investment and it is not an investment, and the woman who
authorised it told me to my face that she had not read it and was not
going to, and I could go back tomorrow and tell them and it would
change nothing except that a man in a registry office in Kirigakure
would feel foolish about the one good afternoon he had this year.*

*So I will not tell them.*

*And I will hand over four years of fabrication under an agreement they
paid for with a scroll they think made me better, and every plate of it
will be made with my own methods, which are better than the ones in
here, which I worked out alone because there was nobody left to ask.*

He rolled it up.

It took him about twenty minutes because thirty-one feet of
three-hundred-year-old paper does not roll quickly and because his
hands were not entirely reliable, and when it was done he put the
decorative tie on it, because the original had not survived and he had
nothing better, and put it back in the paulownia box and put the lid
on.

He did not cry.

He noted that fact at the time with something close to alarm, and wrote
it down in the notebook underneath a column that had no unit at the
head of it:

*I have the last thing. It arrived. I have read all of it and I have
audited it in three columns and I cannot feel anything at all except
that somebody stopped in the middle of a word.*

*There is nobody to show it to.*

*That is not a complaint about Konoha. There is nobody in the world.
The only people it would have meant anything to are the six hands in
this scroll and all of them are dead, and one of them died in the
middle of a sentence about my own work, and I do not know their name.*

Then he blew the lamp out, and lay down on the mat, and at half past
five got up and washed and went to the hospital, because he was on at
six.
