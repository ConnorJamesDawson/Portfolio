# Chapter 12, Scene 2 — Seven Seconds

*Act 2, part C. Sazare Ruri, age fifteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,200 words.*

---

The assessment came at the half-year, unannounced, the way all the important
things in that yard came.

Sakai finished the morning's count, took the bokken off him, set both weapons
against the fence, and said: "Right. Sit down. I'm going to tell you what you
are, and you're going to listen to all of it, because half of it's bad and
people only ever remember the half they came for."

Sazare sat down on the chopping block.

"You started eight years late," said Sakai. "That's not a figure of speech —
it's arithmetic. The boys I took at seven in Iron had ten thousand cuts in
them by your age and the cuts had grown up with their bones. Yours are
guests. Good guests — you've laid them a clean room — but guests, and under
enough fear or enough surprise the house goes back to its own family, and
its own family is a tradesman who flinches with his hands *open*, because
everything he ever made or mended was made or mended with open hands. You
will never be a swordsman. I want that said plainly, once, by somebody who
likes you, so that it's yours and no enemy ever gets to give it to you as
news."

The steam went up beyond the fence. Sazare sat with it.

"That's the bad half," he said.

"That's the bad half."

"What's the other one?"

"The other half is that I've had the teaching of perhaps two hundred men,"
said Sakai, "and of the two hundred there were maybe six I'd have called
hard to kill, and none of the six was the best sword among them. It's a
different quality. It lives lower down than talent." He leaned on the fence.
"The best sword I ever taught died at nineteen in a duel he chose. The
hardest man to kill I ever taught couldn't beat him one bout in ten, and
he's alive today, somewhere, being disappointing. Talent is what you can
do. Hard to kill is what you *won't* do — you won't be drawn, you won't be
flattered, you won't fight the fight that's offered when you can fight the
one that isn't. You've been building that second thing since before I met
you. I've only put a handle on it."

He picked up the bokken and tossed it over.

"So. Doctrine. You get one, it's short, and everything we do from today is
this and nothing else." He held up a hand and counted it off on fingers
that never fidgeted. "Anyone who ever comes for you will be better than
you. Careful, a better man needs about seven seconds to kill you. Careless,
three. Your whole art — the entire sword, for you, forever — is making
careful cost him eleven and careless cost him a hand. And then you leave.
You leave, boy. Every plan you ever make ends with leaving, and the day you
catch yourself planning to *stay* — to finish, to win, to be somebody in
the story — that's the day you've picked up the first of the three wants I
told you about at the door, and you can bring me my jug and go."

"Eleven seconds," said Sazare, "is four more than seven."

"It is."

"Where do the four come from?"

"From the country you've already been to," said Sakai. "From the two feet
you know better than he does. From the wall he didn't know was at his back
and the step he didn't know was loose — you *fence like a mason*, I've
watched you do it, you fight ground the way other men fight people. Good.
Do that. Four seconds is a fortune. Wars have been bought with less."

---

He saw the wrapped sword drawn exactly once.

It was the last night of the year, when the whole town was down at the
shrine street for the bell, and Sazare was coming back late from Tōan's
rounds by the lane behind the woodshed, and the light was on in the yard.

Sakai stood in the middle of it in the cold, bare-headed, sober — the
lamps said so; everything about him said so — with the wrapped thing from
the corner of his room unwrapped at last, and its saya through his belt,
and his hands at rest at his sides.

There was a strip of paper on the woodshed roof, weighted with a pebble,
the sort of strip the shrine sold for wishes. The wind was worrying it.

Sazare stopped in the dark of the lane, and was still.

Nothing happened for a long time. The bell went down in the town, the
first of the hundred and eight, and the wind worked the paper loose by
degrees, the way wind does, and Sakai stood with his eyes shut. Not
waiting the way you wait for a thing. Waiting the way a frame waits.

The paper came free on a gust, lifted, side-slipped, and fell.

Sazare did not see the draw. He was watching for it, from twelve feet, in
adequate light, with the full attention of a boy who read hands, and he
did not see it: there was a man with a sheathed sword and a piece of paper
falling, and then there was a man returning a sword to its sheath and two
pieces of paper on the ground, cut on the diagonal, cleanly, in the
quarter-second between one bell-toll's dying and the silence — cut, he
understood much later, *at the only instant of its fall when it hung
entirely still.*

Sakai stood a moment. Then he said, to the empty yard, in a voice Sazare
had never heard him use — a formal voice, with rust on it, in the accent of
another country:

"Kept, this year also."

And wrapped the sword back into its cloth, and went up his stair, and
Sazare walked home the long way round so as not to have been there, and
never spoke of it, and put it in the collection at the very front, where
he could see it: the one good thing, kept against everything, taken out
once a year in secret and shown to nobody — proof, standing in a
bathhouse yard at midwinter, that a ruin is a building whose best room
may be perfectly intact.

He also understood, walking home under the bell, what the lesson had
been, though no lesson had been meant.

*It becomes an extension of yourself*, said everyone, everywhere, about
swords — Gonta said it, the theatre bills said it, even Jirō had said it
once, aged ten, about a fish knife. Sazare had filed it with the things
people say. But the paper had hung still in the air, and the cut had come
from a man who had thrown away everything in his life except that one
motion, and the truth was narrower and stranger than the saying: nothing
becomes an extension of yourself by being *given* to you.

It has to be paid for at the rate the wood teaches, and the stone, and
the sand.

*You'll make your own. You'll cut it wrong twice.*

He turned that over all the way home — a fifteen-year-old with a
tradesman's parcel of a body, four seconds of ground, a bloodline that
answered him like a struck bell, and no sword, walking through the steam
of the year's last night — and by the time he reached Tōan's porch the
thought had gone where all his real thoughts went, down into the
collection, quiet, patient, to wait its years out with the rest.

Not yet.

But some day, at the bottom of everything, there was going to be a blade
that nobody had handed him.
