# Chapter 23, Scene 1 — Can You Carry Things

*Act 4, part A. Konoha General. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare.
~2,150 words.*

---

He got into the hospital because of a paragraph in a book, and the
paragraph said, in effect, that the book was no use.

It was on the low shelf with everything else — *Field Care and Ward
Practice*, third edition, corners soft, a coffee ring on the frontispiece
and somebody's shopping list in the back — and he had read the whole of it
in four evenings and then gone back to the beginning and read it again
with a pencil, and on page nine, in the introduction, in smaller type
than the rest, was this:

*The student is warned that nothing in this volume, or in any volume, will
teach him to judge. Judgement is acquired at the bedside and nowhere else,
and is acquired slowly, and is acquired chiefly through error. The reader
who has understood this book perfectly and has never yet stood beside a
frightened person is a reader who knows a great deal and can do nothing.*

Sazare sat at the end of the furthest table in the reading room with
eleven-year-olds arguing about homework forty feet away, and read that
paragraph about nine times, and heard, underneath it, a flat dosage voice
from a treatment room up a valley in the Land of Hot Water saying almost
exactly the same thing with about a third of the words.

*You're no medic. You're a tradesman who can keep a man alive till the
medic comes.*

He had been in Konoha for six weeks. He had read eleven books. He was
better at anatomy than he had been in his life and he had not put his
hands on a living person since a bridge in the Land of Waves.

He put the book back on the low shelf where it lived and went and found
his minder.

---

"The *hospital*," said Ashida Kōhei.

"How would a person volunteer there?"

"They wouldn't." Ashida was eating something out of a paper twist and did
not stop. "That's not a thing. You don't volunteer at a hospital, mate,
you get *used*. If you go up there and say *I would like to help* they'll
put you on a chair for an hour and then forget about you, because they've
got about four hundred casualties a year and eleven people who can
actually do anything and no time at all to be nice to a foreigner with a
hobby."

"Then how—"

"You go to Sister Kaburagi's desk on the ground floor at the sixth hour in
the morning," said Ashida, "when she's doing the day sheet, and you don't
say a single word about wanting to help, and when she asks you what you're
for you tell her the two or three things you can physically do, in order,
shortest first."

Sazare looked at him.

"You've done this before."

"My wife's a nurse," said Ashida Kōhei, and put the rest of the twist in
his mouth. "Also I have to write down where you're going in box eleven and
*hospital* is much easier to spell than *library*, so I'm in favour."

---

Konoha General was four storeys of pale green plaster on the north side of
the village with a wing on it that had gone up more recently and did not
match, and it smelled, from about thirty feet outside the doors, of
carbolic and boiled laundry and the specific sweetish under-smell of a
building where a great many people are getting better and a few are not.

Inside it was noise. That was the thing nobody had told him and that he
never entirely got used to: hospitals are *loud*. Trolley wheels on a
tiled floor. Two porters arguing about a lift. Somebody's baby. A bell
going somewhere upstairs, twice, and then a third time with an entirely
different quality to it, and four people moving.

The ground-floor desk was a slab of scrubbed wood in a corridor with a
window behind it and a rack of clipboards on the wall, and at the sixth
hour of the morning there was a woman behind it with a pen and about
eleven sheets of paper.

Kaburagi Setsu was fifty-eight and about five foot one and had the
forearms of a woman who had been lifting adults out of beds for forty
years. Grey hair cut short and pinned back off her face with two plain
grips. A long white apron over the dark blue, ironed, spotless. Half-moon
glasses on a cord. And an expression of settled, comprehensive, entirely
impersonal impatience that Sazare recognised within about two seconds,
because he had seen it on a woman at an exchange counter at Shiranui with
the pen stopping and not-stopping.

She did not look up.

"Yes."

"Sazare Ruri. Resident foreign national, probationary. My conditions are
on file at the administrative office and I've brought a copy."

"Mm." She held her hand out for it without looking, and read it in about
nine seconds, and handed it back. "No combat training. Audited output.
Reports to the Hokage." Now she looked up, over the half-moons. "You're
the crystal boy."

"Yes."

"What are you for?"

And Sazare — who had been given the exact question the night before by a
fat man with a paper twist, and who had spent two hours on the answer, and
who had discarded four longer versions of it — said:

"I can lift. I can do dressings and I've done about two hundred. And I did
field triage on eleven people in the Land of Waves in November and nine of
them were hands."

The pen stopped.

"Who taught you dressings?"

"A country doctor in the Land of Hot Water. Two years. He wasn't a
medic-nin; there wasn't any chakra in it at all. Bandaging, sutures,
splints, packing, and about a hundred hours of being told I was doing it
wrong."

"Show me your hands."

He put them on the desk.

Sister Kaburagi looked at them for rather a long time — at the backs, and
then she turned them over herself, with a firm impersonal grip, like
somebody checking a tool she was thinking of buying, and looked at the
palms and at the scar on the right forearm and at the nails, which were
cut short and square and scrubbed, because they had been cut short and
square and scrubbed since he was seven years old and his mother had told
him that a long nail on a finishing hand is a scratch on somebody's
wedding.

"Hm," she said, and let go.

"Is that hm-good?"

"Don't fish," said Sister Kaburagi. "Sixth hour tomorrow. Wear something
you don't like. You'll be portering, which means trolleys, laundry, and
carrying things that other people are too senior to carry, and if you say
one word to me about dressings in the first fortnight I will put you in
the incinerator yard for a month."

"Yes."

"And you'll be paid," she said, "because everybody is paid, because the
last thing this hospital needs is a boy in the corridors who thinks he's
doing us a favour."

---

He portered for nine weeks.

It was, by a very wide margin, the hardest physical work of his life, and
he had lived in a lime kiln and walked ninety-one lots of forty paces on a
broken knee.

The shift was the sixth hour to the fourth, ten hours, six days. The floors
were tile and the trolleys were iron and the lift on the north side was
out of order from the third week and stayed out of order, and the wards
were on the second and third floors.

He carried, in the first fortnight: seventy-one loads of laundry; four
hundred meals up and four hundred trays down; a dead man, twice, which is
to say two of them, both times with a porter called Gonbei who had done it
for nineteen years and who talked the whole way about a horse he had
backed; a crate of glassware that he was told four separate times not to
drop; and one woman of about ninety who weighed nothing and held on to his
collar with both hands the entire way from the ambulance bay to the second
floor and told him that he was a good boy and that her son was coming.

Her son was not coming. He found that out on the fourth day.

His knee was the worst it had been since Kasado for the first three weeks
and then, in the way of knees, stopped being the worst and became simply a
thing that hurt in a stair-shaped way for the rest of his life.

Nobody talked to him for eleven days.

That was not hostility. He watched it carefully, because he watched
everything, and it was not hostility: it was that a hospital has about
sixty people in it doing shifts and a new porter is a name on a sheet
until he has been there long enough to be worth learning, and eleven days
is the going rate.

On the twelfth day a nurse he had never spoken to said, "You — do the
window in six, it sticks," and he did the window in six, which sticks
because the frame has dropped a quarter-inch at the hinge stile, and he
took it off and packed it and rehung it in about forty minutes on his
meal break.

On the thirteenth day two people asked him about doors.

By the end of the first month he had, without ever intending it and
without once mentioning it, mended eleven things in that building, and the
word had gone round the way it always did.

---

Sister Kaburagi did not speak to him at all for four weeks except to give
instructions, and then, at the end of a Thursday, at the desk, without
looking up:

"How many dressings did you say?"

"About two hundred."

"On what?"

"Quarrymen, mostly. Crush injuries and split hands. Some burns. Two
degloves and I made a mess of the first one." He kept his hands where they
were. "And nine people on a bridge, and one of those was a puncture
through the thigh that I packed and it held."

"Arterial?"

"No. It was venous and it was frightening and it wasn't going to kill him
in under about four hours, and I knew that at the time and I told everybody
in earshot the opposite because they were about to do something stupid."

Sister Kaburagi wrote something.

"Sixth hour Monday you're on the second floor with Nurse Yagi," she said.
"You will do exactly what she tells you and nothing else, and the first
time you touch a patient without being told to, you go back to trolleys
for a month and I will not discuss it."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." She capped the pen. "I've got four hundred casualties a
year and eleven people who can do anything, and you're a pair of hands
that already knows how to tie a knot. This is not a favour, it is
*arithmetic*, and if you were any good I'd have had you upstairs in a
fortnight."

---

He walked home along the north road at the fourth hour with the light
going and the smell of the laundry still on him, past the noodle place on
the corner where four chūnin were arguing, past a training ground where
somebody's team was still at it in the dusk.

He had done, that day: eleven laundry runs, a lift-out, two trays, an
argument about a trolley wheel, and forty minutes on a window that stuck.

He had not grown one grain of crystal. He was two hundred and eleven grams
behind on the month's quota and would have to sit up three nights to make
it, and he would, and Kanzaki would weigh every gram of it on Thursday and
write her initials in the sixth column.

And he had been told, by a woman who did not like him particularly, to be
on the second floor at the sixth hour on Monday.

Sazare Ruri walked back to a room he could not fortify with his shoulders
aching and his knee going and worked out, somewhere on the north road,
that for the first time since he was thirteen years old there was a place
he was expected to be that was not a job, a peg, a counter, a broker, or a
man with a book.

He put it in the tray.

Then he took it back out again, deliberately, and looked at it, because a
man with very clean hands had told him not to leave the middle of himself
empty for another four years and he had been thinking about that sentence
every single night for six weeks.
