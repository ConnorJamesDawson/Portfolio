# Chapter 8, Scene 1 — Attendance Is Not Required

*Act 1, beat 21. Sazare Ruri, age thirteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,600 words.*

---

The notice went up on the fifth day of the fifth month and he read it on the sixth
because he read everything on the way past, and it took him three attempts to get
through it, because the first two times his eyes went over it without picking
anything up.

**NOTICE OF SENTENCE**

*Persons convicted before the district tribunal of conspiracy against the security
of the village will be produced at the fish market square at the fourth hour on the
fourteenth day of this month.*

*Attendance is not required.*

That was the whole of it. There was no list. There were no names anywhere on the
sheet, and there was no charge beyond the one word, and there was nothing about
what *produced* meant, and it was on the same paper as the notice above it, which
was about the summer mooring fees, and the one below it, which was about a lost
dog.

He read it a fourth time.

Then he went and sat on the wall by the third bridge for about two hours and did
not buy anything.

---

There were eight days.

He spent them the way he had spent the last fourteen months, which was badly and
in queues, except that now he could not eat.

That was the thing he had not expected. He had been hungry for over a year and had
got extremely used to being hungry, and he had a system — Ozaki's wrap at the end
of the day, and the bakehouse on the north cutting where they put the burnt ends
out at dawn, and about nine sen put by. And for eight days he took the food and
carried it around and gave most of it away, which made him, briefly, a person of
enormous standing among the four other children who slept behind the boat yard.

He could not sleep either. That he had expected and it made no difference.

---

He heard the word *conspiracy* twice more before the fourteenth.

Once in a queue, from a woman who said it with relish and was corrected by a man
who said it was not conspiracy, it was something to do with the docks, and there
was a short argument about which was worse.

And once from a boy about his own age behind the boat yard, who said his father
said it was the *ones from the north*, and then would not say what he meant by
that, because he did not know, because his father did not know either.

Nobody knew anything. That was the whole texture of that fortnight. There was a
notice on a board with one word on it, and the entire lower district built out of
that one word a complete and confident account of what had happened, in about
nine mutually contradictory versions, all of which were delivered with total
authority, and not one of which contained a single name.

Sazare listened to all of them and did not say anything at all.

---

He did not decide to go.

That was not how it worked and he was clear about that afterwards, when he was
clear about almost nothing else. He did not sit down at any point in those eight
days and weigh it up and decide. He simply understood, from about the second
reading of the notice, that he was going to be in the fish market square at the
fourth hour on the fourteenth, in the same way that he understood he was going to
keep breathing.

He worked at the reasons afterwards for a long time and the honest answer was
never any better than this: for fourteen months he had not known one single true
thing about any of them. Not whether they were alive. Not where. Not why. He had
had a notice on a gate about sequestration, and a man at a counter telling him not
to come back, and twenty-eight pairs of feet on a wet road.

And on the fourteenth day of the fifth month somebody was going to *produce*
people, in public, in the square, in daylight, where anybody could look.

That was all. It was the first offer of information he had had in over a year and
it came from the people who had taken them, and he took it.

---

He got there at the second hour, two hours early, and so did about four hundred
other people.

The fish market square was the biggest open space in the lower district and it
sloped, which was why they used it for things; the market ran up it in rows on
market days and you could see the whole of it from the top. They had put a
platform up at the low end, against the wall of the ice-house — a proper platform,
scaffolded, waist-high, with steps at one end, and it had gone up over three days
in full view of everybody, and Sazare had walked past it four times and had made
himself look at it each time.

There were barriers. That was the detail he had not expected. Somebody had brought
in about sixty yards of the wooden barrier they used for the summer fair, and set
it in a square in front of the platform, and there were men at intervals along it
with their backs to the platform, facing the crowd, and their whole manner was the
manner of men working a fair.

And it was — this was the thing that would not leave him alone — it was *civic*.

There was a man selling roasted chestnuts at the top of the square. There were
children up on the wall of the ice-house because it was the best view and nobody
had told them to get down. Two women near Sazare were having a long, involved
conversation about a third woman's daughter's marriage and did not stop having it
at any point in the next two hours. Somebody had swept.

Everybody was there. That was what *attendance is not required* had done, and he
understood it perfectly at thirteen and could not have explained it: nobody had
been ordered to come, so everybody had come, because the one thing you could not
be was a person who had stayed away.

---

At the third hour the officials came out and stood at the back of the platform in
a row.

There were five of them and they were extremely ordinary. That was the last of it.
They wore what men in offices wore and they stood the way men stand who have been
told to stand somewhere, and one of them had a folder, and one of them kept
checking something in his sleeve, and one of them was old and had come out too
early and was visibly uncomfortable in the sun.

Sazare had worked his way down to about six rows from the barrier by then, which
had taken an hour and a half of the kind of patient sideways nothing that a small
thirteen-year-old in a crowd can do indefinitely.

He was close enough to see their hands.

---

At about the fourth hour a man came out to the front of the platform and read from
a sheet.

He read it in the voice people use for reading, which is to say without any voice
at all, and Sazare — who had spent four years copying out things Mizuho read from
books — recognised the register immediately and completely, and something in him
went very cold and stayed cold.

There had been an investigation. It had been conducted over a period. It had
concerned the movement of restricted material and of information, through the
district and out of it, over a number of years.

Certain households had been found to have been used.

Certain persons had been found to have used them.

The tribunal had sat. The tribunal had concluded. Sentence had been passed on the
persons named in the schedule.

He did not read the schedule.

"The sentence will be carried out," he said, and folded the sheet, and stepped
back, and that was the entire address.

---

Then they brought them up the steps.

Sazare had spent eight days preparing to be able to look, and had told himself
that whatever it was, he was going to look, because he had come to look and that
was the only thing he had.

He looked.

There were nine of them and they came up in a line with their hands tied in front
rather than behind, which he found out much later was because the platform steps
were steep and they did not want anybody falling and making it untidy.

The fourth one up the steps was his mother.

She had been on her feet for the whole of the walk from wherever they had brought
them from, and she came up the steps evenly, and her hair had gone almost entirely
grey in fourteen months, and her sleeves were not pinned.

The fifth was his father.

The seventh was his uncle Iwao, who had to be helped on the second step by the man
behind him, and who said something to him afterwards, and the man nodded.

And the eighth, coming up the steps last but one, with her hands tied in front of
her and her head up and her eyes going along the whole front of that crowd the way
you look at a wall you are trying to find something on, was his sister.
