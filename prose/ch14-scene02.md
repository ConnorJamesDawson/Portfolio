# Chapter 14, Scene 2 — Don't Round Up

*Act 2, part D. Kirigakure. Jirō, age sixteen. POV: Jirō. ~2,300 words.*

---

His brother had died in the second year of the corridor postings, on the
night the resistance took the fish traps at Aragane, in an action so small
it had not earned a name.

Jirō knew more about it than he was meant to, because knowing more than he
was meant to was his whole nature and now also his profession. Isamu's
squad had been six, moving supply along the causeway between the second
and third islands; the rebels had let the first four past and taken the
two with the packs, quick and quiet and correct, and Isamu had been packs.
He had run for eleven hours once, at nineteen, on a bet, on that same
causeway. He had not got to run.

They gave the family a flag, and a pension line, and a sentence: *lost to
insurgent action in the course of resupply.* His mother had folded the
flag corners-in like a sheet, once, sharply, and put it in the chest under
the window, and it had never come out again.

And here was the thing Jirō carried about it, the thing he had never once
said straight in any room, because no room had ever asked the question in
a shape he could answer:

He did not hate the men on the causeway. He had tried. He knew their
trade; it was his trade in a mirror — they had traced, and waited, and
taken the packs, and left four alive who could have been six dead, which
was discipline, which was somebody's doctrine, which was *professional*.
Hating them was like hating weather that had been forecast.

What he hated — quietly, tidily, in the sorted way he kept everything now
— was the *arrangement*. The great settling arrangement that had stood
over his whole childhood like a roof: that took a laundry contract in an
afternoon; that streamed boys at eleven and spent them at twenty-three;
that put one friend of his in the ground for carrying packs and priced
the other one alive-only, premium terms, undamaged, like a fish that had
to reach the capital still swimming. The arrangement was nobody. He had
looked. That was the worst of it — he was a tracer, finding people was
the one thing he was rated best in his intake at, and he had spent four
years trying to trace the arrangement back to a person you could stand
in front of, and there was no person. There were only offices, and each
office was full of tired men saying it straight and stamping clean, and
the arrangement went on overhead like fog, which everyone in that
country had stopped seeing by the age of five.

So he hated it the way you hate fog. Generally. Uselessly. While
breathing it.

And the resistance said they were fighting the arrangement, and had
killed his brother, who was packs.

He did not have anywhere to put any of it. He was sixteen. He put it in
order instead, which was not the same thing, and drew his kit, and
polished his rate, and requested the corridor.

---

He went to see Mizuho the evening before the detail shipped.

She still had the same rooms behind the Academy annexe, and the same
slate wall, and she opened the door already talking — "if it's about the
autumn intake you can tell the office I said—" and then stopped, and
looked at him, at the kit-bag on his shoulder and the corridor flashes
on his sleeve, and said, "Ah," and stood aside.

She made tea the way she taught: fast, badly, with total authority.

"Aragane," she said, first, because she was Mizuho, and the dead came
first or not at all. "Your brother sat two years in that room and never
once stopped talking. I used to set the composition topics to whatever
he was talking about, to catch him out. Never worked. He'd talked about
everything." She put the cup in front of Jirō. "I was sorry. I wrote
your mother."

"She kept it. It's in the chest."

"Course it is." She sat, and looked at his sleeve, and her thumbs began
going, one over the other, slow, the way they had gone over certain
books in a room years deep in his memory. "External corridor."

"Yes, miss."

"Retrieval tracing."

"Yes, miss."

The thumbs went round. Somewhere in the annexe a floor was being swept
by somebody who would rather not be, at the exact tempo of a boy doing
lines.

"You were the one who couldn't sit still," said Mizuho at last. "Two
minutes of bowls in six years. I'd have bet the wage on you never making
chūnin theory, and you didn't, and it never mattered, because they've
built a whole trade now for boys who can't sit still, and given it a
mask, and called it looking." She drank her own tea, wincing at it on
principle. "And they've given you the file where you never have to sit
still again. Haven't they. That's what's on your sleeve. The corridor's
a coastline and the coastline's a name and there's only one name out of
that year worth a detail."

It was not a question, so he was not required to answer it, so the room
did what that country's rooms did, and held.

"I'll tell you two things," said Mizuho. "Not as your teacher. My
teaching's done; it took, or it didn't. As—" she looked for the word,
and discarded three, and settled, with distaste, on the true one —
"as staff.

"The first thing. In thirty-one years in that room I have had perhaps a
dozen come through that I'd call *made wrong for the place they were
born* — and I don't mean weak, and I don't mean soft; I mean built at an
angle to it, so that everything the place asks of them costs double and
everything it gives them fits badly. I've watched what becomes of that
kind. The place grinds the angle out of them, or the angle takes them
somewhere the place can't follow. There's no third thing. In all my
years I never once saw the third thing."

She turned the cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, precise as a lattice
peg.

"The second thing you already know, because I taught it to your whole
year in the spring assessments, and one boy in that room heard it so
completely that I've thought about the look on his face at odd moments
for four years." Her eyes came up, flat and level, schoolroom-grey, and
for a moment Jirō was nine, and it was raining, and the whole world was
a slate wall with five blobs on it. "*Don't round up.*

"You'll find things on that coast," she said. "Traces. Stories. A
mended hairpin. Whatever it is your trade finds. And you'll sit down at
night with a report to write, and there'll be boxes on it, and the
boxes will want feeding — boxes always want feeding, it's what they're
for. And every box you feed generously, every *probable* you write
where the truth was *possible*, every *confirmed* where the truth was
*likely* — that's rounding up, Jirō. Nobody who rounds up ever gets
past six. In the bowls it cost you a wet desk." The thumbs stopped.
"Out there it'll cost somebody the rest of their life. Write the number
you held. Not the number you wanted."

She stood, which meant it was over, because in her room it had always
meant it was over.

At the door, with the dark coming down the annexe lane and the kit-bag
back on his shoulder, Jirō turned — because he was his mother's son,
and something in him had to be said straight once before it went in a
chest forever:

"Miss. If I find him—"

"You'll write the number you held," said Mizuho. "That's all. That's
the whole of what I've got, and I'd give the same to the office if they
stood where you're standing, and you can tell them I said so." And then,
already shutting the door, in exactly the voice she had used a hundred
times to end a lesson that had gone somewhere the syllabus didn't cover:
"Mind the step. It's worked loose. Nobody fixes anything round here any
more."

He walked back through the lower district in the dark, past the third
bridge, where the stall was shut for the night — the stall was still
there; the woman still gave two for the price of one to boys in their
last Academy year, his youngest cousin reported, same as ever — and he
did not stop on the wall, because the wall was a place where his heels
had knocked next to another boy's heels four thousand years ago, and a
tracer learns early which ground costs more than the crossing of it is
worth.

The boats went out under him as he crossed, lamps at the mastheads,
into the black water between the islands where his brother had run for
eleven hours once, laughing, on a bet.

On the eighth, the detail shipped south.
