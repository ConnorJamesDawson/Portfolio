# Chapter 49, Scene 2 — The Tour

*Act 4, part D. Konoha General and the quartermaster's yard. Sazare
Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,991 words.*

---

He had the best morning he could remember.

That was the thing he wrote afterwards and did not soften, because it
was accurate and because everything that followed was easier to
understand if you had it first: he was, for about four hours on the
seventeenth of the eighth month, straightforwardly and completely
happy, and he did not notice a single thing that happened around him
in the whole of that time.

She asked questions.

That was all it was. She asked what the coloured bands on the charts
meant and he told her; she asked why the second floor smelled different
from the third and he told her that as well, at some length, including
the drainage; she asked what an orderly was for, which nobody had ever
asked him, and he found halfway through the answer that he did not
entirely know and had to go and ask one.

At about half past nine she said, "May I ask why you keep touching the
wall as you go round the corner?" and he stopped dead in the middle of
the corridor, because he had been doing it since the invasion and had
never once been aware of it, and the reason was that the corner had had
a barrier in it and his hand had learned the barrier's height.

He wrote that down standing up.

"That is four times this morning you have written something down
because I asked," said Katsuyu.

"Yes."

"I am not doing anything. I am sitting on a shoulder."

"You're asking," said Sazare. "Nobody asks. Emiko asks how I am, and
Yagi asks what the reading was, and Kanzaki asks what it weighs, and
every single one of those is a question about a thing on the table.
You've asked me eleven things this morning that were about *the day*
and I have got four entries out of them and one of them is about a
wall."

---

He was forty minutes into it before anybody stopped him and it should
have been four.

He had gone the length of the second floor, through the sluice, out
across the forecourt to the admissions desk and back up, and he had
been talking the whole way, and it did not occur to him at any point to
look at anybody's face.

Sister Kaburagi took him into the linen room off the fourth-floor
landing and shut the door.

"Sazare," she said. "What is that."

"That's Katsuyu."

"I know what it is. I have seen it before." Kaburagi had her hands
folded. She was fifty-nine and had run that ward for nineteen years and
he had never in three years heard her raise her voice, and she did not
raise it now. "That is why I have brought you in here. Do you know when
I last saw it?"

"...No."

"The third week after the Crush," said Kaburagi. "The Fifth had been in
this village nine days. We had four hundred still on the books and I
had six staff on a floor built for two hundred, and there had been no
new hands since the invasion because there were no new hands anywhere,
and I had stopped writing the ones who were going to die in the
observation column because there was no point."

Her voice did not change at all.

"And then that came through the door and went up onto the second bed on
the left, and every conscious person on this floor understood that the
Hokage had divided herself across the hospital because she had looked
at us and decided there were not enough of us."

Sazare stood in a linen room.

"How long did it stay?"

"Eleven days," said Kaburagi. "And you have walked it through the
second floor, the sluice, the forecourt and the admissions queue, at
nine o'clock in the morning, on a Tuesday."

"Nobody said—"

"Of course nobody said. What would they say?" She let that sit for
about two seconds. "That thing does not mean *a slug*. On this floor it
means the Fifth has looked at what is coming and decided we cannot
carry it. Ōtaki Sue is seventy-four and has been in bed eleven days and
she saw it go past the door at twenty past eight and has been asking
every four minutes what has happened. Tomioka in the side room started
crying and would not say why and I know exactly why. Two of my
orderlies have gone to find out what is coming in."

She paused.

"And one of them has started clearing beds."

---

He sat down on a laundry hamper, which he had never done in that
building.

"I have made a mistake," he said.

"You have made an ordinary mistake in an extraordinary way, which is a
thing you do about twice a year and which I have written down twice."
Kaburagi unfolded her hands. "You have carried a signal through a
hospital. That object does not mean *a slug*. It means the Hokage has
divided herself across this village, and there is exactly one reason
that has ever happened here, and everybody on my floor over the age of
about eleven learned that reason on the seventh of the ninth month."

"I'll go to the wards. I'll tell them—"

"You will not go to the wards and tell forty-one people anything,
because you will tell them accurately and at length and it will take
you until two o'clock." Kaburagi opened the linen room door. "I will do
it. It is four sentences and it is my floor. You are going to go
somewhere that is not a hospital, and you are going to think about what
else that thing is going to mean to people who see it on your shoulder,
and I would suggest you start with the ones who are not frightened of
it."

"Which ones aren't?"

"Ask me tomorrow," said Sister Kaburagi, "and I will tell you, and you
will not like it."

---

Kanzaki did not care at all, which was worth about four hours of the
morning by itself.

She looked up when he came through the yard gate, looked at his
shoulder for approximately one second, said "Right," and went back to
the day book.

Then, after about a minute: "Does it want anything?"

"I do not eat," said Katsuyu.

"Good, because there's nothing." Kanzaki turned a page. "You're on
bench three. And Sazare — I've had the light business back off the west
wall watch, they've logged it three more times through the sacking and
one of them's put *reduced*, which is the first competent thing anybody
in that detail has done all year, so whatever you're at, carry on."

He worked at bench three from about eleven.

He laid four hundred articles that afternoon.

That was not an exaggeration and he counted them, because he had gone
in with the idea of establishing what she could and could not see and
had realised inside twenty minutes that the answer depended on what he
did, which meant a sweep, which meant volume. Four grades of stock. Four
speeds. Seeded and unseeded. Big, small, deep courses and shallow. Every
combination he could get through in five hours with one hand at eighty
per cent.

Katsuyu sat on the corner of the bench and watched every one of them
and did not once say she was tired, and it was somewhere around the
two hundredth that he stopped thinking of her as an instrument.

---

She gave him two things and she gave him the first one at about the
fortieth article, unprompted, in the middle of him weighing something.

"That is not the same," she said.

"Not the same as what?"

"As the one on the fifteenth. The one that hurt you." A slow settling.
"Tsunade-sama described it to me while she was working, at about the
third hour, and I did not understand it then and I understand it now
that I have seen this, and they are not the same thing at all."

Sazare put the balance down.

"Say more."

"This takes a *lump*," said Katsuyu. "Forgive me, I have no better
word. It reaches, and it takes an amount, and then it stops, and the
stopping is extremely definite. Like a mouthful. It is over. There is
nothing going on around that object now and there will not be."

"And the rose?"

"The rose was drinking. It was not a mouthful. It was open, and it went
on being open, the way a root is open, and it would have gone on until
it was stopped or until there was nothing left where it was standing."

Sazare sat quite still on a stool in a yard.

*Confirmed from outside, on the second day, by observation. Not
inference. Not my arithmetic checked by my own arithmetic.*

*Six days is a mouthful running out.*

"Katsuyu," he said, and heard his own voice go somewhere slightly
wrong and did not correct it, "can you see the stopping?"

"See it?"

"The moment it ends. You've just called it extremely definite — is that
something you *watched*, or is it something you worked out afterwards
because nothing was happening?"

"I watched it."

"You can see me close it."

"Yes," said Katsuyu, mildly. "It is the clearest part. It is by some
distance the clearest thing that happens."

---

He did not say anything for about nine seconds.

"Have I said something wrong?"

"No." He put both hands flat on the bench. "I have done that nine
hundred thousand times since I was seven years old and I have never
been conscious of doing it once, and I found out it existed at half
past ten on Friday night when it failed to happen, and I have spent two
days knowing that it is the reason nothing I build lasts and having no
possible way to work on it."

"Why can you not work on it?"

"Because I can't see it. It's a reflex. It fires before I decide and I
have no report of it at all — I would be trying to change a thing I
cannot detect, in the dark, alone, and the only way I would ever know
whether I'd done it is if I stopped being able to move my hand."

"Ah," said Katsuyu.

"Yes."

"And now there is somebody who can tell you," she said.

"And now there's somebody who can tell me."

Katsuyu shifted forward slightly on the corner of the bench.

"Then may I say a thing which I think is obvious and which I am worried
is not," she said, "because I have found this morning that a great
number of things I assumed were obvious are apparently not said."

"Go on."

"You have been doing this alone since you were seven."

"Yes."

"That is the whole of it," said Katsuyu. "That is not an observation
about your character. I have watched four hundred of these today and
every one of them was done extremely well and about a third of them
were done in a way that made me think *that is a peculiar way to do
that*, and it is peculiar because there was never anybody standing
beside you to say *why like that*, and so you did it the way you first
found and then you did it four hundred thousand more times."

Sazare looked at the four hundred articles on the felt.

"Kanzaki said something like that in the fifth month," he said. "She
said I mill wrong and anneal wrong and do a two-man lift on my own by
splitting the load, and that every one of those is worse than the
ordinary way in nine conditions and better in the tenth, and that I
keep getting the tenth."

"She is a very sensible person," said Katsuyu. "I have been sitting
here for five hours and I would put it at eleven conditions."
