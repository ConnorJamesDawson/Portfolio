# Chapter 10, Scene 1 — Rice

*Act 2, part B. Sazare Ruri, age thirteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,300 words.*

---

For the first month Tōan did not touch the arm at all, and the house ran on
three laws, which were posted nowhere and enforced absolutely.

The first law was rice. It went on at dawn and it did not come off, and anyone
in the house ate when the body asked, not when the clock allowed, which Sazare
did not believe for the first week — he kept his old arithmetic going out of
habit, eating half and banking half against the evening — until Tōan, without
comment, without even pausing on his way through the kitchen, took the banked
half off him and replaced it with a full bowl and kept walking.

"I wasn't—"

"You were rationing," said Tōan, from the next room. "This house doesn't run
short. Your body doesn't know that yet. It'll learn it the same way it learns
everything, which is by it being true for a long time."

The second law was work. Everybody in the house worked, sick or well, at
whatever the body in question could honestly do, and the word *honestly* was
carrying a great deal of weight in that sentence and was meant to. Sazare had
the woodpile, one-handed, and the herb beds, and the copper — there was always
copper to clean in a treatment house, basins and instruments and the great
kettle — and he was not thanked for any of it and it was not made light for
him, and within a fortnight he understood that this was a kindness so
precisely judged that it must have been made to measure.

The third law he never heard stated and never saw broken: nobody in that house
asked anybody how they had come to be there.

Patients came up the road every day. Miners from the workings over the second
saddle, with crushed fingers and quartz-cut hands and the cough. Old people
from the town, in ones and twos, for their joints and their hearts and, as far
as Sazare could tell, for the talk. A boy his own age brought down from the
timber camps with a leg the wrong shape, who stayed nine days in the back room
and howled the first two and played cards with Sazare the last four, cheating
placidly and without ambition, and went home on a crutch calling Tōan a robber,
which appeared to please Tōan more than thanks would have.

They came in broken and they left mended or mending, and no one — not once,
not ever — was asked for the story of how. The leg was set without an account
of the fall. The cough was treated without a sermon on the mine. It was the
exact opposite of a ledger: a counter where the only column that existed was
*what needs doing now*, and Sazare, who had been written down by every
institution that ever looked at him, watched it operate for a month with a
feeling he could not put anywhere.

---

The first time he saw it done properly, it was a hand.

A miner had come down off the saddle at midday, walking, which Sazare learned
later was its own bad sign — the ones who walked in holding an arm had already
decided the news was survivable, and were sometimes wrong. This one's hand had
been between a cart and a rock face two hours earlier. It was wrapped in
somebody's shirt, and the man was grey and pleasant and making conversation
about the road, at which Hachirō would have been proud of him, and Tōan took
one look and put down what he was carrying.

Sazare fetched water, and boiled instruments, and then stood where he was told
with a lamp, because the porch light was going.

What he expected — Kiri had medics; he had seen the green shimmer once across
a training field, distant, like weather — was a light that fixed things.

What he saw instead was a man reading.

Tōan unwrapped the hand and held it in both of his and went still, and his
stillness was a working stillness, forearms quiet, breath low — and Sazare's
whole spine recognised it before his mind caught up, because he had grown up
watching that exact quality of attention pointed at a tray of sand at four in
the morning. Reading where it's moving. Not doing anything about it yet.

"Second and third are shattered," Tōan said, to the miner, conversationally.
"Fourth's cracked and lying to you. The little one's whole, which I'd not have
put money on. You'll keep all five."

"Keep them working?"

"Keep them working. Not this season." And *then* the light — not a wash of it,
nothing like weather; a thread. Precise as a nib. It went where his fingers
went, and what it did, as far as Sazare could see, was persuade: bleeding
slowed as if it had been reasoned with, swelling stood down, and twice Tōan
stopped entirely, waited, watched the hand as though listening to it argue,
and resumed somewhere else.

The bones he set by hand, with his thumbs, one at a time.

It took two hours. The chakra was perhaps ten minutes of the two, laid in
threads at moments Sazare could not predict, and everything between was cloth
and wood and hot water and thumbs. At the end the hand was splinted flat as a
board, and the miner was the colour of a person again and asking whether he
could smoke, and Tōan said not in here, and the whole enormous ordinary
transaction was over.

Sazare held the lamp until told otherwise.

"You'll have questions," said Tōan, cleaning down the bench. It was not an
invitation; it was a diagnosis.

"The light. It's not what does it."

Tōan glanced back at him, once, the way you check a hand of cards.

"Ninety in a hundred people who watch that," he said, "ask what the light was.
Go on."

"The light was — small. You barely used it. Everything was where your thumbs
went, and where you *waited*. You kept waiting. You'd stop and let it—" Sazare
did not have the word and used the one he had. "You let it settle. Like it was
coming up too fast and you were holding it back."

The bench got cleaner for a while.

"The body mends itself," said Tōan at last. "That's the whole of it. That's
the trade secret. It knows how, it's been doing it since before it had a
brain, and it will do it wrong in a tearing hurry if you let it — scar's the
body doing it wrong in a hurry. All I do is slow the parts that panic and feed
the parts that build. The light's a word in its ear. The medicine—" he hung the
cloth on the rail — "the medicine is knowing which word, and when, and mostly
it's knowing how to wait. Any fool can pour. Pouring in more is nearly always
the wrong—"

He stopped, and finished the sentence somewhere private, and said instead:

"Wood's low."

"There's a whole—"

"Wood's low," said Tōan, and Sazare went and split wood, one-handed, in the
dark, with his ears roaring — because he was thirteen and could not have
said any of it out loud, but he had heard the sentence land somewhere in the
middle of him with the click of a well-cut joint: *the whole discipline is a
person standing between a body and its own speed, saying not yet.*

He had been raised inside a version of it. His mother had done it to sand.
Tōan did it to men.

And whatever had come out of his arm in the fish market square — eleven yards
of it, flawless, in a fifth of a second — had been the other thing. The
poured thing. The thing you got when nobody was standing between.

---

The nightmares did not care about any of the laws.

They came the same as on the boat — the platform, the flat hand, the
rearranging — three nights, four nights out of seven, and he woke in the dark
of the back room with his heart going and lay still by force until it passed,
because the walls were board and he would be heard.

He was heard anyway.

Nothing was ever said in daylight, and this too he understood to be one of the
house's laws in operation. But in the second week his bed was nearer the
stove when he came in from the woodpile — moved while he was out, no comment,
half a room's difference in the cold — and there was a second blanket on it of
rough undyed wool.

And on a night in the fifth week, surfacing at the third hour with the dream
still on him, he heard from the front of the house the sounds of a man who was
not asleep either, and had not tried to be: the small chime of the good
scales, the tap of a spoon on a jar's rim, the creak of the bench. Tōan,
compounding, at three in the morning, unhurried, on the other side of a board
wall. Working.

Not for him. He was almost sure it was not for him. The house was full of
orders to fill and the man kept strange hours.

But it was a sound with a person in it, on the other side of the dark, and it
was the exact opposite of fourteen frames of silence, and Sazare lay in the
warm listening to somebody measure things until the dream let go of the back
of his neck, and slept, and this happened more than once, and neither of them
ever mentioned it, and it was years before it occurred to him to wonder what
orders a village of that size could possibly generate that needed filling at
three in the morning four nights out of seven.
