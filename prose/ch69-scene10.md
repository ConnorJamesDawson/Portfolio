# Chapter 69, Scene 10 — Four Evenings

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1440 words.*

---

The brush took him four evenings, worked at the yard bench after
the light went, and he understood by the second evening that the
four evenings were not incidental to the object. They were part
of its specification.

The month had grown large around him — a trademark, a category, a
waiting list with a different first digit, a board with standing
orders — and every article he had touched since the docket
belonged to a system: a cohort, a spec sheet, a queue. The brush
was the counterweight, and he built it as one, deliberately, at
the pace of the thing it was for: a population of exactly one, no
docket, no channel, nothing due. Fifty passes a night had taught
him what unhurried was worth. The bench, four evenings running,
taught it back.

**The first evening** was the data, and the data was the best set
he owned: twelve nights of passes logged through a support hand
that had turned out to remember everything, cross-read against
the calibration rig's numbers, which the file would note was
precisely the class of secondary use the fitting data had never
been docketed for, and which the fabricator, reviewing the
ethics of it at the bench, ruled admissible on the grounds that
the entire data set concerned a woman who had appointed herself
lead model *for life* and would audit the brush harder than any
board.

The fault list wrote itself. The old boar-bristle lost the
fiftieth pass to static — her hair banked the winter's charge
like a stored asset and the bristle paid it back in crackle and
cling, so the last passes of every evening undid a fraction of
what the middle ones built. The teeth were uniform where her
hair was not: fine at the temple, heavy at the crown, a
different material at the nape, and one gauge of tooth serving
all four the way one bandage serves four wounds, which is to
say at an average nobody actually was. And the handle was built
for no hand at all — a lathe-turned cylinder, the universal
grip, ownerless as a doorknob.

*Specification,* he wrote, at the bottom of the fault list.
*Drain the charge. Grade the teeth. Build the grip for the one
hand that will hold it. Nothing on the article exists to be
admired; everything on it exists to work. Which is the shop's
whole doctrine, and the shop would note that those two
categories were never as separate as this village believes.*

**The second evening** was the static, and the static was an old
friend wearing a new problem.

Crystal drained charge. That was not engineering; that was the
first property of the material he had ever known — before he
knew it was a property, before he had the word, standing at the
end of the bench in the dry cold of a Kiri winter watching every
surface in the shop spit and cling and his mother's frames sit
quiet as pooled water. The wool of your sleeve crackled. The cat
would not cross the workroom. The frames did not care. He had
asked — of course he had asked; the whole of his life was in the
question — and his mother had said, not looking up from the
wire, *the stone drinks it*, which he had held for eleven years
as a mother's answer and could now certify, at eighteen, on a
Corps bench, as materially accurate to four decimal places. The
stone drinks it.

So the brush-back was one open article, self-keeping, drawing
its trickle of upkeep from the air like the stones at Tarugi,
and bleeding her hair's banked charge off through the teeth as
it went, so the fiftieth pass would lay down as smooth as the
fifth. He ran the numbers twice and then a third time for the
pleasure of them. The draw was below Akimoto's silence floor by
two orders. The article would hum to nobody, glow for nobody,
and work for one.

**The third evening** was the teeth, and the teeth were the real
work, and the real work went wrong the correct number of times.

Forty-one of them, individually grown — not cast in a row, grown
one at a time, each to its own station on the map twelve nights
of passes had drawn: soft-graded at the rim where the fine
temple hair ran, firming toward centre for the crown's weight,
a shorter stiffer rank at the trailing edge for the nape. Each
tip finished round under the loupe to the standard he had last
used on articles that went inside people, because scalp was
skin and the loupe did not care whose.

Rejects: six. Two off-grade, one with a boundary flaw the
fingertip caught and the eye had flattered — *the eye flatters,
the finger audits*; her sentence, at her bench, doing her work
— and three sound but wrong, correct to specification and
somehow not right in the hand, which was a rejection category
no Corps form recognised and every jeweller who ever lived
would have signed without reading. Into the reject bin, logged,
numbered, blamed on nobody. The bin had four hundred rows of
company.

The handle he grew last, off the rig's numbers: her grip, not
his — the geometry of the hand that had signed his governor
and healed his nosebleed and gripped his shirt through sixty
nights, translated into a shape that would meet it like a
made thing meets its jig. And through the whole of it, the
one small constant engineering: hand-heat. The article held
itself at the temperature of a warm palm, always, at the cost
of a fraction of its draw, so that picking it up on a January
evening would feel less like lifting a tool and more like
resuming a held hand.

He surveyed the finished instrument at the end of the third
evening, complete on the cloth, every line of the
specification met, and found that it was not finished, and
sat with that finding for a while, because the lack was not
on the fault list and had no gauge.

**The fourth evening he did not build anything.** He sat at
the bench with the finished brush on the cloth and a graver
he had owned for six years and never used, and had the
conversation with himself that nine hundred articles had been
built to avoid.

*The shop marked its work. Every trade does. The trestle
pieces took the clan stamp — four hundred years of it,
gemstone-certified, the mark buyers paid for. But the keepers
— the pieces that never went on the trestle, that my mother
made for the house, for her sister, for the drawer that was
not for sale — the keepers took her own mark. The
keeper-knot. A cutting of three strokes with a fourth laid
under, and the grammar of it, which she told me once, at the
bench, as a thing every child of the house learned: the
family mark says a Ruri made this. The keeper-knot says a
Ruri made this and it is not for the world. And a child of
the house, when their hands were ready, signed keeper-work
with the knot simplified by one stroke, which said: in the
family style, and my own.*

*I have been ready for eleven years. I have made nine hundred
articles and marked none of them, and I have told myself it
was because they belonged to their specifications, and that
was true, and underneath the truth was the other thing: a
mark is a claim, and a claim says I am still here, and the
knot in particular says the house still stands — and I could
not cut it, because cutting it alone, for no one, in a
lodging over a persimmon tree, would have been the loneliest
act performed on this continent since the water took the
shop.*

*It is not for no one now.*

He cut the mark small, on the underside of the handle's heel,
where a thumb falls when the grip is the resting grip and
nowhere else — invisible to every eye, found by exactly one
hand, exactly where that hand would settle on ten thousand
evenings. Three strokes, a fourth laid under, one stroke
simplified. It took him ninety seconds, and eleven years, and
when it was done he sat looking at it in the lamplight for a
long time, the first mark of his life on the nine hundred and
first article, and the yard was very quiet, and somewhere in
the quiet the shop over the water had one more piece of
keeper-work in the world, signed by the child of the house,
whose hands were ready.
