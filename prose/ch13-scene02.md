# Chapter 13, Scene 2 — The Reason

*Act 2, part D. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,100 words.*

---

He did the work in three days exactly, with the door open, in plain sight of
the road, with cutting tools only.

It was, he judged afterwards, the best lapidary work of his life, and the
whole art of it was in what it refused. The chip came back to a clean facet;
the balancing facet went on opposite, mirror-true; and at every hour of it
the growing stood at his shoulder like a door he was declining to notice —
four hours, seamless, *whole*, his mother's grade, the thing the piece
deserved — and he polished on past it with the patient mediocrity of a
part-trained tradesman, and made the repair a good country jeweller would
have made, and not one stroke better.

The man called Yashiro collected it on the fourth morning, and turned it in
the light, and looked at the two honest facets for slightly longer than a
satisfied customer looks.

"Workmanlike," he said, pleasantly.

"It's what there was," said Sazare.

"Indeed. My patron will be—" the pause was a hair's width, an instrument
being set down — "consoled." He paid the balance, and over-paid it by the
same margin as before, exact as a scale-weight; and then, folding the silk,
not looking up, in the tone of a man remarking on the road's condition:

"He maintains an interest in the old trades, my patron. The dying arts.
He funds their study — generously, I'm bound to say; workshops, materials,
whatever the work wants. It grieves him, the thought of some craft four
centuries deep going out of the world because the last hands that hold it
are mending hairpins up a mountain." The silk went into his breast, and
the smile came out, exactly enough. "No — nothing to say to it. Why would
there be. But we travel this coast in the spring and autumn, and I find
the baths here suit me, and a thing worth saying twice is worth waiting
to say. Good morning, stone-mender."

He went down the road at his easy pace, and paused at the bend to admire
the gorge, and did not look back, and was gone.

Sazare sat on the porch for a while.

Then he cleaned the bench, and oiled the tools, and levelled the tray on
the rail out of pure habit, and walked down into the town and up the lane
behind the new bathhouse, and found Tōan at the herb beds, and said:

"He'll be back in the autumn. He knew the word. He knew the work. He was
never once unkind, and he paid twice over, and he told me — without asking
me for anything — that there's a patron who funds the old trades, with
workshops, and materials, and whatever the work wants."

Tōan straightened up slowly, the way his back made him, and looked at
Sazare for a long moment, and there was nothing in his face at all, which
from Tōan was the sound of an alarm.

"That's the reason, then," he said.

"That's the reason."

"Autumn." Tōan pulled off the working gloves, finger by finger. "Then
we've the summer, and we'll spend it properly. You'll go planned, and
you'll go provisioned, and you'll go the direction he didn't come from."
He looked down the valley, at the steam standing over his town, and his
jaw worked once. "Polite," he said, mostly to himself. "Twenty-two years,
and it walks up the road *polite*."

---

The summer was the finishing school, and both his teachers taught like men
laying up stores.

Tōan built the roll. It grew on the compounding bench across two months,
piece by piece — needles, silk, the burn salve, the fever barks sorted
and labelled in his upright hand, a lancet Sazare was made to strop until
it passed the wet-hair test — and every item went in with a lecture
attached, and every lecture was a year of practice pressed flat: *this
one's for wounds you'll see; this one's for the wound you'll be; this
one you'll trade away someday to somebody who needs it worse, so know
its price.* The finished thing rolled to the size of a forearm and
weighed nothing and held, Sazare understood, the whole portable fraction
of twenty-two years.

"You're no medic," Tōan said, tying it off. "Understand that. You're a
tradesman who can keep a man alive till the medic comes. That's a real
thing, it has a name in the field, and it's worth more than it sounds —
most dying is done waiting." He put the roll in Sazare's hands. "A body
mends from the middle out. Eat first. Every time. There's no wound so
urgent you'll dress it better fainting."

Sakai's stores were stranger, and came at first light, in the yard, in
the last month.

"Doctrine's yours; the cut's as straight as it's getting; footwork's
honest. So: the leavings." And the leavings were a curriculum no school
would own — how to wear a blade so a checkpoint reads *labourer's tool*
and a robber reads *don't*; how to sit in a common room (back angle,
door line, which stranger to buy a drink and which to owe one); how to
lose a fight in front of witnesses so that it ends there ("winning makes
appointments"); how to read a road's traffic the way Hachirō read its
camber, for what had passed and what was waiting.

"You people," Sakai said, on the last morning — he had taken to *you
people* lately, meaning, as far as Sazare could tell, the whole
fog-ridden nation of his birth — "you people train for the enemy in the
dark, and he exists, and I hope he chokes. But the road will try to kill
you far oftener than the dark will, and the road's weapons are wet feet,
bad water, short sleep, and company you didn't choose. Boring deaths.
The world is *full* of boring deaths, boy. I have taught you everything
I know about refusing the interesting ones. Refuse the boring ones too
and you'll bury us all."

He held out the bokken — the third one, the one Sazare had cut to the
wood's own say-so, two years worn now to the shape of his hands.

"Take your own work. Leave mine."

"Sakai—"

"No speeches. I've a door to keep." But at the yard gate, with the mist
coming up off the gorge, the old man stopped, and stood a moment with
his back turned and his hands, for once, not quite still. And he said,
in the formal voice, with the rust on it, in the accent of another
country — the voice Sazare had heard once through a woodshed wall at
the year's last bell, and was not supposed to know existed:

"Kept, this year also."

And went in, and shut the gate, and did not look out again.

---

Gonta cried, furiously, exactly the way Jirō had, and threatened to
follow him down the coast, and was talked out of it by the alarming
discovery that he did not actually want to go — that the hitch, the
gang, the beer he never collected, the girl at the chestnut stall he
had been failing to speak to for a year, had become, when he wasn't
looking, a life. He shoved a fish-cloth of food into Sazare's chest at
the ford, hard, with both hands.

"You're the worst cheat I ever taught," he said, wetly. "You never
once used it on money."

"You never taught me. I corrected your thumb."

"*Details*," bawled Gonta, and hugged him, and stumped away up the
new-cut road without looking round, exactly like his predecessor, and
Sazare stood at the ford with the cloth in his arms and let himself
have the whole of it, both boys, four years apart, the parallel and
the ache, because Tose's country had taught him that you let these
things through the door when they knock, or they come back at the
third hour with keys of their own.
