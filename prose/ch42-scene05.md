# Chapter 42, Scene 5 — Of Inquiry

*Act 4, part D. Konoha General, the fourth-floor landing. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,836 words.*

---

He noticed it on the eighteenth and he noticed it the way he noticed
everything, which was at the bottom of the ladder and by accident.

The third-floor stair was the short one. It came up off the west
corridor two flights from the apprentices' room and put you out forty
feet from the sluice, and every person on the medical side of that
building used it, and had used it since before either of them arrived,
and it was the reason the tread on the third landing was worn through
to the boards in a half-moon and the fourth-floor tread was not.

Sakura had come up the east stair every morning for a fortnight.

She had come up the east stair, he was fairly sure, for considerably
longer than a fortnight, because the fourth-floor landing had been
where he found her at half past seven on days he had no reason to be
there, going back into the previous year, and he had filed that under
*route* and never once asked what it was a route away from.

Four flights. Every morning. Past the apprentices' room and up the far
side of the building.

He worked out the second rung on the stairs and the third rung on the
landing, and he did not work out the fourth, because he had been told
by a man in a mask for a year that the fourth rung was where he
belonged least.

So he asked.

"Why do you come up the east stair?"

---

Sakura had the paper twist in one hand and the marking sheet in the
other and she stopped with both of them raised slightly, like a person
interrupted while setting something down.

"What?"

"You come up four flights on the east side. The short stair puts you
out forty feet from where you're going and everybody uses it, which is
why the third-floor tread is worn through and this one isn't." He kept
his hands where they were. "You've done it every morning for a
fortnight and I think you've done it for longer, and I filed it as a
route, and I've been looking at it since the second landing and I can't
get past what it's a route away from."

Sakura looked at the sheet in her hand.

Then she folded it, which she had done every morning for a fortnight,
and he saw that the fold had gone from three to four at some point and
that the creases had gone soft and furred where a satchel had worked
at them.

"The apprentices' room is on the third floor," she said.

"Yes."

"That's my year." She sat down on the top step, which she had not done
before. "Ino's in there. Everybody I came up with. Nine of us started
the medical stream the same month and eight of them are in that room
and I'm on the fourth floor with the Hokage."

Sazare said nothing.

He said nothing for what he estimated at eleven seconds, which was
longer than the nine that had broken him on the fourth of the month,
and it did not break him this time, and he did not entirely understand
why except that this silence had a person in it rather than an absence.

"Nobody was horrible," said Sakura.

"No."

"That's the thing I can't explain to anybody, and I've tried twice, and
both times the person I was telling said *oh, are they jealous?* and I
had to say no, and then the conversation was over, because there wasn't
anywhere else for it to go." She turned the twist of paper over without
opening it. "They were pleased. Genuinely. Ino hugged me in the
corridor and cried and she meant it, she absolutely meant it, and that
is not a thing you can be angry about."

"What stopped."

"The asking." She said it fast. "Nobody stopped talking to me. They
stopped *asking* me. Everything's a handover now — how's it going,
what's she teaching you, is it hard — and they're all real questions
and they're all about the apprenticeship, and not one of them has been
about me since the eleventh month, and I don't think a single one of
them has noticed, and I would have to be an absolute monster to bring
it up, because what would I even say? *You're all being extremely nice
to me and I hate it?*"

"That's chapter three," said Sazare.

"What?"

"They've stopped doing chapter three at you. And you can't complain
about the absence of a thing nobody knows is a thing, because the
absence doesn't have a name, which is the entire reason a
two-hundred-year-old manual has eleven pages on it." He stopped. "I'm
doing it again."

"Doing what?"

"Turning it into a finding. Ignore the last thirty seconds."

"No," said Sakura, "keep it, that one was useful," and she wiped her
face with the back of the hand holding the paper twist, briskly, in the
manner of somebody who had done it before in a stairwell and had a
system.

---

He got it wrong four minutes later.

He asked whether she had considered saying it to Ino directly, which
was a solution, and he heard it become a solution somewhere around the
fifth word and could not stop the sentence, and Sakura's face closed
about a quarter of the way.

"I don't want it fixed."

"No. That was—" He put both hands flat on the rail. "That was criterion
one. I supplied a cargo. You'd given me something and I was carrying it
somewhere useful, because carrying things somewhere useful is what I
do, and it's what I do to Yagi and it's what I do to Handa and I did it
to the Hokage in an office at the eleventh hour and she put a stack of
paper on the floor rather than let me."

"...She did what?"

"That's not the point I'm making."

"It's a *bit* of a point—"

"Ask me another time," said Sazare, "because if you ask me now I will
tell you the whole of it and it will take nine minutes and it will be
about me."

Sakura shut her mouth.

Then she opened it again and said, quietly, "That was the best thing
you've said in three weeks," and did not score it, and he understood
that not scoring it was the score.

---

She told him the rest of it on her own, which the book said was the
only way it ever came.

"The bit I don't say," she said, "is that I don't know why she picked
me."

"Control."

"That's what everyone says. That's what *she* says, when she says
anything, which is about once a season." Sakura had her chin on her
knees now, which made her look about eleven and which he had the sense
not to remark on. "But she'd seen my control for four days. Four days.
And there were two others in that intake with control inside a hair of
mine and one of them is better than me at it now, honestly better, and
she's on the third floor. So either the control was the reason and she
got it slightly wrong, which — she doesn't get that wrong. Or the
control wasn't the reason."

"And you can't ask."

"I can't ask, because there are only two answers and one of them ruins
it." She said it to her knees. "If she says *the control*, then I'm a
technique she picked up, and I'll spend the rest of my life being the
best pair of hands in a room and knowing that's the whole of it. And if
she says anything else — anything at all — then I'll never know if she
said it because it was true or because I'd asked her a question with
one obvious kind answer in it."

Sazare stood on the landing in the bar of light.

"I have that," he said.

"You have what?"

"That exact question. From the other end." He was not sure, as he
started, that he was going to finish, and finished anyway. "I've built
a fourteen-month table and burned three designs and had two entire
evenings I'd rather not describe, all of them circling the same
question, which is whether the thing anybody in this village values is
me or the material I came with. And it has the same shape as yours.
Two answers, and one of them ruins it, and asking makes the good answer
unusable. I've constructed it twice and refused it twice and I have
written *unresolved by design* in a notebook and meant it as a
decision, and I don't think it is one, I think it's a wall I keep
walking into and re-labelling."

Sakura lifted her head off her knees.

"Nobody else has that," she said.

"No."

"That's — I've had that since the eleventh month and I have not been
able to say it to one single person, because everybody I know who'd
understand the shape of it is somebody it's *about*."

"Yes."

"And you've had it for how long?"

"Eleven years, in one form or another. Two, in this one."

They stood and sat, respectively, in a stairwell smelling of hot water
and sterilising fluid, while the trolley went over the join in the
corridor tiles below, twice, the way it always did.

"Right," said Sakura, eventually. "So that's three."

"What's three?"

"You said there were two people who could hold the whole of what you
do, and you wanted three. That's not what this is — I can't hold the
material half, I've read your degradation study and I understood about
a fifth of it." She stood up and brushed the step off the back of her
skirt. "But there's exactly two people in this village with the *other*
thing, and I'd got to one, and now I've got to two, and that's what I'm
counting."

---

She asked about the study on the way down.

He explained it — six practitioners, matched injuries, half of them
working in silence, a review board of eleven and between four and nine
months — and he explained the objection Tsunade had made to it, which
was that a board would ask him why he was withholding comfort from a
bleeding man and would be right to ask.

"I want to be one of the silent ones," said Sakura.

"It's the unpleasant half."

"I know."

"Why?"

She stopped at the second-floor turn.

"Because it's a job where nobody in that building can be nice to me,"
she said. "I'd like one of those. I haven't had one in two years."

Sazare thought about it for the length of a flight of stairs.

"I'll put it to Yagi," he said. "Not to Tsunade. If it comes from me to
Tsunade it arrives as a favour and it stops being yours."

"...Thank you."

"That's criterion four," he said. "I'm going to stop now."

And he did.
