# Chapter 39, Scene 6 — Information

*Act 4, part D. Konoha General. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen. POV:
Sazare. ~1,459 words.*

---

It reached the second floor before he did.

He was on at six and he came in at ten to, and the auxiliary at the
desk — three weeks in, the one who noticed things and reported them to
Emiko — went the colour of a beetroot and said "Morning, Ruri-san" at a
pitch about a third above her normal one and then dropped a pen.

He did not work it out for nine minutes.

What he worked out at nine minutes, standing at the sluice with his
hands under the tap, was that of the eleven people in that room, four
were hospital staff, one was married to a man in the liaison office,
one had a stall on the market row that fed about four hundred people a
day, and one was a seventy-two-year-old woman who could not hear a
word and wrote everything down.

*I have been reading distribution networks since I was fifteen.*

*I built one out of counters and pegs across four towns and it never
failed once.*

*And I sat in a room with the single most efficient one in the Land of
Fire and did not identify it until this morning.*

---

The forms it took were various and he catalogued all of them, because
he could not stop, and because cataloguing it was the only thing
standing between him and the other available reaction.

**Nurse Tomoe** said nothing at all and was extremely kind to him for
four days, which was worse than anything anybody said.

**The two orderlies** stopped talking when he came round the corner on
the Tuesday, which is the only unambiguous signal in the whole of human
behaviour and which he had been reading correctly since he was nine.

**Ashida** did not mention it for six days and then, at the gate, said,
"So," and then, after about four seconds, "no," and put his pencil
away, and Sazare had never in three years seen the man decline to
finish a sentence.

**Anko** intercepted him at the yard wall on the Thursday and said,
without any preamble whatsoever: "Well."

"Don't."

"I'm not going to say anything."

"You've come across a yard to not say anything."

"I've come across a yard to be *present*," said Anko Mitarashi, "which
is chapter one, and I'd like it noted that I've read your book." She
put the senbon in her mouth. "Fourteen months of Thursdays."

"That isn't—"

"Fourteen months, every week, one table, and you've told me about it
four separate times without once noticing you were telling me
anything." She pushed off the wall. "I'm the last person in this
village who's going to give you a hard time about who you drink with,
and I'm also not going to stand here and let you tell me it's a
professional arrangement, because I've been lied to by the best and you
are extremely bad at it."

"It *is*—"

"Same time in a fortnight," said Anko, and went.

**O-Shige** gave him two extra without being asked and said, "You
should eat more," in a voice of enormous significance, and did not
explain.

**Ōtaki Sue** wrote on the slate:

*I SAW*

*I AM NOT GOING TO SAY ANYTHING ABOUT IT*

*I AM 72 AND I HAVE EARNED THE RIGHT TO WRITE THIS DOWN AND THEN NOT
SAY ANYTHING ABOUT IT*

And then, underneath, after he had read it:

*SHE PUT HER HAND ON THE BACK OF YOUR HEAD*

*THAT IS NOT NOTHING*

*I AM NOT SAYING ANYTHING*

---

Sakura found him on the stairs on the Friday and did the only thing
anybody did that week that actually helped.

"Right," she said. "I'm going to say one thing and then I'm never going
to mention it again, and I want you to let me get to the end of it."

"All right."

"Everybody in that room likes you and nobody in that room is laughing
at you." She had gone slightly pink and was doing it anyway, which he
had learned in three months was simply how she operated. "That's it.
That's the whole thing. I know what you'll have done with it, because
I've watched you do it with about four other things — you'll have taken
it apart and worked out that eleven people saw something you couldn't
control and that they've all got a piece of you now that you didn't
authorise."

Sazare stood on the stairs.

"That's exactly what I did."

"I know. And it's wrong, and it's the same wrong you were about me not
remembering you." She shifted the tray. "They've got a piece of you. So
what. That's what having people *is*. You've had eleven pieces of them
for two years — you've got Yagi's ear and Emiko's scissors and
Handa's daughter who writes twice a year — and none of them have gone
round afterwards worrying about what you were going to do with it,
because it did not occur to them that anybody would do anything with
it."

"That isn't the same. They chose to give me theirs."

"Yes," said Sakura, "it is exactly the same, because you *asked* and
they *answered*, and the answering wasn't controlled either. Yagi
didn't plan to tell you about her sister's needle. It came out because
you asked her a proper question and she trusted you, and she went home
that night and thought about it, and I know that because she told me."

He had not known that.

"That's the whole of it," said Sakura. "One thing, and I'm not
mentioning it again. Sorry." She went up two steps and then stopped.
"Also I'm fourteen and I've got about nine hundred opinions about the
rest of it and I am keeping every single one of them to myself, and I
want you to know that this is costing me *enormously*."

---

The Thursday was the part he had been braced for and it did not
happen.

It ran exactly like every other Thursday.

She was twenty-five minutes late, which she always was. She was furious
about a bridge, which she always was. She asked him about the
degradation work and he told her about a control he had run that had
not worked and she found the fault in it in about ninety seconds and
was right. She turned her cup a quarter when she was deciding whether
to answer honestly. She refilled her own and did not touch his.

Nothing was mentioned.

Nothing was avoided either, which was the part he could not get past —
there was no moment in three hours where the conversation went round
something. He had watched people go round things his entire life and he
knew the shape of it at forty feet in poor light, and it was not
there.

It was an ordinary Thursday.

It was such a completely ordinary Thursday that he walked home along
the market row afterwards with his hands in his pockets and could not
make it sit still, and stopped under the lamp at the fourth street, and
stood there.

*Nothing about that evening was unusual.*

*That is the observation and I have checked it four ways and it holds.
Duration, subject matter, her arrival time, the number of times she
refilled, the number of times she turned the cup, the point at which
she stopped being angry about the bridge. All inside the ordinary
range.*

*All of it.*

*I have fourteen months of Thursdays and I have never once had one that
was inside the ordinary range on every single measure at the same
time.*

He stood under the lamp for a while with the cold coming off the
stones.

*A thing can be too ordinary.*

*I do not know what to do with that, and I am not going to ask her, and
I am not going to work it out, and I have decided both of those and I
would like it on the record that deciding not to work something out is
now the second time I have managed it and that it did not get any
easier.*

He went home.

He wrote up the inventory — the one and a half seconds, the cup she put
down during, the left hand and then the right, the breath at four
inches, the six-to-nine seconds — and read it back, and added nothing
to it, because there was nothing he could add that was not a guess.

And at the bottom, under the column with no unit at the head of it, he
wrote:

*Eleven people turned six tables ninety degrees for a date that was
three months gone.*

*Whatever the rest of it was, that part is not ambiguous, and I am
going to hold on to that one.*
