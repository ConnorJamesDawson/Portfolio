# Chapter 27, Scene 2 — The Weight

*Act 4, part B. Konoha General, rear yard. Sazare Ruri, age
sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,868 words.*

---

Kanzaki Uta brought three scales to the hospital.

The first was the brass balance she used every Thursday, packed in its wooden
case with the set of stamped weights. The second was a grocer's platform scale
borrowed from the ration office. The third belonged to the hospital laundry
and ordinarily weighed bundles of wet sheets.

"No single instrument covers the range," she said. "That is already
irritating."

She established herself in the rear yard because the evidence would not fit
indoors. Wrapped fragments occupied four trestle tables. Larger sections stood
on canvas beneath the covered passage. Natsumi remained with them under orders
from village security. Ashida sat by the door with his sling and an amended
movement form. Nurse Yagi had put Sazare in the evacuation chair and removed
the crutches so he could not improve his position independently.

The nineteen unused spaces in the tobacco tin showed dark against the cloth.

Six grains remained in the right-hand fold.

Kanzaki counted them, transferred each with bone tweezers and weighed them
together. Two point three seven grams.

"Declared starting mass?"

"Seven point five."

"Consumed original stock, five point one three grams."

The clerk wrote it.

"Recoverable output?"

"Preliminary two hundred and eighty-four pounds," Natsumi said.

"Preliminary is a word used by people who have not calibrated their scale."

"The street office used two scales."

"They used cargo scales in the rain."

Natsumi looked at the clear sky. "It hasn't rained."

"Then they have one fewer excuse."

Kanzaki began with the ordered fragments. Each was unwrapped, assigned an
evidence number, inspected under a lens and weighed twice. She dictated face,
fracture, colour, occlusion and whether the retained course was visible.
Sazare supplied where it had come from when he knew.

Fragment seven: gate barrier, outer face, partial seed, forty-one grams.

Fragment twelve: forearm plate, lower course, cloth inclusion, three hundred
and eight grams.

Fragment nineteen: gutter rib, resonant fracture, no visible seed,
twenty-seven grams.

At fragment twenty-three Kanzaki stopped.

"This was grown from a broken structure?"

"Yes."

"Which?"

"The first gate barrier."

"And then broken again."

"By sound."

"So it is a second-generation fragment."

"Possibly third."

"How can it possibly be third?"

"The gate barrier broke. I grew the forearm plate from one piece. The plate
shed an outer course under resonance. This may be from that course."

Kanzaki removed her glasses and looked at the fragment without them.

"Your seed replicated."

"The order replicated. Not the material."

"The distinction will comfort everyone."

"It matters."

"I know. That is why it will comfort nobody."

She put her glasses back on. "Mark it generation three, disputed."

The clerk wrote the phrase.

They weighed for three hours.

The hospital yard changed around the audit. Laundry staff brought baskets past
the evidence tables and complained about losing their scale. A kitchen porter
crossed twice with sacks of rice, slowing on the return journey to read the
labels. Three shinobi from security arrived to collect sealed fragments and
remained when Kanzaki refused to release anything before the total reconciled.

At midday, the ward bell rang and Yagi left to answer it. Sazare watched her
go, then looked at the ceramic dishes.

Kanzaki did not look up from the scale. "No."

"I didn't move."

"You stopped moving in a manner that suggested intent."

"I was already seated."

"And yet."

She placed a clear cover over the ordered sample until Yagi returned.

The platform scale showed a consistent error of three ounces under loads above
fifty pounds. Kanzaki demonstrated this with stamped iron weights from stores,
recalculated the six largest structures and made the clerk amend every
subtotal. The laundry scale was accurate when dry and under-read when its
canvas sling was damp. She replaced the sling rather than accepting a
correction factor.

"Why does four ounces matter?" Ashida asked.

"Because next month somebody will compare output mass with chakra
expenditure," Kanzaki said. "If the starting number is convenient rather than
true, every conclusion afterwards will be precise nonsense."

"It's two hundred and eighty-six pounds."

"And four ounces."

"The street is missing part of itself."

"Yes. We are determining how much."

Sazare understood the question beneath the weight. Nineteen grains had not
become two hundred and eighty-six pounds. They had instructed two hundred and
eighty-six pounds of Konoha to stop being road, wall, iron scale and shop
foundation. A larger deployment did not require transporting larger weapons.
It required access to somebody else's ground.

The ration-office scale, the laundry sling and Kanzaki's balance were
measuring the military value of being allowed to arrive early.

The recoverable total was two hundred and eighty-six pounds, four ounces,
excluding mineral grit, contaminated material retained by the hospital and
crystal still embedded in four structures. The natural-weight rule accounted
for the mass: the technique had converted available mineral rather than
producing matter. It did not account for the number of active seeds.

"Nineteen original grains," Kanzaki said. "Six unused. Thirteen exposed to
load. Eighteen recovered pieces still ordered strongly enough for Ruri to
detect at range, twenty-seven visible to the Byakugan but not consistently to
him, and an unknown quantity below both thresholds."

"Yes," Sazare said.

"That is not an audit. That is a population."

Kanzaki arranged the eighteen ordered pieces by generation where it could be
inferred. Thirteen might descend directly from original grains. Three had
certainly grown from first-generation fragments. Two could not be placed.

"If order reproduces during growth," Natsumi said, "one grain could create an
unlimited number of active sites."

"No," Sazare said.

"Why not?"

"Chakra, material, time, distance and loss."

"Those are practical limits."

"All limits are practical when the work is physical."

Kanzaki pointed her lens at him. "Do not be glib because the alternative is
frightening."

He stopped.

She selected the third-generation fragment. "The order weakens. We do not yet
know whether that is generation, resonant damage, poor fabrication or his
condition during growth. A seed may reproduce indefinitely in principle and
fail after two cycles in use. Both statements are presently unsupported."

"What do we report?" the clerk asked.

"Observed replication through at least three growth events. Unknown
degradation curve. No extrapolation."

"Security will extrapolate."

"Security can put its own name under it."

Ashida shifted by the door. "Is that bad?"

"It is bad for forms. Other consequences require study."

Kanzaki selected three samples: one strong, one partial, one apparently inert.
She placed them in separate ceramic dishes.

"Grow from the first."

Yagi said, "No chakra demonstration."

"That was yesterday's interview condition."

"It is today's medical condition."

"I require four seconds."

"You may require them next week."

Kanzaki looked at Sazare. "Can you?"

"Yes."

Yagi stood between the table and the chair.

"No," Sazare said.

It was the first time he had refused Kanzaki a test.

She considered him for a moment and moved the dishes out of reach. "Next
week."

The audit resumed.

At the forearm plate, they had to cut away the remaining sleeve. The crystal
retained the folds and seams in negative, including three stitches and a line
where his blood had entered a fracture. Kanzaki turned it over.

"This is poor work."

"Yes."

"The load path terminates at the wrist. The elbow is obstructed. The plates
overlap against movement. This edge would cut the wearer."

"It did."

"And it survived two resonant strikes."

"Three. The outer course shed after the second."

"Because it is poor work."

Sazare looked at her.

Kanzaki tapped the layered face. "A continuous lattice carries vibration.
Misaligned courses interrupt it. Cloth inclusions damp it. The thing survived
because you made armour as if you had never seen armour."

"I haven't."

"Excellent. We must prevent anybody teaching you until we understand which
mistakes are useful."

Ashida laughed and then held his collarbone.

Kanzaki did not smile. She was already drawing the overlap in her notebook.

When the last piece had been weighed, she asked for the tobacco tin. Sazare
passed it over. She compared its serial mark with the Thursday ledger and
checked the seal impression inside the lid.

"No unrecorded stock," she said.

"No."

"No seal blanks removed from stores."

"No."

"No prepared material concealed at the hospital before the attack."

"No."

"Nineteen grains entered. Six remain. Thirteen were used in violation of the
combat restriction. Their use is fully accounted for."

The clerk's brush hesitated at *violation*.

Kanzaki waited until he wrote it.

"The restriction existed," she said. "An audit does not improve events."

"No."

"It records them accurately enough that somebody else cannot improve them
later."

She signed the bottom of the page.

Under disposition, security requested permanent retention of every ordered
fragment. The hospital requested authority over contaminated medical pieces.
Kanzaki requested twelve samples for controlled study. The public works office
requested disposal of everything still blocking repairs.

The arguments were written in different vocabularies. Security called the
fragments active hazards. The hospital called some of them removed foreign
bodies. Public works called them obstructions. Kanzaki called them samples.
Each description produced a different owner.

"What does the amnesty agreement call them?" Sazare asked.

Ashida found the copied terms in his folder. "Fabricated substrate produced
under quota remains village property following acceptance and audit. Failed
material is disposed under supervision."

"These weren't produced under quota."

"No."

"Then?"

The clerk said, "They were produced during prohibited combat activity."

"That still doesn't assign ownership."

Natsumi looked towards the security request. "Evidence can be retained
regardless of ownership."

"Retained isn't owned."

The distinction delayed the yard for twenty minutes and altered the form.
Evidence remained held by security; ownership was marked unresolved pending
the integration decision. Hospital disposal authority superseded retention
where leaving material in a patient created medical risk.

The village would keep almost everything. It would now have to say why.

Sazare was not listed as requesting anything.

"The plate," he said.

Kanzaki looked at the form. "What about it?"

"I want it returned."

"Why?"

"It is mine."

Nobody in the yard spoke.

The answer was insufficient for every institution represented there and
complete for him.

Kanzaki drew a fifth disposition line in the margin: *one forearm structure
returned to fabricator following evidentiary release; no active use pending
evaluation.*

"Sign," she said.

Sazare signed.

The plate remained on the table, still evidence for now, but it had acquired a
destination not chosen by the village.

Natsumi added the plate to the release schedule and asked how it should be
transported when the evidentiary hold ended.

"Wrapped in cloth," Sazare said.

"Sealed container?"

"No."

"It remains active."

"So do I."

Ashida looked away before anybody could decide whether the sentence was
humour.

Kanzaki specified untreated cotton, no mineral packing, no contact with other
ordered fragments and delivery directly to Sazare's registered room. The
precautions occupied five lines. Returning one badly made piece of armour
required more administration than issuing the headband had.

Kanzaki closed the brass scale case.

"Write down what you noticed on the roof," she said. "Not for their report.
For yourself. Exact words. Date it."

"I don't know exactly what happened."

"Then write that first. In nine years you will want to know which parts you
invented afterwards."

She took the platform scale back to the ration office. The laundry scale
remained because the hospital needed sheets weighed before evening.
