# Chapter 71, Scene 10 — Hell, Scheduled

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the north fields. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1180 words.*

---

She arrived at the north fields at twenty past six on the Friday,
in the full robes, through the gate, at a pace, and the morning
understood immediately that it had been requisitioned.

Sazare, mid-footwork, saw Jiraiya see her — saw the whole
professional apparatus of the Toad Sage of Mount Myōboku read
the robes, the pace, the hour, and the specific weather of her
face, and arrive at the correct conclusion with the speed that
had kept him alive for four decades.

"Ah," said Jiraiya, to the frost, philosophically. "The
twenty-seventh of December has matured."

"You *knew* this was coming?" said Sazare, out of the side of
his mouth.

"Kid, I've known since I handed it over. There was never a
version where this didn't arrive. I just budgeted it for
March." He rolled his shoulders once, the way he did before
the heavy drills, and planted his feet, and — Sazare noted it
for the file, because it was the finding of the morning — made
no move whatsoever toward any of the eleven exits a Sannin
keeps in any field. "Stay for it. That's an order from your
other teacher. This is a class too."

Fukasaku, on the cloth, arranged himself with the unhurried
comfort of four hundred years of front-row seats.

What followed was, for its first three minutes, the finest
one-sided engagement Sazare had ever witnessed, and his
standard of comparison included the twentieth of December.

She did not raise her voice past the second sentence. She did
not need to; the first two had cleared the airspace. She
conducted the prosecution at parade pitch, over the frost,
with the sun coming up red behind her like sympathetic
staging: the *entrusting of that pornographic disgrace to a
boy in her — in her senior medical fabricator's condition*;
the bad kisser intelligence, "and we WILL be returning to
where you heard that, you enormous leak"; the months — *the
MONTHS, Jiraiya* — of her conducting a private investigation
into a curriculum that this white-haired disaster had
personally issued; the sheer, the *documented*—

"Tell me it didn't work," said Jiraiya.

The field went silent. Sazare, notebook already out per
standing orders, wrote the sentence down verbatim with the
particular reverence owed to men lighting cigarettes on
their own pyres.

"*What* did you say to me—"

"Tell me it didn't work, Hime." He stood in the frost, coat
off, taking the whole front of the storm the way he had
taken forty years of everything, and his voice did the thing
Sazare had heard exactly twice — the bathhouse register, no
jokes in the load path. "In November I stood on this
village's wall and watched the two most careful people I
have ever loved orbit each other at arm's length, both of
them so terrified of spending one honest sentence that
they'd have died of solvency. He had no manual, no father,
no brother, nobody he could ask one question that wasn't
routed through a Corps file — and one book in the world
that a bad old man knew from the inside. So yes. I gave the
kid the only thing I had. It was a filthy, badly-plumbed,
anatomically negligent thing—" a half-bow toward Sazare, "—
his notes are correct, all fourteen — and I would do it
again at six sharp tomorrow, and here is my entire defense,
delivered to the court on the ground of its choosing: look
at him. Look at YOU. Tell me, in front of the toad and the
frost and the boy's little book — tell me it didn't work."

And there, Sazare recorded, the prosecution met the
defense's only weapon, which was that every word of it was
true, and the field held its breath while the Hokage of the
Land of Fire stood in the red morning visibly weighing
forty years of this exact man — every furlough he'd
wrecked, every wall he'd peeped, every debt and every war
and every coffin they had carried together — against a
small orange book that had, when the ledger was honestly
run, helped deliver her the finest winter of her life.

"...The sentence," she said, at last, in the voice of a
judge who has already ruled and is now merely enjoying the
robes, "is in three parts."

"There she is," said Jiraiya, with enormous affection, and
took his sentencing at attention.

"Part one. The next edition's medical review — every
manuscript, before the printers, is submitted to the
village's senior fabricator for anatomical and structural
audit. His findings are binding. You will pay the standard
consultancy rate, and you will pay it to the hospital's
winter fund, and you will thank the reviewer in the
acknowledgements of every edition until you die: *by name
withheld*, since apparently you can keep nothing else."

Jiraiya's face performed a complicated manoeuvre in which
horror and delight fought for the same ground and merged
into something that could only be called family. Sazare,
appointed the official editor of Icha Icha in a frozen
field before seven in the morning, wrote his own sentence
into the record with a hand that nearly held.

"Part two." She crossed the frost to Jiraiya, and stood
before him, and — Sazare logged it — took one breath that
was not for volume. "The bit you got right. He needed a
manual and he had nobody. That's a true finding and it's
MINE now, and here is what it costs you: you don't get to
be the bad old man at arm's length any more. You're in
this family, you catastrophic wreck — you've been in it
since you handed a lonely boy a book because it was the
only door you had. So: dinner. The second floor. Monthly.
You, him, me, Shizune, and whatever the toads eat. First
one is Sunday. Attendance is mandatory and you will
BEHAVE, and if you are ever late I will tell Fukasaku-sama
what actually happened to Ma's kitchen in the Land of
Rivers."

The silence that followed contained an elder toad turning
his head with geological slowness, and the Toad Sage of
Mount Myōboku — read by everyone present at survey grade —
going very slightly grey.

"Accepted," said Jiraiya, at speed.

"Part three." And this one she delivered on her way past
him, quietly, at the range married people and old
teammates keep for the sentences that matter, and Sazare,
eight feet off with a notebook, caught it only because
the frost held every sound that morning and carried it
to him whether he had standing or not. "Thank you, you
disgusting old fool. It worked."

She was through the gate before Jiraiya moved. He stood a
while in the risen morning, looking after her, and then he
turned back to the field, clapped his hands once, and was
entirely, seamlessly, the Monday-morning monster again.

"RIGHT. Footwork. Where were we. And kid—" as Sazare
squared up, the grin arriving with the sun fully over the
hills now, enormous, indestructible, and, the file would
note, suspiciously bright at the edges— "welcome to the
editorial staff. The pay's an insult and the author's
impossible. You'll fit right in."
