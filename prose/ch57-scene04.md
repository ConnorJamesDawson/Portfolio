# Chapter 57, Scene 4 — Nineteen Pages

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the Hokage's tower. Tsunade, age fifty-three.
POV: Tsunade. ~1820 words.*

---

"I know," said Tsunade.

"You know."

"I've known since about the twelfth. I've been up three nights out of
five and one of the things I have been doing with them is exactly the
arithmetic you have just done out loud, and I got your answer, and I got
it faster than you did because I had a head start."

"Then—"

"I haven't got a solution, Jiraiya. I've got the problem, in detail,
with a figure on it, and I have been carrying it about like a stone
since the eleventh, and if you have come in through my window at four in
the morning to hand me a stone I already own then you can go back out of
it."

Jiraiya put his hands up.

"Fair," he said.

"It isn't fair, it's just true."

"It's both. It's usually both, with you." He sat back. "So what's the
part I've got wrong? Because you've got a look on you that says I've got
a part wrong and you have been sitting on it for ten minutes enjoying
it."

"I have not been *enjoying* it."

"Hime."

"I have been enjoying it slightly," said Tsunade.

---

"Your file's from outside."

"My file is the best file on that boy in four countries."

"Yes. And every single line of it is a thing he *did*, which somebody
else wrote down afterwards." She put her feet off the drawer and sat up.
"You've got output. You've got eleven hundred articles and a study and a
table and a post and a price. That's all outside.

"You have not got one line about what he is actually doing, because he
has not done it yet, and when he does it there will not be an object at
the end of it for anybody to weigh."

"Then tell me."

"No," said Tsunade. "I'm going to show you, and I want to say two things
first, and then I'm going to go and stand at that window and not watch
you read it, because I have watched somebody read it once and it was
enough."

---

"First thing.

"That paper is forbidden. I forbade it, in this office, on the first of
the eighth month, on security grounds, and I was right and he agreed with
me inside about four minutes and has not stopped agreeing with me since,
and if you tell me it should be published I will tell you the same thing
I told him, which is that it names a very short list of families and that
list is a *targeting document*."

"Understood."

"Second thing, and this one matters more.

"He wrote it anyway."

Jiraiya looked up.

"Of course he did," said Tsunade. "He can't not. He's had it in ink in
the front of a notebook since he was seventeen that striking a thing out
doesn't remove it. So he wrote the whole of it in the tenth month, on his
own, at night, unasked — and then he brought me the only copy.

"With a covering note. Four lines. Saying that he was not going to submit
it, that he was not asking me to do anything with it, and that he *did
not want to be the only person holding it*, because a man holding a
document nobody knows about is concealing something, and he doesn't
conceal.

"Nineteen pages and a note. He put it on that desk in the tenth month and
he has not mentioned it once since, and it has been in that drawer under
the fragment ever since, and I have read it four times."

---

She opened the drawer and took it out.

It did not look like anything. That was the part she had never got used
to: nineteen sheets in his hand, close and small and absolutely regular,
with the tables ruled by eye and better than a printed sheet, tied once
in the corner with a bit of the string out of the fabrication room.

She put it on the desk in front of Jiraiya.

"Go on," she said.

Then she got up and went to the window and stood with her back to the
room, which was where she had been at four in the morning for eleven
hundred mornings, and listened to a man turn over a page.

---

It took him nineteen minutes.

She knew that because she counted it, and she counted it because there
was nothing else to do and because she had counted it the first time as
well.

He read it once at speed, which took about four minutes, and she heard
the pages go and she knew exactly what he was doing because it was what
she had done: going to the end to find out whether it was mad.

Then he went back to the front and read it properly.

There was a long stop about six minutes in, which was the reject table
and the four hundred rows.

There was a longer one at eleven, which was the derivation.

And at about fifteen minutes there was a stop that went on so long that
she nearly turned round.

---

"Hime," said Jiraiya.

"Mm."

"Come and sit down."

"I'm all right here."

"Tsunade. Come and sit down."

She came and sat down.

He had the pages squared in front of him and both hands flat on the desk
on either side of them, and he was not doing the voice and he was not
doing the face, and she had seen that man without either of them perhaps
five times in fifty-three years.

"Say it back to me," he said, "in your own words, so I know I've read it
right and not what I wanted to read."

"A kekkei genkai is a technique."

"Say the rest."

"It isn't in the blood. There's no substance being passed down. What gets
passed down in a clan is *instruction* — a method, and the conditions to
learn it in, and a family that starts you at four instead of fourteen,
and four generations of people who already know it standing round you.
And that produces the same result as inheritance so reliably that
everybody for eight hundred years has called it inheritance.

"The dōjutsu are different and he says so, in the paper, twice, and he
doesn't pretend to have solved them.

"Everything else — everything, Jiraiya, the whole of the rest of it — is
*taught*."

---

Jiraiya sat back.

"There's a hole in it," he said.

"There's a hole in it."

"It's testimony."

"It's testimony," said Tsunade. "A slug told him. Four hundred years of
oral tradition out of Shikkotsu delivered by a creature who was told it
by another creature, about a period before anybody kept records, and he
knows exactly what that's worth in a room — it's on page fourteen, in
his own hand, in a paragraph that is harder on himself than anything
either of us would have written.

"Four clans in this village would take it apart in nine minutes and every
one of the nine would be technically correct, and he says so, and then he
says he's right anyway, and then he says — and this is the line, this is
the one that got me the first time —"

"*I preferred the uncertainty*," said Jiraiya.

"You've found it."

"It's underlined."

"He doesn't underline," said Tsunade. "In three years and about nine
hundred documents, that is the only underlined word I have ever had off
him."

---

Jiraiya got up.

He walked to the end of the room and back, which she had seen him do
about four times in his life, and he stopped at the corner of the desk
and looked down at nineteen sheets of paper.

"I'm in this," he said.

She did not answer.

"I'm not in it. There's no name. He hasn't got a single word about me in
here and I've looked — I went back through it at fifteen minutes
specifically to find out whether he'd used me, and he hasn't, and I don't
think he's thought about me once."

"No," said Tsunade. "I don't think he has."

"But I'm the shape of it."

He put one finger on the page.

"A man with no clan name. No line, no technique in the family, no
instruction, nothing — my father sold eels, Hime, I've never told
anybody in this village that and I'm telling you at five in the morning
— and I came up alongside a Senju and an Uchiha-grade prodigy and I was
the *worst* of the three of us by a distance for eleven years.

"And I got here anyway. By working. By nothing else on this earth but
working.

"And I have spent fifty-four years being told that was remarkable. Do you
know what that word actually means when they say it to a man like me? It
means *anomaly*. It means the rule held and you were the exception, and
everybody gets to keep the rule.

"And this says there is no rule."

He took the finger off the page.

"This says I'm not an anomaly. This says I'm the *control*."

---

She let that sit.

She had known it would come and she had not known it would come like
that, and she found that she was looking at the desk rather than at him,
which she did not do.

"That's why you gave it to me," said Jiraiya.

"That's one of the reasons."

"Give me the others."

"You're a sage," said Tsunade. "You've handled natural energy for
thirty-odd years and you are one of about four humans alive who can read
the middle third of that paper and know whether it's nonsense. Yagi
can't. I can't, not properly — I got the medicine and I took the rest on
trust, and I have never taken anything on trust in my life and I hated
it.

"And you're outside the structure. Nothing you do about it goes in a
record. You have no clan, no faction and no committee, and there is
nobody you have to tell."

"And the third?"

"You've just said the third yourself," said Tsunade, "and I'm not going
to say it twice."

---

Jiraiya sat back down.

"Right," he said. "What do you actually want?"

"I want you to take it apart."

"Hime—"

"Properly. Not kindly. I want you to go at it the way Ariake Fumi would
go at it and find every place it doesn't hold, and I want it done by
somebody who understands the mechanism, because everyone in this village
who understands the mechanism is either a clan head with an interest or a
child.

"And then I want you to tell me whether there's a way to prove it that
doesn't involve a summon's word."

"There's one," said Jiraiya.

"I know there's one."

"There's exactly one, and you know his name, and so do I."

Tsunade closed her eyes.

"Tenzō," she said.
