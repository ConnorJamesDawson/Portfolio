# Chapter 70, Scene 3 — The Dosimeter

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the tower, the second floor. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~850 words.*

---

The full set had been delivered on the Monday, and the delivery
had been, by the standards of any previous month of his life, the
most dangerous hour ever scheduled: the lead model taking receipt
of the line's first complete series, upper and lower, slate blue,
bespoke to the rig's numbers, gratis per the terms of an
appointment letter with a reference number.

The specification session that had produced it — the previous
Thursday's fitting, clause by clause, on the record — had been
its own event, and the study file carried the full minute. What
mattered on the Monday was the reading his instruments gave at
delivery, and the manner in which the person in the room with
fifty years of clinical practice disagreed with the reading
before he did.

She had taken the set, disappeared behind the screen, and
emerged in the upper half and the house trousers to check the
band fit — an ordinary trial, a fitting-room commonplace, the
exact class of exposure that had cost him a nosebleed in
January and a cardiac stutter over a shirt — and his systems
had produced: a mild warmth. Two copper patches, faint,
economy-grade. The vocabulary had not even flickered.

"Hm," said Tsunade, watching him log the band numbers with a
steady hand, and the *hm* had a chart behind it.

"The armour is improving," he said.

"Mm," said Tsunade, and said nothing further at the time, and
he wrote *desensitisation curve ahead of projection* in the
study file, and was wrong, and the person in the room who knew
he was wrong had fifty years of reading exactly this class of
error off exactly this class of patient, and filed it, and
began, quietly, to worry.

*What I know now and did not know then,* the honest entry would
run, days later, *is that a dosimeter that stops registering
has not become tolerant. It has become exhausted. The response
was never the weakness the study treated it as. The response
was the reading. And the reading had stopped because the
instrument was running on reserves so deep that nothing —
not the set, not the clause-four seam rendered to tolerance,
not her — could raise a full signal off it.*

*She saw it on the Monday. I saw a study succeeding.*

*This is why practitioners do not treat themselves.*

The evenings had compressed to one fixed ritual and its
increasingly abbreviated coda.

The hair. Fifty passes. That held — it held the way the deepest
courses hold, below the reach of weather — and he did it every
night regardless of the state of his hands, because it was the
appointment the whole day funnelled toward, and because the
brush with the mark in it lived on the shelf beside the
pinwheel and had a population to serve.

But the coda — the talk after, the slow unspooling hour that
January had built, the second-best conversations of his life
conducted horizontal in the dark — the coda had shortened by
increments the way the noons had filled by increments: to
twenty minutes, to ten, to the Tuesday of the second week,
when it ran, by her count, which he did not know she was
keeping, four sentences.

He was aware, dimly, at the fading edge of each day, that
something was being said to him in the dark and that his
returns were arriving from further and further away. On the
Wednesday she tried a tease — a good one, unhurried, the
full apparatus, something about the seat seam and the minuted
dissatisfaction — and he heard it the way you hear rain on a
roof from deep in a warm bed: as weather, lovely, distant,
requiring nothing.

"Have I ever told you," he said, from most of the way down,
with complete sincerity and no context whatsoever, "that you
should be the definition of beauty. They should print your—"

And was asleep. Mid-sentence. Hand still loosely closed around
a fistful of her sleeve, breath gone long, the whole
formidable instrument of him shut down between one word and
the next like a lamp pinched out.

He did not hear what she said to the dark after that, or know
how long she lay awake cataloguing, or that the sentence had
joined thirteen previous uncertified statements in a private
collection she was keeping in the register nobody read, or
that two floors of the tower and one signature could have
returned his evenings to her at a stroke, or that she had, at
around one in the morning, itemised every reason she could
not use them, found the list complete and correct and
unbearable, and called herself, out loud, in the dark of her
own room, an embarrassment to the profession of grown women.

He knew none of it.

That was the finding the week was building toward: the man
who read the whole village off walls and columns, the
instrument that had mapped her moods to four decimal places
across three years — asleep at his post for the first time in
his life, at the exact station where the reading mattered
most.
