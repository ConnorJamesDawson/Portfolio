# Chapter 3, Scene 3 — The Gulls

*Act 1, beat 10. Sazare Ruri, ages nine to ten. POV: Sazare. ~2,700 words.*

---

The Nakashimas went in the third month.

Sazare found out about it the way he found out about most things, which was by
noticing something and then getting the explanation wrong.

What he noticed was the washing.

The Nakashimas dried fish on their roof, which was why the gulls came, which was
why Iwao had spent Sazare's entire life conducting a one-sided feud with a family
who had never once been rude to him. They also, being fish-driers, did a great
deal of laundry, and it hung along the side wall on a line that ran from the
corner of their house to a hook on the wall of the yard next door, and it was
there every day of the world except when it rained.

It was there on a Tuesday and it was still there on a Friday.

By the Friday the two shirts on the end had blown off and were lying in the gutter
underneath, and nobody had picked them up.

"Their line's fallen down," he said at dinner.

"Mm," said his father.

"The shirts are in the gutter. They've been there two days."

"Mm."

"Should I go and—"

"No," said his father, and asked Iwao something about copper.

---

He went round the front on the Sunday, because he was ten years old in nine days
and had decided this made him nearly an adult, and because the shirts were still
there and it had begun to feel like something he was personally failing to deal
with.

The door was shut. That was all. It was shut and it did not look any different
from a shut door, and the mat was out, and there was a bucket by the step with
rainwater in it.

He knocked.

He was still standing there when his father came round the corner of the wall,
walking fast, and took him by the shoulder and turned him round and walked him
back the other way without saying a single word about it.

Not roughly. Ren did not do anything roughly. He put a hand on his son's shoulder
and turned him and walked, and it was the most frightening thing that had ever
happened to Sazare in his life, and neither of them mentioned it again.

---

The gulls stayed.

That was the part he could not get past. The fish had gone off the roof — somebody
had come and taken it, or it had been taken, or it had rotted and been cleared,
he never found out — but the gulls came back every morning for weeks afterwards
and stood about on the tiles of an empty house arguing about nothing at all, and
Iwao, who had complained about those gulls every morning for nine years, stopped
complaining about them.

---

Sazare asked Jirō, because Jirō knew things.

"Nakashima," said Jirō. "Fish?"

"Fish."

"Yeah, they went."

"Went *where*?"

Jirō chewed the inside of his cheek. They were on the wall at the third bridge.
The stall was open; the woman had done them two of the fried things and had not
charged for the second, which she had started doing sometimes and which neither of
them had commented on.

"Dunno," he said.

"You always know."

"I know when my mum knows." He shrugged. "She doesn't know. She said it's not the
same as the others."

"What others?"

"Just — others." Jirō looked at the water. "There's been a few."

"A few *what*?"

"Sazare." Jirō turned round and looked at him properly, which he almost never did.
"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't go asking people. That's all." He had gone pink. He did that when he was
serious, which was rare and awful. "My brother says you shouldn't go asking about
houses. Because if you ask about a house it's like — it's like you're saying
there's a reason. And if there's a reason then somebody thought of it, and then
they're thinking about it, and then they think about who else."

"That's stupid."

"I know it's stupid," said Jirō, unhappily. "I'm just telling you what he said."

They ate the fried things. The gulls went over.

"Anyway," said Jirō, "she said it's probably nothing to do with anything. She said
they probably owed somebody."

"Do you think that?"

"I think they had a *cat*," said Jirō, with sudden and total misery, "and I don't
know where the cat's gone," and they did not discuss it any further.

---

Other things.

Sazare would not have been able to put them in an order. They arrived across
about eight months in the way that weather arrives, and each of them was, in
itself, nothing.

The fishmonger on the cutting — Ozaki, who had been calling him *young Ruri*
since he was four and who had once given him a whole crab claw for nothing — began
saying *son*. Not coldly. Just *son*, the way you'd say it to anybody's child, and
he said it for about four months and then went back to *young Ruri* again, and by
then it had stopped meaning what it used to mean.

The permit table appeared at the west quay in the fifth month. It was for the
boats. Everybody said it was for the boats. Sazare's father had to go and sit at
it for two hours in the sixth month with the ledgers to get the guild movement
signed off, and came home and said it had been fine, and had been unusually quiet
for the rest of the evening.

There was a gate put on the Hōzuki compound. Sazare only knew this because they
went past the end of that street on the way to Genzō's, and one week there was a
gap between two walls and the next week there was a gate in it, and a man beside
the gate who was not a Hōzuki.

"Is that to keep them in?" he said.

"It's theirs," said his father. "They asked for it."

"Why would you ask for a gate?"

"Because it's better than being given one," said Ren, and then, immediately,
"forget I said that," which of course guaranteed the opposite.

---

The last of it, and the only piece Sazare noticed at the time as a piece, came in
the eighth month.

A boy in his year called Tomoharu did not come in for four days, and when he came
back he sat somewhere different.

He had always sat two along from Sazare. He had sat two along from Sazare for
three years, and they were not friends exactly but they were the specific kind of
not-friends who have a running joke, theirs being a long and idiotic business
about the drawing of the continent on the slate wall and which blob was worst.

He came back on the fifth day and sat at the front on the other side, next to a
girl he did not like.

Sazare caught him in the corridor at the break.

"You've moved."

"Yeah."

"You've moved next to *Chiyo*."

"Yeah."

"Why've you—"

"My dad said," said Tomoharu, and went past him.

That was the whole of it. He said it without any particular feeling and he did not
look up, and Sazare stood in the corridor with his bag on his shoulder and worked
at it for the rest of the morning and never did get it to come out to anything.

He told Kohaku about it in the ninth month, when she was back, and she listened
all the way through with her hands flat on her knees, and then asked him three
questions, which were: what was Tomoharu's other name, and what did his father do,
and had anybody else moved.

The answers were Kaneda, and something at the harbour office, and no, not that
Sazare had seen.

"Right," said Kohaku.

"It's stupid."

"It is stupid," she agreed. "Play the chips."

They played the chips. He got eleven families out of twenty-one, off the ugly ones,
which had taken him four months and about which he was insufferable.

---

The notice came at the end of the ninth month.

It was pinned to the board outside the district office along with the other
notices, and it was on the same paper as the other notices, and it said what it
said every year, which was that the household intake register would be collected
between such and such dates and that heads of household should attend or make
arrangements.

Underneath, in the same hand, it said:

*For households in the registered trades, an officer will attend at the premises
to assist with completion.*

Sazare read it on the way past because he read everything on the way past. He got
about as much out of it as he got out of the notice above it, which was about
mooring fees.

At home, Iwao had the register out of the drawer on the table and was going down
it with a finger.

"It's the same one," he said.

"It'll be the same one," said Ren.

"It's the *same one*. I've got last year's here. It's word for word." Iwao put the
two sheets side by side and squared them up. "There. Same. So all that fuss for
nothing."

"Nobody was making a fuss."

"Hisui was making a fuss."

"I was not making a fuss," said Sazare's mother from the workshop, in a tone that
suggested she had been listening to every word of this from the beginning. "I said
I'd rather do it myself."

"Well, you can't, because they're sending a man."

"Yes," said Hisui. "I know."

Sazare came and stood at the table and looked at the form upside down, which was
how he read most things, because nobody ever moved anything for him.

*Name. Trade. Persons resident.*

And then a fourth line, which had more words in it than the other three put
together.

"What's manifest mean?"

"It means shows up," said Iwao.

"Shows up how?"

"Shows up like — like you've got it and it works." Iwao turned the sheet round so
it was the right way up for him, which was the wrong way up for Sazare, and
started counting on his fingers. "Right. So that's your mother. That's Kohaku.
That's me. And that's you."

Sazare stopped.

"Me?"

"You."

"It's not manifest in me," he said. "I make *hollow ones*."

Iwao looked up.

And Sazare, who was ten years old, and who had spent three years being told he was
getting better, and who had exactly one thing in the world that he was
unambiguously bad at — Sazare saw his uncle's face do something complicated, and
misread it entirely, and felt his ears go hot.

"It counts," said Iwao.

"It shouldn't count if it doesn't *work*—"

"It counts, boy."

"That's not *fair*—"

"*It counts*," said Iwao, so loudly that the frames next door faltered for a
second and came back, and Sazare shut his mouth.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Sorry," said Iwao. "Sorry. It counts, that's all. It's four names. It's the same
four names as last year and the year before and it'll be the same four names next
year." He straightened the sheets again, needlessly. "Go and do your frame."

Sazare went and did his frame.

It came up hollow, but only at the very end, and shorter than last time — nine
tenths of it solid all the way through — and he sat on the sill with it in his
palm for a long while in the dark and could not work out why he did not want to
show it to anybody.
