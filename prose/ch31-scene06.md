# Chapter 31, Scene 6 — Stamped

*Act 4, part C. Konoha, the evaluation office. Sazare Ruri, age
sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,559 words.*

---

The evaluation office was on the second floor of the administration
building, third door along, and had a bench outside it with four people
already on it.

He was fourth.

The bench was pine, worn pale in two patches where generations of
people had sat in the same two places, and the wall above it carried a
notice about the correct submission of expense claims that had been
there long enough for the paper to have gone the colour of weak tea.
The corridor smelled of floor polish and, faintly, of somebody's lunch.

Ahead of him: a chūnin of about thirty with a splint on two fingers,
reading nothing, staring at the opposite wall. A woman in her twenties
with a rolled scroll across her knees who had checked the tie on it
four times in six minutes. And a boy of perhaps thirteen with his
mother, both of them upright and both of them very carefully not
talking, the boy's forehead protector so new that the cloth still had
the fold-creases in it from the packet.

*Rung one: the creases.*

*Rung two: he's had it less than a week and he's already wearing it to
an administrative appointment.*

*Rung three: he wants to be seen in it.*

*Rung four: he's frightened this office is going to take it off him.*

Sazare looked at the mother's hands, which were folded in her lap and
which had been folded in her lap without moving for six minutes, and
found that he had arrived at rung five without going through four, and
could not have said how.

*She's more frightened than he is and she's decided he isn't going to
find that out today.*

He looked away, because it felt like reading somebody's post.

---

The clerk called him in at twenty past.

The office had two desks, one window, four filing cabinets and a
stamp-pad. The man behind the near desk was about forty-five, in
civilian dress, with a cardigan on over it and reading glasses pushed
up on his head and a second pair in his top pocket, and he did not
stand up.

"Ruri Sazare?"

"Sazare Ruri."

"Sazare Ruri." He corrected it on the form without any interest in
having been corrected. "Out-of-cycle operational evaluation, three
components, sat over four days. You'll want the result."

"Yes."

"Pass, qualified." He turned the sheet round on the desk, put one
finger on a line about two thirds of the way down, and slid it across.
"Read the classification, not the word *pass*. The word *pass* is for
the file. The classification is the part that decides what happens to
you."

Sazare read it.

*Operational classification: SUPPORT — FORTIFICATION AND CASUALTY
MANAGEMENT. Not certified for independent combat operations. Not
certified for unaccompanied deployment. Deployable in a supported role
under a designated commander.*

And underneath, in the deferrals box:

*Deferred pending re-test: academy ninjutsu (three techniques,
Clone/Transformation/Substitution — candidate to re-sit no earlier than
six months). Deferred pending medical: full pathway clearance
(Ubukata). Deferred pending further evidence: large-scale technique
cessation — no test currently exists; not to be authorised in a
populated area under any circumstances (Hyūga).*

"There's a lot deferred," he said.

"There's a lot deferred because your evaluators wrote *I don't know*
in three separate places and I have been in this office for eleven
years and that is unusual enough that I read the whole file twice."
The man pulled the sheet back and squared it. "Most people's
evaluators write something confident. Yours have written a set of
questions and a note explaining why they can't answer them yet, which
is either the most honest paperwork I've handled this year or the most
inconvenient, and I've decided it's both."

"Which one goes on the form?"

"Neither. The form has a box." He inked the stamp. "Any questions
before I do this?"

Sazare thought about it.

"What does *supported role under a designated commander* mean in
practice?"

"It means you never go anywhere alone, somebody senior is always
carrying the decision, and if it goes wrong it's their name and not
yours." The stamp came down. "For most people that's an insult. Given
what's in your file about the last time nobody was carrying the
decision, I'd suggest you take it as a load somebody else is agreeing
to pick up."

He signed under the stamp, tore the carbon off, and held out the copy.

"That's yours. Keep it — you'll be asked for it, and if you lose it
you'll spend a fortnight in this building getting another one." He was
already reaching for the next folder. "Congratulations, by the way.
Send the next one in, would you."

---

Sazare stood in the corridor with a piece of paper in his hand.

The woman with the scroll went in. The bench had one person on it now,
the boy with the new forehead protector, who was sitting where the
chūnin with the splint had been and who looked up at him with an
expression of naked interrogative panic.

"Was it bad?" the boy said.

His mother put a hand on his arm.

"No," said Sazare. "There's a man in a cardigan and a stamp and he'll
call you by the wrong name and correct it without minding. It takes
about eleven minutes."

"Did you pass?"

"Yes. With about six things they've written down that I can't do."

The boy absorbed this.

"That's allowed?"

"Apparently."

He went down the stairs and out through the front, and the day had
gone bright and cold while he had been inside, the kind of clear
afternoon that comes late in the year with the light coming in low and
hard from the west and everything at the wrong angle, throwing long
shadows up the street from ankle height. The wind had dropped. It
smelled of dust and woodsmoke and, from somewhere along the row,
frying.

He got about forty feet before he stopped and read the paper again.

*Operational classification: SUPPORT.*

He had turned eleven in a classroom in the fog. He had sat the
Kirigakure Academy's first-year paper and come fourth of thirty-one,
and had been told by a woman he trusted more than anybody alive except
his sister that the sealed scroll came at graduation. His clan had held
its techniques behind that word for four hundred years. *When you
graduate.* His mother had said it in a workshop with a copper lattice
on the bench and both hands busy. His uncle had said it twice. The
scroll had gone off a shelf in a house with the door broken in and had
been sold somewhere south with everything else that had a price, and
was on a stranger's shelf now, in a collection, uncatalogued, and he
had chased three rumours of it in four years and caught none of them.

Eight years late. In a foreign country. Out of a small office, past a
bench, from a man in a cardigan who had spelled his name backwards and
had not known there was anything behind the word at all.

Nobody in that building knew.

Not one person in the whole village knew, and there was nobody left
alive who could be told, and the only two people who would have
understood what the paper in his hand was standing in for were a woman
in an unmarked grave in a district that no longer existed and a girl on
a shingle spit with the sea going out through the stones.

Sazare Ruri stood in a bright cold street in the Land of Fire and
found, with some surprise, that this did not feel like a wound.

It felt like an appointment he had finally kept.

---

He got to the hospital at the fourth hour and did not tell anybody
until somebody asked.

Somebody asked at eleven minutes past, because O-Shige's nephew's wife
worked in the administration building and the news had come up the
Nakadōri considerably faster than he had.

By the sixth hour the second floor had it. Emiko shook his hand at the
desk, once, briskly. Nurse Tomoe, who had never in four months said
anything to him that was not about a dressing, said "well done" and
went back to a dressing. One of the new orderlies asked if it meant he
was going away, and Sazare said no, and the orderly said "oh, good,"
and looked embarrassed about having said it.

Handa was asleep when he came round the screen and woke up
approximately four seconds later, which he always did and always
denied.

"Well?"

"Support classification. Fortification and casualty management. Not
certified for independent operations, six things deferred, and I can't
do the Clone Technique in front of anybody for another six months."

"That's a pass."

"It's a qualified pass."

"It's a pass," Handa said, "and I've been listening to you take the
good half off everything you're handed for four months, and I'd like
one afternoon where you don't."

Sazare stood at the end of the bed with the carbon copy in his hand.

"All right," he said.

"Say the thing."

"I passed."

"There he is." Handa settled back into the pillow with enormous
satisfaction. "Now go and put that somewhere it won't get wet, and then
come back, because the window's sticking again and I've been saving it
for you all day."
