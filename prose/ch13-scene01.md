# Chapter 13, Scene 1 — Noon

*Act 2, part D. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,200 words.*

---

The winter he was sixteen, he helped bring a child into the world, and that
was the high-water mark, and he knew it at the time, which is rare, and
worth recording.

It was the charcoal-burner's wife from up the second valley, three weeks
early, in the worst night of the year — the road shut with snow above the
shrine, the husband arriving at Tōan's door on borrowed snowshoes, grey to
the lips — and Tōan had looked at the weather and the distance and then at
Sazare, and said, "You're past the door tonight. Boil, carry, hold, and do
exactly nothing on your own account," and they had gone up the white valley
together under a hard moon.

It went long and then it went wrong and then, near dawn, it went suddenly,
shockingly right, the way Tōan's work so often went — the cord where a cord
should never be, the thread of light going in patient and precise while the
wind worked the shutters, Sazare holding the lamp and the woman's knee and
the whole night in the palms of his hands, still water, four hours, not
yet, not yet — and then a raw furious wail into the cold air, and the
burner on his knees by the bed with his face in his wife's hair, and Tōan
saying, in the dosage voice, "Well. Good morning."

They walked home in the blue snow-light with the gear shared between them.

"Most of the trade's the other end of it," said Tōan, after a mile. "You've
seen that end. I wanted you to have this one before you go — a man should
know what the back of the door is *for*, not only what it costs." Another
mile. "She'd have died forty years ago, that woman. Both of them. What I
did tonight, four men alive can do — and the difference between four and
four hundred is nothing but teaching, and the difference between four
hundred and none is a country that decides children matter more than
secrets. Remember which trade you saw tonight. You'll meet the other kind."

Sazare walked, and carried it, and did not ask *before you go* — because
it was midwinter, and the road was shut, and some sentences you leave
under the snow where they fall.

---

Everything that spring stood at noon.

Sakai's yard: he lasted eleven seconds now, twice in ten, against Sakai at
half-drink — which Sakai called "adequate against a ruin, so: three seconds
against the living" and privately, Sazare could tell, regarded as a
monument. The counts had long since stopped being lessons and become what
they had always secretly been, two men keeping each other's mornings
honest. Some days now the first jug went untouched until noon. Neither of
them said one word about that either.

The porch trade thrummed. He had the mending of three valleys, and a
waiting list, and Gonta — foreman of a timber gang now, hitch and all —
had appointed himself agent and took a cut in beer he never collected.
The tenth and the tenth went down the mountain on the first of every
month. The tray on the rail was in use most nights, and small clear
useful things had begun to appear about the house, unremarked, the way
the copper wire had appeared: a set of true straight-edges. A lens-blank
for the old magnifier whose glass had crazed. Stoppers for the acid
bottles, ground to their necks, tight as breath.

Nobody said anything. The house's laws held. But Tōan used them, daily,
in the sight of both of them, and there is a way of using a thing that is
louder than thanks.

Even the nights had gone quiet, mostly. The hand still came — it would
always come; he had stopped asking it to leave — but it came now the way
weather came, known, seasonal, survivable, and some nights the river ran
through the square instead and the lamps went by, and everything waited,
and he woke slow and whole in the warm dark and heard, through the wall,
a spoon tap twice on a jar rim and go still, as though somebody had only
been checking.

He was sixteen. He had a trade, a teacher, a doctrine, a town, a waiting
list, and a count paid monthly against a debt he never named.

He caught himself, one evening on the porch in the long light, doing a
thing he had no word for — sitting with the whole of it around him, the
steam and the sawmill's two notes and Gonta's laugh coming up from the
lane, and holding it the way you hold a finished piece, turning it under
the light, checking the clarity —

and understood, with a small cold start, that he was memorising it.

That some part of him, lower than decision, had already begun to pack.

---

The stranger came at the end of the fifth month, on foot, in good boots,
and everything about him arrived a half-beat early.

Word arrived first — Gonta's runner, ahead of the man himself: *gent
coming up asking after the stone-mender, talks like a ledger, tips like
a lord* — and Sazare, cutting a hairpin blank at the porch bench, filed
the two facts and felt them not fit together, because gentlemen who
talked like ledgers did not tip like lords; a tip is information, and
ledgers hate spending it.

The man came up the road at mid-morning, alone, at an easy pace, in
travelling clothes that had cost more than they admitted, and stopped at
the gate, and did not come through it until asked, and was pleasant.

He was perhaps five-and-forty, smooth-faced, with a traveller's tan that
stopped at his collar and hands — Sazare read them by long habit — that
had never done a day of any trade Sazare knew. Not soft. *Unmarked.*
Hands that were kept the way instruments are kept.

"Forgive the intrusion. They speak of your work in three towns — the
stone-mender of Yumoto. Yashiro." He gave the name the way you set down
a coin you know to be adequate. "I wonder if I might impose."

He laid a folded cloth of deep blue silk on the bench and opened it with
one turn of his kept fingers, and Sazare stopped hearing the sawmill.

It was a pendant, a flat teardrop the length of a thumb, on a worn silk
cord, and it was crystal — not quartz, not paste, not anything a river
or a mine had ever made. Grown. Four hours' work, an even feed, a clean
release; the colour ran through it the way the blue ran through
everything from home, faint at the point, gathering toward the shoulder
the way it gathered when the grower fed from the north side of the frame
because the light in the workshop came from the south —

It was home. It was pre-war trade stock; there had been ten thousand
such pieces sold down the years through jewellers in six countries; it
proved nothing, meant nothing, could have been bought honestly in any
port on the coast.

The chip was on the shoulder. Small. Catastrophic. Exactly where the eye
went first.

"A keepsake of my patron's," said the man called Yashiro, mildly. "Not
valuable, I'm told, except in the sentimental way. It suffered a fall.
The jewellers I've consulted say the material is unusual — some
old island trade, one gathers; *shōton* work, one of them called it —
and that matching it is beyond them." He smiled, exactly enough. "They
say cutting it is a specialist's art. And then in Ashigawa one hears of
a young man up the springs who mends what nobody else will touch — and
here I am, at your gate, entirely at your mercy."

The porch was warm. The steam went up the valley. Sazare held his
family's work in his hand — some cousin of a piece, grown by some hand
that had eaten at some table he knew the grain of — and looked at a chip
he could have healed invisibly, grown seamless, made *whole*, in four
quiet hours with the door shut.

And he heard, in order, without hurry, the way you hear the parts of a
chord:

Gent coming up asking after the stone-mender. His people are polite,
and patient, and they pay for introductions. A tan that stopped at the
collar. Hands kept like instruments. *Shōton, one of them called it.*
Kindness from strangers who already know what you are.

*You don't explain. You walk.*

But you did not walk at mid-morning, off your own porch, from a pleasant
man with a commission. That was the other thing four years had taught
him, the Anzu thing, the ledger thing: a bolt is a *description*, and
descriptions sell.

So Sazare turned the pendant twice under the light, tradesman to the
bone, and said, in the flat pleasant voice of the second-best doctrine,
the voice his mother had built him at eleven and Mizuho had proofed at
twelve:

"Whoever made this is beyond me. The material's beyond me — I couldn't
tell you what it is. The chip I can polish out to a facet, and match one
opposite for balance, and it'll be honest work and look intended. Three
days. It won't be what it was."

"Nothing ever is," said the man called Yashiro, comfortably, and paid in
advance, and it was very slightly too much, and Sazare took it, because
the boy he was being would have taken it.

The man walked back down toward the town, unhurried, pausing once at
the bend to admire the gorge, and Sazare sat on the porch with his
family in his hand, and did not go back to the hairpin for a long time.
