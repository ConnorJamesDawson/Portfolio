# Chapter 45, Scene 4 — The Fourth

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, training ground eleven. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,617 words.*

---

He asked on the twenty-sixth, at the end of the falling, sitting in the
dust with his back against the post and the light going orange along
the west treeline the way it did at that hour in that month.

"Can I ask you about the Rasengan."

Kakashi, four feet away with the book out, did not move for slightly
longer than he usually did not move.

"You can ask," he said.

"It's a shape technique."

"It is."

"Pure. No nature transformation anywhere in it." Sazare had the
schoolbook in his bag and did not get it out. "It's in a first-term
textbook as the standard example of shape transformation taken to its
limit, on the page after the exercises, which nobody reads because the
page before it is about homework."

"That's about right."

"He built it from nothing."

"Three years," said Kakashi. "About that. He didn't keep a start date,
which used to annoy people."

"And then he taught it."

Kakashi closed the book on one finger.

"Ask the actual question," he said. "You've been circling for four
minutes and I've watched you do it three times this month and I'd
rather have it straight."

---

Sazare put his hands on his knees.

"There's no bloodline in it," he said.

"No."

"He wasn't born able to do it. There's no clan, there's no eye, there's
nothing in his blood that made it possible. He was a man who worked out
a thing over three years, and it is, as far as I can establish, the
single most respected technique developed in the last century."

"That's a fair summary."

"And then a man who invented a thing from nothing handed it to your
teacher's student, and to you, and I'm told to a boy of fifteen who is
somewhere in the Land of Rivers as of last month."

Kakashi said nothing.

"So the question," said Sazare, "is: what did the three years actually
consist of. Not the technique. I don't want the technique and I
couldn't do it if you gave it to me — I can't hold a sphere between my
palms for a count of three, I've been trying for four nights. I want
the *shape of the work*. What was he doing on an ordinary Tuesday in
the second year."

The crickets started up at the far end at almost exactly the minute
they started up every evening.

"Why," said Kakashi.

"Because I've got a containment problem," said Sazare, "and he had a
containment problem, and he solved his, and I've been going at mine
alone for eleven years on the assumption that nobody has ever had one,
which I have this week found out is the assumption I make about
everything and it has never once been true."

---

Kakashi put the book away.

He did not sit down, but he stopped standing in the way that meant he
was about to leave, which for him was the same thing.

"Three stages," he said. "He worked them in order and he named them
afterwards, not at the time — at the time it was one problem that kept
turning into a different problem, which is what work is."

"Go on."

"Rotation first. Getting it to turn, and to keep turning, without a
hand on it. That took months and it's the one everybody thinks is the
whole technique because it's the one you can see."

"Then?"

"Then power. More of it, faster, without the rotation coming apart.
That's arithmetic and stubbornness and it went quicker." He looked out
at the treeline. "Then containment, and that took longer than the other
two put together, and it very nearly didn't work."

"Why."

"Because rotation and power both want to go outward and containment
wants them not to, and there's no version where you get the first two
right and the third one follows. The third one is a separate problem
wearing the same coat." Kakashi's hand went back in his pocket. "The
first two are yours. The third one is the material's, or the air's, or
whatever it is that's at the outside of the thing — and it either takes
it or it doesn't."

Sazare did not say anything for about four seconds.

"Say that last part again," he said.

"Which part."

"*The third one is the material's.*"

"That's not how he'd have put it. That's how I've put it, standing in a
field, twenty-odd years later, to a fabricator." Kakashi looked down at
him. "He said something like: the first two years I was learning what I
could do and the third year I was learning what it would let me."

"Would let you."

"Would let him."

---

Sazare got the notebook out with his left hand and transferred it,
which he had stopped noticing he did.

"That's my four per cent," he said.

"Kanzaki mentioned. Something about rocks."

"It's not about rocks." He was writing fast. "Reject rates by starting
medium. Twenty-two per cent for yard aggregate, thirty-one for granite,
forty-four for site rubble, four for crystal I grew myself. And the
finding is that time isn't for the maker — the material takes up part
of the instruction, and the reason a master looks fast is that he can
get it to take it up quickly. I put that in front of the Hokage in the
third month at four in the morning."

"And?"

"And he got there in the third year of the Rasengan and nobody wrote it
down as a finding, because he wasn't a materials man, he was a man
trying to make a technique work, so it went in as *what it would let
me* and stayed there for twenty years as a turn of phrase." Sazare
stopped writing. "It's the same law. In air. With no medium in it at
all."

"Is that good news or bad news?"

"I have absolutely no idea," said Sazare, "and I'd like about a
fortnight."

---

Kakashi walked him back, the long way, which he always did.

They were most of the way down the hill before he said the rest of it,
and he said it in the flat register he used for camber and for six
short faces and for a field that had killed twenty-two people, and
Sazare understood by now exactly what that register was for.

"The part you should be looking at isn't the three years," he said.

"No?"

"Anybody with enough time and enough stubbornness can spend three years
on something. It's rarer than it should be and it isn't remarkable."
Kakashi had his hands in his pockets. "The remarkable part is the
second thing he did, which nobody talks about, because it isn't
dramatic and there's no scroll for it."

"Which is."

"He broke it into three stages so it could be handed to somebody else."

Sazare stopped walking.

"That's not the same act," said Kakashi. "That's a second invention and
in my opinion it's the harder one. When you build a thing yourself, you
don't build it in stages — you build it in a mess, in the wrong order,
with four dead ends, and the dead ends are load-bearing because you had
to walk down them to know they were dead. Turning that into three
rungs somebody else can climb means going back through your own mess
and working out which parts were the road and which parts were you
being lost."

"Most people wouldn't."

"Most people can't," said Kakashi. "And a lot of the ones who can,
don't, because the mess is the only proof that it was difficult, and if
you hand somebody three clean stages they'll do it in eight months and
think you were lucky."

---

Sazare walked the rest of the hill with the whole of it going round.

*"You can't put a ladder in a book. You can only put the rungs, and a
man who learns off rungs will fall the first time he needs to reach."*

*Urushibara Gen, to a twenty-year-old who wanted to write the sealwright
side down in stages so it could be taught, in front of two other people,
two years ago.*

*He was wrong.*

*He was not wrong about the risk and he was not wrong about the rungs.
He was wrong about the arithmetic, and the proof is that the most
respected technique of the last hundred years was put into three stages
by the man who invented it, deliberately, and has since been taught to
at least four people including a boy of fifteen, and the thing has not
degraded.*

*And Urushibara knew it. He must have known it — he was seventy, he
lived in this village, he taught at that Academy, the Fourth was
Hokage while he was working.*

*So it was not an argument about pedagogy. He said the pedagogical thing
because it was available.*

He stopped at the corner by the dye-works, in the last of the light.

*She said it in front of two people. "The most convenient thing a man
has ever said about his own indispensability."*

*She was right, and she knew she was right, and that is why it went two
years instead of two weeks — because he could not be told, and she
could not unsay it, and both of them knew which one of them had the
better of it.*

*And then he did it anyway, in the eleventh month, in a cold room, four
evenings, retainer first, for me.*

*I have known that for six days.*

*She has known it for eleven, and she has not been in the yard since
Thursday, and she said next week some time and did not name a day.*
