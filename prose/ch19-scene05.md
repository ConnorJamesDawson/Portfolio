# Chapter 19, Scene 5 — Further Off

*Act 3, part A. Tazuna's house, Nami. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~2,600 words.*

---

He went on the Sunday, which was three days after Haku told him to, and he
went for both of the reasons Haku had given him and was clear with himself
about both.

The house stood at the end of the plank walk on its own piles with the
water under it, two storeys, weatherboarded, the boards gone the silver of
everything in that town, and a lamp lit in the downstairs window at four
in the afternoon because the sun goes off that side of the bay early. The
tide was up. It knocked and sucked under the floor the whole evening, the
way it did at the loft, and the smell coming up through the boards was
mud, weed, and woodsmoke.

Tsunami had him in before he had finished saying he had brought her the
handle for the water bucket, and had a bowl in his hands within four
minutes, and would not hear one word about him having eaten.

Tazuna was out on the bridge and would be until dark.

The four of them were up in the woods.

The man with the crutch was asleep upstairs and had been, according to
Tsunami, for most of two days, in the manner of a man making up an
enormous debt.

So Sazare sat on the floor of a warm room with a bowl of rice and grilled
mackerel and a dish of the pickled thing everybody in Nami made and nobody
outside it would eat, and did not meet a single one of them, and read the
entire team off the floor of the room in about ninety seconds.

Four bedrolls along the wall under the window, all four put away for the
day, and they had been put away by four different people:

One rolled square and strapped, ends squared off, laid parallel to the
wall at a hand's width, with a folded spare shirt on top of it and nothing
else. Somebody had done that in about forty seconds and would do it every
day for the rest of their life.

One that was not rolled at all so much as *heaped*, with a sock in it, and
shoved against the first one at an angle, and a bright orange jacket
hanging over the fire-rail above the hearth steaming gently and giving off
a smell of wet dog and pine.

One rolled neatly but slowly — you could tell; the strapping had been
gone round twice and adjusted — with a comb and a little round mirror on
the sill above it, and beside the mirror a hair-band and a tin of
something and, tucked behind the tin where a person puts a thing they are
faintly embarrassed to have, a small book with a bookmark two thirds of
the way through.

And the fourth, by the door.

Not rolled. Not touched. Lying open where its owner had got out of it, in
the one position in that entire room that covered both the door and the
stair, at an angle, with the crutch propped against the wall beside it and
a pair of sandals set toes-outward.

Sazare looked at that fourth bedroll over his rice for a while.

*He hasn't tidied it in eight days,* he thought, *and he laid it there on
the first night without thinking about it, and the two facts are the same
fact. He is spending nothing at all on anything that isn't the fight, and
he'd rather be caught out on a made bed than a foot further from that
door.*

---

The photograph was on the wall by the stair and he had seen it within the
first minute and had not looked at it twice, because he did not need to.

Four people on a jetty in the sun. Tazuna, ten years younger, without the
beard. Tsunami, laughing, holding a much smaller Inari on her hip. And the
right-hand quarter of the print torn away, raggedly, by hand, in a curve —
not cut, torn, from the edge inward — so that what remained of the fourth
person was part of a shoulder and one arm going out of frame with a hand
on the top of the boy's head.

He ate his rice and did not look at it and was aware of it the entire
time, the way you are aware of a door.

Inari came down at about the sixth hour with the frame under his arm.

He did not say hello. He came and stood at the end of the low table with
his white hat still on indoors and put the frame down on the boards in
front of Sazare, and it was a different frame — an empty one, cheap
lacquer over softwood, with two of the mitres sprung and the glass gone
and a long split down one side where somebody had put a thumb through it.

"Can you do that," he said. It was not a question and it was not polite.

"Probably." Sazare put his bowl down and picked it up and turned it over
twice. "Yes. The mitres want re-cutting and I'll have to make a new fillet
for that side, and I can't do the glass, there isn't any in this town."

"I don't need the glass."

"All right."

"It's not for anything," said Inari, and sat down four feet away with his
back against the post and his arms round his knees and did not go away.

---

Sazare worked on it for about forty minutes on the floor of that room with
the lamp and the fire and the tide going under the boards.

He knew what he had been handed and it took no working out at all. It was
a cheap frame and it was empty and it was the wrong size for the
photograph on the wall by about two inches in the long dimension, and a
nine-year-old who has torn a quarter off a picture does not go looking for
a frame for it afterwards. This one had come out of a cupboard or off a
shelf where it had been since before any of it, and it had been broken for
a long time, and there was dust in the split.

The boy had brought him the nearest thing to hand that had a reason to be
mended.

So Sazare mended it, and took his time, and said nothing.

He cut the two sprung mitres back true with the small saw and glued them
and pinned them, and let that stand while he made the fillet: a strip of
the offcut larch, planed down and down until it was the thickness of a
fingernail, tried in the split four times, taken out, taken down again.
Inari watched the whole of the fillet business without moving. When Sazare
finally slid it home and it went in with the resistance of a thing that
fits, and the split closed and stayed closed, the boy made a small
involuntary noise and then was extremely still, as though he had been
caught at something.

"You're going to leave," said Inari, at about the thirtieth minute.

"Yes."

"When?"

"I don't know exactly. Soon."

"Everyone leaves." The boy said it to his own knees. "That's what happens.
People come and they say things and then they go."

"Yes," said Sazare.

He did not look up while he said it and he did not soften it, because it
was true, and because the boy had said it as a test and would have known
instantly if it had been answered with anything else.

Inari's arms came a little tighter round his knees.

"Those ones are going to die," he said.

"I don't know."

"They *are*. They're stupid. The yellow one's stupid, he shouts all the
time and he can't even climb a tree, and the old one's got a *stick*." His
voice went up and cracked and came back down. "They think they can. They
all think they can. And then he sends the men and then it doesn't matter
what anybody thinks."

Sazare set the frame down flat on the boards and put his palm on it to
hold the fillet while the glue took, and looked at the grain of the floor
between his knees.

"I don't know," he said again.

---

It went quiet for a while. The fire ticked. Out in the kitchen part
Tsunami was doing something with a pan and a great deal of water, loudly,
and Sazare understood that she was doing it loudly on purpose and had been
for some minutes.

Then Inari said, in a completely different voice — small, flat,
unarmoured, the way you ask a person a question when you have decided they
are not going to bother lying to you:

"Does it stop?"

Sazare sat on the floor of that room with his palm on a mended frame.

There were, he found, a great many things available. He went through them
in about two seconds, which was the fastest and most complete piece of
thinking he did in the whole of that year, and every one of them was on
the shelf and within reach and would have gone straight into the boy like
water into dry ground.

*It gets better.*

*You'll be all right.*

*They're stronger than you think.*

*He'd want you to.*

All of them the sort of thing an adult says. All of them free. All of them
would have worked, that evening, on a nine-year-old, for at least a week.

And every single one of them was a thing Sazare Ruri did not know to be
true, and there was a man twenty-two months in the ground up a valley in
the Land of Hot Water who had never once in two years said a thing to him
that he did not know to be true, and it was the only thing of that man's
he had managed to keep, and he had kept it through a lime kiln and a
noodle shop and a shingle spit at Kasado, and he found — sitting on a warm
floor with a lamp going and a child four feet away asking him the only
question he had — that he could not put it down for this.

"No," said Sazare.

Inari did not move.

"It doesn't stop," said Sazare. "I'd tell you if it did." He kept his hand
on the frame. "It gets further away. That's all that happens. You end up
standing a long way off from it, and from a long way off it looks smaller,
and it isn't smaller, it's the same size it always was and you're just
further away. And some days you're closer than others and you don't get to
choose which."

The tide went under the floor.

"That's it?" said Inari.

"That's what I know."

"That's rubbish."

"Yes," said Sazare.

The boy got up. He did it fast and badly, banging his heel on the post,
and stood there for a second with his fists down at his sides and his face
absolutely rigid under the white hat.

"Then what's the *point*," he said — not shouting; worse than shouting;
saying it, at a stranger, on the floor of his mother's house — "of any of
it? What's the point of you doing your — of *mending* things, and being
nice, and — if it doesn't—"

And he stopped, because he had run out, and he went up the stairs two at a
time and the door at the top shut.

Sazare sat on the floor with his hand on a frame that had four minutes to
go on the glue.

---

Tsunami came in from the kitchen part after a while and did not say
anything about it at all, which he was grateful for and which he
understood to be a very deliberate piece of kindness.

She gave him tea. She looked at the mended frame and turned it over and
put her thumb on the fillet, in exactly the place his mother would have
put a thumb, and said, "You've made it better than it was."

"It's a bad frame."

"It's his father's," said Tsunami.

Sazare put his cup down.

"Not the picture," she said. "Kaiza had it in his kit on the boats — it
was his mother's and there was never anything in it, he used to say he was
saving it. It's been in that cupboard four years and Inari's had it out of
there about nine times and put it back." She set it down on the sill,
square, where the lamp got at it. "He didn't ask you to mend it for the
frame."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Tsunami. "That's why I let him bring it down."

---

He heard the other half of it four days later, at the water queue, from
two women who had it from Tsunami and were not being especially quiet
about it.

There had been a row at that house on the Tuesday night. A proper one. The
boy had said something at the table about them all being stupid and about
how they were all going to die, and had cried, and had gone out; and the
yellow-haired one had gone after him and had *shouted* at him — everybody
had heard it, the whole plank walk had heard it — and had said something
about his own life, something about nobody ever having been there at all,
and had told him to stop crying, and had gone off into the woods
afterwards on his own and not come back till morning.

And the boy had been different since. Up early. Out with his grandfather.
Carrying things.

Sazare stood in the queue with a kettle in his hand and the gulls going
over and the smell of wet tar off the quay and did the arithmetic, because
he was not able not to.

*I gave him the truth. I checked it four ways first and I gave him the
only thing I had that I knew to be true and I gave it to him carefully and
I did not lie to him about one single word of it.*

*And it went into him like a nail.*

*And a twelve-year-old in an orange coat who cannot climb a tree, who has
never in his life had a thought about anything, who I have watched fail at
the same trunk eleven times in twenty minutes and who I wrote down as dead
inside two years — he went out after that child and shouted at him with no
evidence whatsoever, and made him cry, and I do not think he had one clear
idea in his head about what he was doing.*

*And it worked.*

He carried the kettle home along the plank walk with the tide out and the
heron standing in the mud where it always stood, and climbed the stairs,
and sat down at the window; and was still sitting there when the light
went, turning it over, unable to make it come out any other way.

He had been right about the boy. Every observation was correct. Every
single item of the evidence was correct, and the assessment built out of it
was sound, and he had watched it fail with his own eyes and could not find
the error in it anywhere.

*There is something I do not have,* thought Sazare Ruri, at the window,
above the mud, in the dark, at sixteen years and one month old, *and it is
not a technique, and I have not got the first idea where you would go to
be taught it.*
