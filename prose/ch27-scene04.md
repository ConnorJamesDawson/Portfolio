# Chapter 27, Scene 4 — The Terms

*Act 4, part B. Konoha General. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen.
POV: Sazare. ~1,906 words.*

---

The village buried the Third Hokage on a wet morning six days after the
invasion.

The hospital could not send everybody. Sister Kaburagi selected twelve staff
by drawing names from a dressing bowl and then removed three whose patients
were unstable. Sazare's name was not in it. He remained in the men's ward with
Nurse Emiko, two students and thirty-one patients while rain ran down the
repaired window and found the place where the gauze packing ended.

Handa had predicted one rain.

Water gathered on the inner sill in a line no wider than thread.

"Don't," Emiko said when she saw Sazare looking at it.

"It's wet."

"You are not repairing a window from an evacuation chair during a state
funeral."

"Those conditions are unrelated."

"I have been speaking to Yagi."

She put a folded towel on the sill.

At eleven the memorial bells reached the hospital. Everyone who could stand
did so. Everyone who could not remained where they were, which made the
gesture less equal and more honest. Sazare put both feet down, braced his
hands on the chair arms and rose until the repaired knee took part of his
weight.

It held in the centre.

He stood for the first three bells. On the fourth, the inner thigh began to
shake. On the fifth, Handa said, "Sit down before you make this about you."

Sazare sat.

The bells continued to thirty-seven.

Between the nineteenth and twentieth, the fever-ward doors opened and Fujita
came out still wearing his mask. He stood in the corridor rather than entering
the ward. At the twenty-third, one of the students began crying without sound
and continued holding a patient's water cup level. At the thirtieth, somebody
in the women's end said the Third's name aloud.

The bells did not distinguish the Hokage from the shinobi and civilians being
buried with him that morning. Thirty-seven was ceremonial, not numerical. The
village's actual dead exceeded the number of sounds it could give them.

After the last bell, work resumed without announcement. Emiko collected
medicine cups. The student wiped her face on her shoulder because both hands
were occupied. Sazare lifted the observation board and found that bed three's
temperature was due.

Grief in a hospital received the time not required by the living.

He knew very little about Sarutobi Hiruzen. An old man had read the top sheet
of his file on Wednesdays, asked questions about the parts nobody thought
relevant, and signed a medical ruling in his own hand. The personal attention
had looked like distrust. Only after his death did the administrative space
around Sazare reveal what else it had been.

Root requested access to the crystal evidence on the morning after the
funeral.

The request did not name Root. It came from a special operations office under
an emergency recovery authority and asked for all retained substrate,
fabrication records and the patient himself for technical debriefing.
Hiruzen's standing instruction that inquiries concerning Sazare Ruri pass
through his desk had nowhere to pass.

Kōji brought the request to the ward rather than executing it.

"Is this part of the offer?" Sazare asked.

"It is one reason the offer now has a deadline."

The special-operations request included transport authority despite his
medical restriction. It proposed examination at an undisclosed secure facility
for a period "not expected to exceed seventy-two hours" and gave no name for
the person responsible for returning him. His designation in the first
paragraph was *crystal-release subject*.

"Would they take me while injured?" Sazare asked.

"They have medical personnel," Kōji said.

"That isn't an answer."

"Yes. If the Council authorised it."

"Would the Council?"

"Three days ago, no. Today, I cannot promise. Emergency authority rewards
arguments that begin with what might happen next time."

Sazare read the signature block. The issuing officer's name was absent beneath
an office seal.

"Does accepting prevent them asking again?"

"No. It gives your commander grounds to refuse and creates a record if refusal
is overridden."

Protection, in Konoha, repeatedly took the form of making harmful action
require more signatures.

"You said there wasn't one."

"There was no disclosed deadline. Circumstances have disclosed it."

On the table beside the headband lay the revised integration document. Clause
eleven now exempted material under hospital disposal authority. A new schedule
identified the reverse-recall technique as pre-existing work disclosed upon
integration rather than a technique owned through service. Konoha retained
operational secrecy and deployment authority; Ruri retained authorship and the
right to continue independent study under supervision.

It was better than the first offer.

It still made his work available to the village.

"If I refuse?" he asked.

"Your amnesty remains legally valid. The Council will review your restrictions
and security classification. The special operations request will be considered
during that review."

"And if I accept?"

"You enter the ordinary shinobi chain. Special operations cannot remove
hospital personnel for technical examination without mission authority,
medical clearance and your commander's approval."

"Who is my commander?"

"Initially, the hospital operations office while on duty. Outside duty,
provisional personnel administration until evaluation."

"Kaburagi."

"For practical purposes."

From the desk, Kaburagi said, "I heard that."

Kōji had chosen the meeting location carefully.

"You are offering ownership as protection from ownership," Sazare said.

"I am offering one institution with rules you can read instead of another
whose rules I cannot show you."

"You work for the first one."

"Yes."

"So that answer is interested."

"Also yes."

The honesty cost Kōji nothing visible and made refusal harder.

Sazare read the fourteen clauses again. The headband remained on the bedside
table, moved twice daily for cleaning and returned to the same square of wood.
Nobody had asked him to wear it. Patients had stopped asking whether he would.

"Combat training," he said. "Who decides what is taught?"

"The evaluation office assigns an instructor. You may object on safety or
conflict grounds."

"May I refuse bloodline instruction?"

"From Konoha?"

"From anyone."

Kōji understood the other name. "Yes, unless ordered during an immediate
operational emergency."

"Put it in."

"That clause is unusually specific."

"So is the person."

Kōji wrote in the margin.

"Hospital post."

"Retained."

"Not conditional on producing medical crystal."

"Your employment is conditional on performing hospital duties. Your technique
is not prescribed."

"Put that in."

He wrote again.

"The quota remains four hundred grams."

"Yes."

"Combat output cannot be counted against it without my agreement."

"Why?"

"Otherwise every deployment can create quota debt."

Kōji looked at the page. "Reasonable."

"Put it in."

By the end, the document contained six handwritten amendments, each initialled
by Kōji and witnessed by Kaburagi. It did not give Ruri freedom. It gave names
to several restraints and placed boundaries around others. The production
quota remained. Deployment remained compulsory. The village could order him
outside the walls, decide where his fabricated material went and punish
refusal.

It could also train him, rank him, pay him, treat his injuries, place him
inside a command structure and be required to record when it used him.

"I need Inoichi to read it," Sazare said.

"He has."

"With me."

Kōji folded the document. "Tomorrow."

The next afternoon Inoichi read every clause aloud. He explained three ways
the mission-authority language could be abused, two ways the medical exemption
might fail during wartime and one way the authorship schedule protected pride
without protecting control.

"Do you recommend it?" Sazare asked.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the previous arrangement was made for a person Konoha believed might
be a passive foreign asset. That person no longer exists administratively,
whether or not you sign. Refusing will not make them forget what happened in
the street. It will only leave you outside the rules they use to govern it."

"And personally?"

Inoichi looked through the ward screen at the beds, trolley, repaired window
and evacuation chair.

"You already chose," he said. "This is deciding whether to write it down."

"If I sign because the other office frightens me, that isn't choosing Konoha."

"No."

"If I sign because the hospital is here, that isn't choosing the command
structure."

"No."

"If I sign, they become the same decision on paper."

Inoichi pulled the chair closer and sat. "Institutions are good at making
several motives look like one signature. You do not owe the form a pure
reason."

"What do I owe it?"

"Understanding what authority you are granting. After that, yourself,
presumably."

"Presumably."

"I am a mind-walker, not a priest."

Sazare read clause two again. Hospital command while on duty. He imagined
Kaburagi receiving a request to surrender a patient for technical examination
and understood the precise quality of the refusal it would encounter.

The choice was not between ownership and freedom. Freedom had not appeared on
either page. It was between an arrangement whose people had names and one
whose issuing officer did not.

Sazare disliked the sentence because it might be true.

Ashida witnessed the signature. His right arm remained in a sling, so he held
the page flat with his left while Sazare wrote his name. Sister Kaburagi
signed for the hospital. Kōji signed for the provisional administration.

Nobody applauded.

Ashida turned the completed document over and filled the administrative cover
sheet. Previous status: conditional resident, foreign amnesty probationer,
restricted bloodline holder. New status: Konoha shinobi personnel, provisional
grade, hospital assignment, operational suspension pending evaluation.

The categories occupied four lines. The person did not move between them until
Ashida stamped the bottom.

"That's it?" Sazare asked.

"There are copies," Ashida said. "Seven, I think. Personnel, hospital,
intelligence, mission office, finance, gate administration and one for
whichever room eats forms."

"Archives," Kōji said.

"That's what I said."

The finance copy established genin base pay from that date. It was less than
Sazare had earned on a good contract in Kanezaki and more than the hospital
paid him for lifting. Hazard supplements applied only when formally deployed,
which meant nothing he had done during the invasion qualified.

"The commendation includes a payment," Kōji said when Ashida noticed.

"For heroism?" Sazare asked.

"For voluntary defence resulting in preservation of critical infrastructure."

"The public works damage claim is larger."

"Yes."

"Does the commendation pay O-Shige's excess?"

"No."

"Can it?"

Kōji looked at Kaburagi. She looked towards the repaired window.

"It is your payment," Kōji said.

Sazare wrote an assignment instruction beneath the amount: first to
outstanding hospital-approach civilian damage claims, remainder to the
hospital casualty fund.

"You are permitted to keep money," Ashida said.

"I know."

"Do you?"

"I have kept some before."

"Name an occasion."

Sazare could not immediately do so. Ashida sighed and witnessed the assignment
as well.

Kōji placed the headband in front of him.

"Effective immediately for administrative purposes," he said. "Operational
status remains suspended pending medical clearance and evaluation."

"Rank?"

"Provisional genin."

Eight years late, in a foreign country, from people who did not know what they
were handing him.

Sazare picked up the headband.

The cloth was too long for his upper arm and too short to go comfortably over
the knee splint. He tied it around the handle of his medical bag, metal plate
facing inward so it would not strike the bed frame.

Kōji looked at it.

"There is no regulation specifying where," Sazare said.

"No," Kōji said. "There isn't."

Sister Kaburagi handed Sazare the observation sheets for beds one through six.

"Since you are now effective immediately for administrative purposes," she
said, "you can be effective immediately at observations. Chair only."

He put the bag beneath the seat and went back to work.
