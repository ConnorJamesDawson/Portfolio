# Chapter 72, Scene 9 — Upstream

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the tower, the second floor. Tsunade, age
fifty-three. POV: Tsunade. ~1200 words.*

---

He came home on the Tuesday smelling of eggs and zelkova
shavings, an hour past the hair, and told her the whole of it
before the fifty passes — the audit, the workshop, the chair,
the finding delivered level across a table in the old quarter —
because he reported everything, and because this report, he
said, was owed to her twice: once as the standing rule, and once
because she was in the chain of custody.

"I told him he was the largest creditor in my ledger," said
Sazare. "I did not tell him he was the largest in yours. That
entry is not mine to read out. But I have seen the shape of it
for months, and tonight I am formally noting that I know it is
there."

And then he did her hair, fifty passes, and went to sleep the
way he went to sleep — completely, trustingly, mid-thought,
one hand finding her sleeve — and left her awake with the lamp
and the register and an entry she had been not-writing for six
years.

*Fine,* she wrote. *Tonight, then. The upstream entry. He
audited his channels and found the brat at the head of all of
them, and he's right, and he doesn't know the half of it,
because his ledger starts at the village gate. Mine starts in
a bar in Tanzaku with a bottle in front of it.*

*I was done. The register can carry the whole phrase for
once: not grieving, not resting, not "taking time" — done.
Everything I had ever put my hands to had died of being
loved by me, and I had run the only audit I was capable of
running in those years, which was the drunk one, nightly, and
it returned the same finding nightly: the village took
Nawaki, the war took Dan, the hat was a curse people wore
before dying disappointed, and the last of the Senju was
going to sit in gambling towns turning her inheritance into
losing slips until the arithmetic finished with her too. I
had a genius apprentice keeping me alive and a necklace in a
box like a loaded weapon, and I was DONE, and everyone who
had ever had standing to argue with me about it was in the
ground.*

*And then a twelve-year-old called me a coward in a street.*

*A loud, graceless, badly-dressed twelve-year-old with
Minato's face and Kushina's everything else, who had every
excuse this world manufactures — orphaned by the fox, hated
for the cage, raised by nobody, lied to about all of it —
and who had somehow come out of that furnace carrying the
exact faith this village had buried with my grandfather. He
wanted the hat. Not the way the ambitious want it — the way
Nawaki wanted it, the way Dan wanted it, wanted it so hard
I heard both their voices come out of his mouth and had to
sit down. And he called me a coward for laughing at the
dream, and he was RIGHT, and I bet him a week against it —
one week, one Rasengan, impossible odds — because I needed
him to lose so I could go back to being done in peace.*

*He didn't lose. First bet I ever won by losing. I hung the
curse around his neck — worst thing I've ever done sober,
some nights I still think that; the two before him died with
it on — and he wears it like a festival prize. And I came
home. Picked the hat off the pile of my dead and put it on
because a child in an orange jacket had decided I was going
to, and the concept of me declining did not survive contact.*

*Everything since runs downstream of that street. The
administration. The war winter survived. The hospital
rebuilt — which trained the medics — which staffed the ward —
which had, on its second floor, a respiratory case teaching
seals to a boy from Kiri that my hospital had taken in
because my hospital had a policy, MY policy, the one I
signed in my first month with the hat I only wore because—*

*So the fabricator's audit is correct and understated. He
descends from the brat. So do I. The entire life I am
living — the desk, the line, the Thursdays, the daylight
treaty, the man asleep beside me with his hand in my
sleeve, all of it, every article — descends from one
twelve-year-old refusing to let a drunk woman rot on
schedule.*

*And on Sunday that boy sat at my table with a pot of
amended hotpot, worried there wouldn't be enough food, and
asked, at the door, in the voice he thinks sounds casual,
whether he was invited-invited.*

*Invited-invited. Gods.*

*The chair was his before he sat in it. I said it as a
hostess line and it is the truest sentence I produced all
month: that chair, this table, this tower, this WOMAN — his
before he ever sat down, bought and paid for in a street in
Tanzaku with a week's faith at impossible odds. The
fabricator ran his audit and found his largest creditor.
Fine. I ran mine tonight, at last, six years late, and here
is the finding, entered in ink, where nobody will read it:*

*same creditor. The whole family is leveraged to one
sixteen-year-old, and he thinks he got invited to dinner as
a thank-you for hotpot.*

*Payment schedule, since audits without remediation are
just guilt with a ruler: the interest gets paid the way it
has been paid — the table, the chair, the noons of his own
he doesn't know I've started planning, seven weeks of the
best trauma care of my career, and every door of mine open
to him for as long as I hold doors. The principal—*

She looked at the window, at the dark where the monument
stood, four faces and the space the mountain kept.

*—the principal gets paid the day I hand him the hat. Not
soon. He isn't ready; the village isn't; there's a war
coming first, and gods know what it costs us all. But I
have watched the succession from both sides now — watched
what this office does to the ones who want it wrong, and
buried the ones who wanted it right — and I am telling
this register what I have told no one, not Shizune, not
Jiraiya, not the man asleep in my bed with his ledgers:*

*the fifth face on that mountain believes there will be a
sixth, and knows its name, and has known since a street in
Tanzaku, and is going to spend every year between now and
then making sure the hat he gets is worth wanting — a
village with its accounts in order, its women in decent
underwear, its walls sound, its people fed at tables with
enough chairs.*

*That's the estate I'm keeping for him. He brought me back
to it. It's his.*

*Chart closed. Fifty-six on the boy's count, per his entry
tonight — he showed me the both-columns finding like a man
showing a supernova. Both columns. Family cooks.*

*The brat has no idea what he built.*

*They never do. That's how it works. You just do the thing.*
