# Chapter 52, Scene 5 — Six Chapters

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the village library and the quartermaster's
yard. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,218 words.*

---

He got there on the twenty-seventh and he got there from the wrong end,
which was where he got everything.

*She cannot offer.*

*She told me so in this yard on the third of this month and it took her
eleven days to say it and she said it badly on purpose because badly
was all she had. Four hundred and eleven years and not once. Not a
gift, not a suggestion, not a shall-I, because there has never been a
shape in which that would go.*

*I cannot compliment. It has been in a column since the ninth month of
last year with the word "cannot" against it.*

*Same fault. Opposite side. Neither of us was ever given the chance to
practise and both of us have made an entire method out of going round
it.*

He sat with that for a while.

*And I got a book.*

*In the ninth month of last year I stood at a desk and could not ask
for it, and a man of seventy-eight got off a stool, which took a
moment, and put one in my hand, and I have paid four chapters of it
since and there is a fourteen-year-old in that hospital who built me a
marking scheme off the back of it.*

*Nobody has ever given her anything.*

*Chapter four. State: refused, five times, by me. Reason known. Not
resolved.*

---

The problem was the object and it took him two days.

*An article means nothing to her. That is the whole of the difficulty
and it is not sentiment, it is physics: she divides. Anything I make
she can be beside, and anything she wants to keep she keeps by being
it, and a thing on a shelf is a thing that requires somebody to have a
shelf.*

*Everything in chapter four assumes a person with a room.*

*So: not an article.*

*Which leaves what I actually have, which is that I know one thing she
does not and it was given to me by a librarian on a Tuesday.*

---

Ochiai Sumiko was in Handa's chair with the cardigan re-elbowed in a
third colour, and he listened to the whole of it without interrupting,
which took about four minutes.

Then he said: "For a slug."

"Yes."

"The one on your shoulder in the eighth month that had this building
in an uproar for a week."

"That's her."

"Right." Ochiai looked at the ceiling. "And you want the copy off the
low shelf, permanently, out of the building, to a place with no
address, to be given to a creature that does not read."

"She knows what reading is. She's never done it." Sazare kept his hands
still. "And I know it isn't a thing a library does. I'm not asking you
to bend anything — if the answer's no I'll find a copy in the fourth
quarter and buy it out of the jewellery money and it'll take me two
months and that's fine."

"Mm." Ochiai got the card box out and went through it with a thumb
that had spots on it and had not lost the copperplate. "Nine hundred
and eleven days on the shelf since it was last out."

"That was me."

"That was you, and the four before that were also you, and the entry
before that is nineteen sixty-one." He put the card down. "It has been
on that shelf for forty-one years and it has been out four times and
every one of them was a young man who did not want to be seen asking
for it. It is not doing anything here."

"So—"

"So I shall strike it off the accession as *withdrawn, condition*,
which is a lie, and I shall enjoy that considerably more than a man of
seventy-nine ought to, and you are not to tell anybody for at least
four years." He wrote something in the box, in copperplate, unhurried.
"Take it. And Ruri—"

"Yes."

"When you asked me for it the first time you could not get the sentence
out and I had to get off the stool." Ochiai closed the box. "You have
just come in here and asked me for a book in order to give it away, out
loud, in about four sentences, and you did not once say the word
*friendship* in a way that required me to look somewhere else."

"...That's chapter one."

"I have no idea what chapter it is," said Ochiai. "I have been watching
you for four years and that is the whole of my remark."

---

He gave it to her on the twenty-eighth, in the yard, at about nine.

He had thought about how for two days and had constructed eleven
approaches and discarded all of them, and in the end he put it on the
felt in front of her and said:

"That's yours."

Katsuyu did not move.

"...I do not understand."

"It's a book. It's the one I was given in the ninth month of last year
and it's why I can do about half the things I can do with people, and
I've paid four of its six chapters and been marked on all four by a
fifteen-year-old, and it's yours."

"Yours means—"

"Yours. To keep. Not to look at here." He kept his voice ordinary,
which took some doing. "I can't give you an article. You divide. A
thing on a shelf needs somebody with a shelf and everything in chapter
four assumes a person with a room. So this is what I've got, and I've
got it because somebody gave it to me when I couldn't ask, and it's the
only thing I own that was given rather than made."

There was a silence of some length.

"Nobody has given me anything," said Katsuyu.

"No."

"Not in—" She stopped. "I was going to give you a number and I find
that I do not have one, because it is not a thing that has happened
rarely, it is a thing that has not happened. I have been given
*instructions*. I have been given a great many instructions and several
of them were kind."

"I know."

"I do not know what to do."

"You don't have to do anything," said Sazare. "That's chapter four,
page sixty-one — *a gift that requires a response is not a gift, it is
an invoice.* It's the only line in the whole book I've never had to
argue with."

---

She asked him to read the contents page, which he had not expected and
which took him about four seconds to understand: she was going to carry
it four hundred miles and she wanted to know what she was carrying.

So he read it out in a cold yard at nine at night.

"*Of Presence, and the going where people are.*"

"...Ah," said Katsuyu.

"What?"

"I cannot do that one. I go where I am called. I have gone where I am
called for four hundred and eleven years and I have never in my life
*gone where people are*, and I have been sitting in this yard for two
months because you asked me on Fridays, which I now think may be the
only time it has ever happened." A pause. "Go on."

"*Of Speech without Object.*"

"That is the one where you talk about nothing."

"That's the one I couldn't do at all."

"You did it in a gateway on the fourteenth," said Katsuyu. "I was
there."

Sazare did not say anything for a moment, and then read the next one.

"*Of Inquiry, and the asking after a person's affairs.*"

"Oh," said Katsuyu. "Is *that* a chapter?"

"Page thirty-one to forty-four."

"I have been doing that all year. I have been doing it since the first
morning in her office and I did not know there was a name for it and I
did not know it was a thing that could be done wrongly." She sounded,
for the first time in two months, genuinely rattled. "I have asked you
about drains."

"You have."

"Was that—"

"That was the best morning I've had in three years," said Sazare, "and
I wrote it down that night and I have read it about nine times since."

---

"*Of Gifts*," he said.

Katsuyu did not say anything at all.

He waited, and after about four seconds understood, and had the sense
not to explain it, and read the next one.

"*Of the Hand and the Shoulder.*"

"...I have no shoulders."

"No."

"Is the chapter of any use to me whatsoever?"

"It's about touch," said Sazare. "Ordinary contact between people who
aren't doing anything else. The book says it's a language of about nine
words that most people learn before they can speak, and that anybody
who hasn't will find it almost impossible to learn from a description."

"That is the situation I am in with all six of these."

"That's the situation everybody's in with all six of these. That's why
it's a bad book." He turned the page round even though she could not
read it. "It's a bad book, Katsuyu. I want to say that before you carry
it anywhere. It's forty-one years old and it was written by a man who
thought about this a very great deal and had nobody to check him, and
about a third of it is wrong and another third is only true if you're
already most of the way there.

"It is also the only one that exists, and a fifteen-year-old improved
it on a staircase inside a fortnight, and I have got further with it
than without it."

"*Of Occasions, and the marking of them*," said Katsuyu.

"That's the sixth."

"You have not read that one out."

"I just did."

"You read it in a different voice," said Katsuyu, "and I should like to
know why, and I am asking that under chapter three, which I am told is
pages thirty-one to forty-four."

---

She took it back to the forest on the second of the eleventh month.

She told him on the last evening of the tenth, in the ordinary way,
having plainly thought about how to say it and having got nowhere.

"I am going to take it home."

"Good."

"I want to say what will happen to it, because I do not think it will
be what you imagine and I would rather you knew before rather than
after." A slow settling. "It will be kept. It will be kept extremely
carefully, in a dry place, and a great many of us will come and look at
it, and it will be there in four hundred years."

"But."

"Nobody will read it."

Sazare put the file down.

"We read when we have to," said Katsuyu. "There are four things written
down that concern us and every one of them is a contract, and they are
read once, carefully, by somebody, and then they are held the way
everything is held.

"But we do not *keep* books. A book is a thing you go back to, and we
do not go back to things — we carry them. That is the whole of our
method and it is a good method and it does not lose very much."

"So the book—"

"The book will be the first thing written down in that forest that
nobody has had to sign," said Katsuyu, "and it will be looked at by
everybody and consulted by nobody, and I will tell them what is in it,
and *that* is what will be kept."

"You'll tell them."

"I have it. You read me the contents page and I have the six of them
and I will have them in four hundred years." A pause. "And then they
will tell each other."

---

He worked out what that meant on the walk home and stopped in the lane
by the dye-works.

*She will tell them. And they will tell each other. And they divide.*

*There is one copy and it is going to a place where nobody will need
it, and the text is going into an oral tradition on the second of the
eleventh month and it is going to move through — I have no idea. A
hundred of them. A thousand. However many there are.*

*And every one of them will have it perfectly and every transmission
will be a person saying it to a person.*

*I have read four accounts of how a thing survives four hundred years
of that and none of them is encouraging.*

He stood in the lane for a while.

*The original will be in a dry place, uncorrupted, four hundred miles
away, being looked at by everybody and consulted by nobody.*

*And whatever is going round that forest in fifty years will not be
what is in that book, and any one of them could walk over and check —
that is the part I cannot get past. It is not that they can't. It is
that it will not occur to anybody that a thing they are holding
perfectly might have stopped being the thing.*

He wrote it down that night and put a heading on it, and the heading
was a joke and he knew it was a joke and he left it standing.

*Provenance: unknown.*
