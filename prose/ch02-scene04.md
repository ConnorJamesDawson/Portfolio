# Chapter 2, Scene 4 — Clean

*Act 1, beat 9. Sazare Ruri, age nine; Kohaku, fifteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,600
words.*

---

Kohaku came back three weeks after the carts.

She came in the middle of the afternoon, which was wrong — she came at lamp-time
or not at all — and she came without stopping to eat anything on the way, which
was wronger. Sazare got to her at the gate and she put her arm out and he ran into
it as usual, and she held on for a second longer than the usual, which he noticed
and did not mention.

She had the earring in. It had gone slightly dull at the hollow end from being
handled.

"You've grown."

"I haven't."

"You have. Look at that." She measured him off against her own shoulder with the
flat of her hand, unfavourably. "Still nothing. Right. Is there food?"

There was food, and she went through it in the usual way, and it was almost the
usual evening, and Sazare had begun to relax into it when Iwao put his bowl down
and said:

"They've had the Momoi."

Nobody answered.

"Off the east road," Iwao said. "Whole household. Tuesday."

"We know," said Ren.

"Do we."

"Everyone knows, Iwao. It was in the market by Wednesday."

"Right," said Iwao. "Right." He picked his bowl back up and put it down again
without eating anything out of it. "And the Yuki, three months back. And the
Kanō, before that, which nobody said a word about because there were only four of
them left by then and nobody could be bothered."

Sazare's mother said, "Not at the table."

"I've been not saying it at the table for two years."

"Then you can manage another night."

"I can't, though," said Iwao. "That's the thing. I find I can't."

---

He was not shouting. That was what Sazare would remember. Iwao shouted about wire
and about gulls and about the price of everything; he did not shout about this. He
put both his hands flat on either side of his bowl and spoke at a volume slightly
below his ordinary one.

"You want to look at who's still standing," he said. "That's all I'm saying. Go
on. Go through them. Who's still here."

"Iwao—"

"The Hōzuki are still here. Nobody's touched the Hōzuki. And they keep to
themselves and they marry each other and half of them can't hold a conversation
with an outsider to save their lives, and there they are, still standing." He
turned his hand over. "And the Yuki thinned out. Two generations of thinning out.
Married anybody who'd have them, moved out to the villages, stopped counting who
was who. And when it came they went like *that*, because there wasn't anything
left of them to hold on to."

"That's not why," said Kohaku.

"It's exactly why."

"It's not remotely why—"

"A clan that's a clan is a thing," said Iwao. "It's a thing with a shape. You can't
pull one piece off a thing with a shape without the rest of it noticing. A clan
that's thinned out isn't a thing, it's a list. And a list is easy." He looked
round the table. "We are a list. There's eight of us and one of us is nine and
one of us is me. That's what we are now."

"And whose fault's that," said Kohaku.

"I didn't say fault."

"You said it four different ways."

"I said," said Iwao, "that we thinned out. And we did. And I'll tell you exactly
when it started if you want the date."

And he did not look at Ren.

He was extremely careful not to look at Ren, and he did it so carefully that
everybody at the table looked at Ren instead, including Sazare, who had not until
that moment understood that his father was in the room in a different way from
everybody else in it.

Ren was eating. He went on eating, steadily, through the whole of it, with the
particular attention of a man doing something else with his hands.

"Iwao," said Hisui.

"I'm not saying a word against him. Not one word. He's a good man and he's better
to you than you deserve and I'd say that to his face any day of the week." Iwao's
voice had gone slightly hoarse. "I'm saying we're eight. I'm saying we were
thirty when I was a boy and we're eight, and the ones that are still standing out
there are the ones that closed the door, and I'd like — I would like, very much,
just once, for somebody in this family to say out loud that that might have had
something to do with anything."

The frames hummed in the next room.

"And the boy can't grow," Iwao said.

---

Sazare felt the whole table go absolutely still around him.

It took him a second to understand that he was the boy, and another second to
understand what had been said about him, and by then his mother had put her
chopsticks down on the rest with a click.

"He's nine," she said.

"Hisui—"

"He's *nine*, and he did four minutes of still water last month, and Mizuho stopped
the class to look at it." Her voice had not changed at all. That was the frightening
part. "So you'll say the rest of it, or you'll stop."

Iwao did not say the rest of it.

He sat with his hands on either side of his bowl and after a while he said, "I'm
sorry, Hisui," which Sazare had never once heard him say to anybody, and Hisui
said "I know," and did not accept it.

---

It was Kohaku who finished it, and she did it about a minute later, when everybody
had begun to breathe again and the thing might have been allowed to settle.

"The Kaguya," she said.

Iwao looked up.

"Three weeks ago. Everybody in this room heard it happen." She had not raised her
voice either; she had her elbows on the table, which she was not allowed to do,
and nobody was going to mention it. "Name me a clan that kept the door shut
tighter than the Kaguya. Go on. They didn't marry out. They didn't *live* out.
They didn't come into the village from one year to the next, they didn't sell
anything to anybody, they had their own — everything. That's your thing with a
shape. That's the shape, exactly the way you want it."

"That's different."

"How?"

"Because they came *in*," said Iwao. "Nobody made them. Nobody was at their door.
They came in and did what they did and got what was coming."

"Yes," said Kohaku. "They did. And that's what a clan that's a thing with a shape
turns into when you shut the door on it for four generations, uncle. It doesn't
turn into the Hōzuki. It turns into a family that walks into a city at two in the
morning for no reason anyone can name and dies to the last man." She sat back.
"You want to be safe by being separate. There isn't a way to be separate that
doesn't end up somewhere. Either they let you alone and you go strange, or they
don't let you alone and there's no one who'll speak for you, because you spent
forty years making sure nobody knew you."

"And selling earrings to the quartermaster keeps us safe, does it?"

"It's kept us safe so far."

"It's kept us *useful* so far," said Iwao. "That's not the same and you know it
isn't."

Kohaku did not answer that.

Sazare, watching, saw her not answer it, and saw her mouth do something, and had
no idea what it meant.

"The Momoi were useful," Iwao said, more quietly. "They did the barracks laundry
for thirty years."

"The Momoi weren't taken for being a clan," said Kohaku. "The Momoi were taken
because Aoi Momoi wouldn't put his name on the intake register in the ninth month
and he told four different people why."

There was a pause of about a second and a half.

"Who told you that?" said Iwao.

"Everyone knows that."

"I didn't know that."

"You don't go anywhere," said Kohaku, and picked up the teapot, and found it
empty, and got up to fill it — and Ren laughed, once, at the *you don't go
anywhere*, because it was funny; and Iwao made a noise that was nearly a laugh
too; and the table came apart into ordinary noise the way tables do, and Sazare's
mother started clearing, and the thing was over.

---

He found Kohaku afterwards out on the step with her boots off, looking at the
yard.

He sat down next to her. She moved over about two inches to make a space that was
already there.

"What's an intake register?"

"It's a list."

"Of what?"

"Of people." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "It's a form. Every
household in the district does one. Names, ages, trades. Uncle's had ours in a
drawer for years."

"Then why wouldn't he put his name on it?"

"Because it changed."

"Changed how?"

Kohaku looked at him.

He got the impression — it was very brief, and he could not have described it, and
he would spend a considerable part of his adult life trying to — that she was
deciding something about him, and that it was not the first time, and that the
answer had come out the same as it always did.

"They put an extra line on it," she said. "That's all. One line at the bottom.
And Aoi Momoi didn't fancy it and said so in a bar, and there's your reason."

"What was the line?"

"Doesn't matter."

"But what *was* it—"

"Zare," said Kohaku, "it genuinely doesn't matter, because we're all going to fill
it in when they ask, and it'll be fine, and if you get any more interesting than
that I'll have to start lying to you and I'd rather not." She knocked her shoulder
into his. "Did you keep the chips?"

"'Course I kept the chips."

"Go and get them."

He went and got them.

They did the game on the step until it got too dark to see the marks, and he found
six families out of twenty-one, which was two better than last time, and she said
"yeah" in exactly the tone she used for "no."
