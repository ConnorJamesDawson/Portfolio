# Chapter 30, Scene 3 — The Pile Behind the Shed

*Act 4, part C. The Hokage's office. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~3,053 words.*

---

It was the same room and almost nothing in it was the same.

The windows were the windows: the whole far curve of them, the roofs
going away red-brown to the trees, the four faces on the cliff straight
ahead at eye level with the tool marks visible. The light came in flat
and hard off a white sky rather than gold off an evening, and the room
smelled of ink and dust and, unmistakably, of sake, not recent.

The desk had been moved. It stood at an angle to the window now instead
of square to it, which meant whoever worked at it had turned herself
away from the view. The three nine-inch stacks were gone. In their
place was a landscape: paper across the entire surface, in eleven or
twelve separate arrangements, weighted down with a teacup, a paperweight,
a sake cup used as a paperweight, an empty inkstone and — Sazare
identified it at four feet and had to stop looking at it — Kanzaki's
brass balance, borrowed, with the case open beside it.

The pen stand had two pens in it and neither of them was broken.

Behind the desk, with her sleeves shoved back to the elbow and her
forearms flat on the paper, was a woman in her fifties who did not look
it.

Sazare catalogued her the way he catalogued everything, before he had
decided to and without any capacity to stop.

The hair was pale gold and in two loose tails over the shoulders and
had been put up and taken down again at least once that morning — there
was a crease in it where a pin had been. The eyes were brown and moved
faster than the rest of her. There was a violet mark on the forehead
about the size of a thumbnail, and a line between the brows that had
been put there by reading rather than by temper, and the skin around
the eyes had the particular dryness of somebody who had not slept
properly for a long time and had stopped treating it as a problem to be
solved.

The haori was good and had been good for a long time. Grey-green silk,
lined, and the lining had been replaced — he could see it at the cuff,
a slightly different weight of cloth turned under and stitched with a
running hem by somebody with small neat competent hands who was not a
tailor. There was a scorch the size of a coin near the left hip,
brushed and left. The kimono under it was plain and had been washed
until the dye had gone flat in the folds.

The hands were the thing.

They were immaculate. Nails cut to the quick and filed square, no
rings, no varnish, the skin of the fingers dry and slightly rough from
being scrubbed several times a day for thirty years. There was a
callus on the outside edge of the right index finger, low down, in the
place a pen puts one — and a second on the inside of the same hand,
across the top of the palm, which no pen puts anywhere and which he
had seen exactly twice before in his life, both times on men who
worked with a hammer.

Then he saw the necklace and everything else stopped.

She did not look up.

"Sit down. Don't move anything on the desk. Shizune, the yellow ones."

Sazare sat. Shizune put a folder down at the woman's left elbow without
being told which yellow ones, and stepped back to the side table.

For eleven seconds nobody said anything at all.

Then Tsunade turned a sheet over, put one finger on a line halfway
down it, and spoke without raising her head.

"Attempt one. You keyed the trigger to contact alone and it fired in a
coat pocket crossing a yard."

"Yes."

"Whose idea was contact alone?"

"Mine."

"Why?"

"Because it worked on the bench at home and I didn't consider that a
bench is a place where nothing touches anything by accident."

Tsunade made a short sound that was not quite a laugh and wrote
something in the margin of his own log, in her own hand, which he
watched happen with a sensation he had no name for and did not have
time to examine.

"Good," she said. "Next."

---

He got four more questions in before she caught him looking.

It was not deliberate and he could not afterwards reconstruct how long
it had been going on, because the necklace had taken hold of the part
of him that had been trained before he could read and had never once
been successfully switched off.

*The stone is not cut.*

That was the first thing and it was, professionally speaking, absurd.
It was a pale green-blue crystal about the size of the end of his
thumb, and it had not been faceted, or cabbed, or even properly
polished — it had been taken more or less as it came out of the ground
and mounted. Nobody did that. A stone of that size and that water went
to a cutter, always, because cutting it was the difference between a
curiosity and money, and any workshop in four countries would have put
eleven facets on it inside a week and sold it for a house.

Which meant nobody had ever intended to sell it. Which meant it had
never been bought.

*The mount is wrong for the stone and right for the era.* Plain, heavy,
a simple closed bezel in a yellow gold too soft for daily wear, made by
somebody good, at least eighty years ago and probably more, in a style
Sazare had only seen in his mother's pattern books under a heading
about work that had stopped being fashionable before the villages were
founded.

*It has been repaired twice, by two different hands, decades apart.*
The first repair was excellent: a new bail, cleanly done, the join
polished out so well that he could only see it because the gold from
the two periods had aged to fractionally different colours. The second
was not excellent. It was a re-solder at the base of the bezel, done
hot, done fast, with a bead of excess left in the angle that nobody had
cleaned off — the work of somebody who needed it back on within the
hour and did not care what it looked like.

*The cord is not jewellery at all.* Ordinary braided cord, the sort a
person buys by the arm's length in any market for tying a parcel,
knotted at the back by hand rather than fitted with a clasp. It had
been replaced recently; the ends were still sharp where they had been
cut. A woman who could have had a chain made of anything she liked, in
a village with nine goldsmiths in it, was wearing four ryō of parcel
string she had tied herself.

And on the face of the stone, off-centre, on the lower left, there was
a patch about the width of a thumb where the natural surface had gone
smooth and slightly cloudy in a way no polisher had ever produced,
because it had been rubbed. Not once. Not by accident. By the same
thumb, in the same place, over and over, for a very long time, by
somebody who did it without knowing she was doing it.

"Are you going to keep doing that," Tsunade said, without looking up,
"or shall I turn round so you can get a proper look."

Sazare, who had been raised by a woman who had beaten professional
courtesy into him before he was eight, answered the question that had
been asked.

"The second repair was done in a hurry," he said. "There's solder left
in the angle at the base of the bezel. It'll be catching on your
clothes."

Tsunade's pen stopped.

She looked up, and for a moment her whole face did something
complicated and fast that Sazare could not read at all, because faces
were not a thing he had ever learned to read and he had spent the last
minute and a half proving it.

"That's not where people look," she said.

"I'm sorry. Should I not have—"

"No, you idiot, I mean that is *not where people look*." She sat back
an inch. "Thirty years. Kage, Council, foreign delegations, a
gambling hall in every town between here and the coast. Every single
one of them looks at one of two things and I could not tell you which
is more tedious, and *you* have spent ninety seconds staring at a
solder join."

"It's a bad join."

"It's a *terrible* join," Tsunade said. "A field medic did it in the
Second War with an instrument torch and about four minutes, because I
wanted it back on before we moved out and I wasn't prepared to discuss
it. Everyone since has offered to have it made good and I've said no
to every one of them."

"Because the mount would have to come off the stone."

She stopped again.

"Yes," she said, more slowly. "Because the mount would have to come off
the stone. That's exactly what the last man told me and it took him
about an hour to get there."

She picked the pen back up.

"Ruri."

"Yes?"

"Your file says jeweller's workshop. It says it in one line, under
*background*, between the date of your mother's death and the name of a
district that doesn't exist any more, and I read it four times this
morning and it did not once occur to me that it meant anything." She
found her place on the sheet again. "That's my error and I'd rather
have it out loud than carry it round for a month."

Sazare had no idea what to do with that, and so did the only thing he
had ever been able to do with anything, which was to answer the
technical part.

"It's a good stone," he said. "I've never seen one like it. I don't
know what it is."

"Neither does anybody else," said Tsunade. "Next."

---

She went through eleven weeks of work in under half an hour and she did
not go through it in order.

That was the first thing that undid him, because he had built the file
chronologically and had assumed, without ever noticing the assumption,
that chronological was the same as correct. She read the way a person
reads a room they have been in before: straight to the load-bearing
walls, ignoring the furniture entirely.

"You smooth," she said, at one point, apparently in the middle of a
different subject. "Yagi's notes. Four minutes on a clean two-inch
laceration, and she's written *control excellent, output wasteful*
without saying why. Do you know why?"

"Because I'm slow."

"You're not slow, you're thorough, and they aren't the same fault." She
found a second sheet without appearing to look for it. "You hold both
edges of a wound in apposition for the entire duration of the closure.
Correct?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because they move."

"They move for the first forty seconds. After that the fibrin's laid
down and they'd hold themselves if you took your hands off them, and
you would know that if anybody had ever taught you what fibrin does,
which nobody has, which is not your fault." She tapped the sheet twice.
"You are spending three of your four minutes doing a job the patient's
own blood finished while you were concentrating. That's not a small
inefficiency. On one wound it costs you nothing. On a bad night with
nine casualties it is the difference between reaching the ninth and
not."

Sazare said nothing, because there was nothing in it to argue with and
he had begun, dimly, to understand what Yagi had been warning him
about.

"Write it down," Tsunade said, not unkindly, without looking up.
"You're going to want to check it. Check it. I'll be right, but check
it anyway, because a man who takes my word for a mechanism is no
better off than a man who took his mother's."

He wrote it down.

---

"Anatomy," she said, ten minutes later. "Where does the blood come from
that fills the wound you closed on the man's thigh in the fourth
month?"

"The femoral—"

"No. That's the one you've read about because it's the one that kills
people. The one you actually cut across, on the outside of the thigh,
four inches long. Name it."

He could not.

"You don't know," Tsunade said. "You've been doing surface work for
seven months and you don't know the name of the vessel you've closed
forty times, because you learned this from the outside in — you learned
what a wound looks like and what stops it, and you never learned what
was underneath before somebody opened it." She sat back for the first
time since he had entered the room. "That's not a criticism of you.
It's a criticism of how you got here. Everybody else who does this
learned the inside first and met the outside afterwards. You have
built the entire thing backwards and it works, and it will keep working
right up until the first time it doesn't, and the first time it doesn't
will be a bleed you can't see and can't name and therefore cannot
find."

"How do I fix that?"

"You do four hundred hours of anatomy with somebody standing over you,
and you hate every one of them, and at the end of it you find out that
about a third of what you invented for yourself was correct and the
rest is why the third worked." She said it flatly, as a schedule
rather than a punishment. "Shizune, put that on the list."

"Already on it," said Shizune.

---

"The forearm," Tsunade said.

Sazare put his hand over the eleven faint lines without deciding to.

"Yagi's idea?"

"Mine. She supervised it."

"Of course she did, or I'd be having a different conversation with her
this afternoon." Tsunade held out a hand and he put the arm on the desk
because there was no version of refusing that ended anywhere he wanted
to be. Her fingers were warm and completely impersonal and went along
the line of scars once, from wrist to elbow. "Eleven cuts. All clean.
All the same depth, all in the same plane, all on a sixteen-year-old
with good circulation and no contamination and no shock and no fat to
speak of."

"Yes."

"So you have taught yourself to close the easiest wound that exists, on
the healthiest patient available, under laboratory conditions, eleven
times." She let go. "It gave you control. Genuinely — the control is
the best thing in this file and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. It
also taught you three things that are going to get somebody killed."

"Which three?"

"That wound edges are straight. That tissue is warm. And that the
patient holds still." She turned another page. "None of those are true
after about the second hour of any real night, and you have eleven
repetitions of muscle memory telling you they are. That's worse than
never having practised, because you'll be fast and confident and
wrong."

"I've closed on the ward. Those weren't clean."

"How many were ragged?"

He thought about it honestly, which took longer than he wanted it to.

"Four."

"Out of?"

"Sixty-one."

"Sixty-one," Tsunade repeated. "And the four that were ragged — how
long did they take you?"

"Longer."

"How much longer?"

"...I didn't record it."

"No," she said. "You didn't. And you record everything." She let that
sit exactly as long as it needed to. "That's the finding, not the cuts.
You have a nine-year habit of measuring the parts of your work that
behave. The parts that don't behave are the entire reason to keep
records, and they are the only category in this whole file that is
thin."

---

She got to the crystal last, which he understood afterwards to have
been deliberate, and she got to the knee before it.

"Thirty-eight degrees," she said. "In eleven weeks off a Fujita
reconstruction with pathway damage in both arms and a chest leak."

"Yes."

"Whose programme?"

"Yagi's."

"Yagi's programme says twenty-nine by now, and I know that because
Yagi's programme is on this desk." She looked at him properly for the
first time, and it was not a comfortable thing to be looked at by. "So
either she's been letting you run nine degrees ahead of her sheet, or
she doesn't know."

There was no answer to that which was both true and survivable, so he
gave the true one.

"She knows. She hasn't said."

"Because she's picking her fights, which is correct of her and I'd have
done the same." Tsunade tapped the sheet. "Here's what nobody has told
you, so listen to the mechanism and not the instruction. The joint is
fine. Fujita's work is good, and the tissue will take more than you're
giving it. Your *pathways* are not fine. They abrade under load, they're
healing on a curve about four times slower than tendon, and every
degree you win by grinding is paid for in chakra you push through arms
that are still repairing. That's why you're tired at the sixth hour and
you think it's the leg."

He opened his mouth and closed it, because she was correct, and he had
been tired at the sixth hour for nine days and had catalogued it under
the leg without checking.

"Technically correct programme," Tsunade said. "Too aggressive, for a
reason that has nothing to do with knees. Take it back to the sheet.
You'll get the same range by the same date and arrive with arms that
work."

"Yes."

"Say it back to me."

"The joint isn't the limit. The pathways are, and I've been paying for
range with pathway load without recording either."

Tsunade looked at him for a moment.

"Shizune," she said. "He repeats things back accurately."

"I noticed that too," said Shizune.

"It's rarer than it should be." She pulled the last arrangement of
paper toward her, the one with Kanzaki's balance sitting on the corner
of it, and for the first time since Sazare had entered the room, she
stopped moving quickly. "Right. The stone."
