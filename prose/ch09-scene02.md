# Chapter 9, Scene 2 — The Exchange

*Act 2, part A. Sazare Ruri, age thirteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,600 words.*

---

The town was called Shiranui and it was wrong in every direction he looked.

It was wrong because the harbour faced east and the light came into it in the
morning instead of leaving it. It was wrong because the houses went up the hill
in colours — ochre and rust-red and a blue-washed one, actual colours, on
outside walls, where the weather could get at them, as though weather here were
a friendly acquaintance rather than a thing you survived. It was wrong because
the street by the water was full of people who talked to each other across the
whole width of it, at volume, in an accent that swallowed half of every word,
and none of them adjusted their pace going past anybody, because there was
nobody to adjust for.

There were no shinobi on the bridges. There was one bridge, and it had an old
man on it, fishing.

And it smelled wrong. Under the fish and the tar, which were the same
everywhere, the whole town smelled faintly of eggs — sulphur, the boat-medic
said, from the springs — and the gutters along the upper streets ran warm, and
steamed gently in the early morning, so that the town looked from the harbour
as though it were simmering.

Hot Water. Mizuho had drawn the whole continent in eight strokes and put her
hand flat on five blobs, and this country had not been any of them. It had been
somewhere in the remaining scribble — one of the nine that didn't matter,
disposed of in half a sentence on a wet morning six years ago, and Sazare,
standing on a foreign quay at thirteen with everything he owned in a cloth roll,
found that half-sentence sitting in his mouth like a stone.

The town was full of people. Every single one of them appeared to matter to
somebody.

---

The exchange was a long low building at the west end of the harbour with a
counter across the front of it, open to the street, like a fish stall that sold
work.

That was, as far as Sazare could tell, exactly what it was. There was a board
along one side with chalk on it — *quarry, 6; nets, 2; timber up the valley, ask;
bathhouse, girls only, ask* — and a queue of men in front of the counter at first
light, and behind the counter a fat ledger and a thin man, and the thin man
matched the men to the chalk and wrote in the ledger and took a coin from each
of them out of their first week's wages, and none of it was hidden in any way at
all.

The fourteen off the boat went in through the side door, and that was not
hidden either. Nobody watched them go in. A town that lives off a harbour has
seen every kind of arrival there is, and has learned exactly how much interest
is safe to take, which is none.

Inside there was a room with benches, and a stove with tea on it, and a second
ledger, and the woman from the boat — the one with the bag, who had not slept,
who had asked him what they called him — sat down behind it and became, in the
space of sitting down, an official.

Her name was Anzu. He had had four days to assemble it and had got it on the
second.

"Right," she said, to the room. "You know most of this. In you come, one at a
time, family's one visit. It's a labour exchange. That's not a story we tell,
it's what it is — you'll work, the work's real, the pay's short a cut and the
cut goes home. Anyone who has a problem with the cut can tell me about the
problem, and then the cut goes home anyway."

Nobody had a problem with the cut.

Sazare sat on the bench and watched thirteen people be processed, which took
the whole morning, and it was the Academy intake and it was Schedule B and it
was the district office queue, and he watched it the way he watched everything
now, and it was none of those things, and he could not immediately find the
difference, and worked on it, because working on it was something to do.

The difference, he decided eventually, was where the pen stopped.

The couple with the baby: the man was a caulker, and the pen took his trade and
where he'd worked and what his hands could do, and then Anzu asked what the
baby was called, and the pen *did not move*. She just asked. She wrote down the
work and asked about the baby with her hands still, and the mother said the
name, and Anzu said it was a good name, her own aunt's name, and then the pen
went on with the caulking.

He had never once seen an official ask a question and not write down the
answer.

He put that away with the other things he was keeping, and did not know what
the collection was for, and kept it anyway.

---

His own turn came last, after the room had emptied, which was not an accident.

He sat down on the stool in front of the ledger. Anzu looked at him across it —
she had slept, finally, in the town, and it had taken years off her — and then
she wrote, in a quick small hand, upside down to him and perfectly legible,
because he read everything and always had:

*Ruri Sazare, 13.*

"We'll skip the part where you tell me you make earrings," she said, not
unkindly. "Everyone in this building knows whose brother you are. That's not a
thing you'll be able to spend, and it's not a thing you'll be able to put down
either, so best to know you're carrying it. Trade?"

He looked at the ledger.

"Crystal," he said. It came out in a voice he had not used for a while.
"Growing. Artisan-grade. I'm not — I wasn't finished being taught."

She wrote *crystal (artisan, part-trained)*, which was the most accurate thing
anybody had ever written about him in a ledger, and he watched her do it with a
sensation he could not name.

"Other skills?"

"I can read hands."

The pen paused, for the first time.

"Whose hands?"

"Anybody's. Inscribers'. I can't read the marks, I don't know what they say. I
can tell who wrote them. I can tell if two things were written by the same
person." He heard himself adding, absurdly, after four days of near-silence, the
way the whole of him had once told a clerk about a line where he stopped
feeding it: "There were twenty-one families in the offcuts and I found eleven."

"I have no idea what that means," said Anzu, "and I know exactly what it's
for." She did not write it down.

He watched her not write it down. She held his eye while she was not writing it
down, deliberately, until she was sure he had seen her not do it, and then she
moved her hand and wrote instead:

*Other: none. Not fit for work. Medical — refer T.*

"That's the ledger anyone might read," she said. "The true version lives in
heads. Yours is: nothing on paper, ever, that Kiri would pay for. They have a
file on you, and the file is good, and the one advantage you have for the next
five years is that the boy in that file is a growing-boy who makes hollow
earrings, and the day some piece of paper anywhere says otherwise, that
advantage is gone and it does not come back. Do you follow me?"

"Yes."

"Say it back."

He had not heard those two words in that order since a wet street and a package
for a man called Bekku, and they went through him like cold water.

"Nothing true about me goes on paper," said Sazare.

"Good lad," said Anzu, and shut the ledger.

---

The billet was a loft above a net store, shared with the caulker's family and
an old man who coughed, and he was in it for nine days while the arm was argued
about and the road was arranged.

On the third day he found the wall.

It was inside the exchange, at the back, past the benches — a whole wall of
planking painted black, and chalk on a string, and it was covered from
knee-height to as high as arms went in names.

They were written in every hand there was. Some were printed out carefully by
people who did not write much; some were fluent; some had been written by one
person for another, you could tell, the same hand doing three names in a row
with different surnames. Beside most of them was a place and a season — *Nagihama,
autumn* — and beside some there was a line through, and a second hand, and a word:
*found. found. Kirigakure, held. found.*

And beside some the chalk had simply faded, season by season, nobody renewing
it, until the name was a ghost of a name on the black board, and you could not
tell whether that meant found, or given up, or that the person who had been
looking had themselves stopped being anywhere.

People came in the evenings. That was what the wall was for. Men off the quarry
shift stood in front of it with their caps in their hands and read it like
scripture, top to bottom, every name, in case. A woman came in every single
evening of the nine days, checked one name, and left.

Sazare stood in front of it on the third evening with the chalk on its string
by his shoulder.

He knew eight names he could write. He stood there long enough for the lamp to
be lit behind him, and in the end he wrote none of them, because a name on that
wall was a question, and he was the only person alive who already had the
answer, and the wall was for people who were allowed to hope, and he was not
going to steal from them.

He put the chalk down. It swung on its string.

Behind him, quite close, somebody said: "Ruri."

It was Bandō.
