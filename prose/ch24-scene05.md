# Chapter 24, Scene 5 — Nineteen

*Act 4, part A. Konoha, the seventh month. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~2,700 words.*

---

The village filled up over eleven days and he did not sleep properly for
nine of them.

It started with the flags. He came down the block on a Monday morning at
the sixth hour and there were poles going up along the Nakadōri, and by
that afternoon there was bunting across the market row, and by the
Wednesday the inns on the west side had put boards out in three languages
and the price of a room had gone from nine hundred ryō to four thousand
and nobody was complaining, because Konoha had not had the finals in six
years and every trestle and every futon and every skewer of anything in
that village was about to be worth double.

By the second week the streets had a sound in them he had never heard
there before, which was the sound of people speaking Konoha's language
badly.

Sazare Ruri walked to work through it every morning with his collar up and
his hands loose at his sides, and read the ground the way he had read the
ground in four countries, and found that he could not stop.

Grass sandals with a Wind-country heel binding. A Kusa hitai-ate on a boy
of about fourteen buying a peach. Three Ame chūnin at a noodle stand with
their masks pushed up, drinking, at the ninth hour of the morning. Suna
everywhere — the largest delegation, in the tan and the wraps, with the
sand-grit in the folds of their kit that no amount of laundering ever
gets out, and he knew that grit, he had swept it off a bench in a rope-
walk in Tsuzura for a man who came through twice a year.

Every single one of them was a person who might have a book with a tab in
it.

He got to the eleventh day of it before he did the sensible thing, which
was to ask.

"Delegation list?" said Ashida Kōhei, with his mouth full, at the reading
room desk. "It's *pinned up*. It's been pinned up since the fourth. It's
by the door, you walk past it twice a day."

It was by the door. He had walked past it twice a day.

He stood in front of it at the tenth hour of the evening with the lamps
lit and read the whole thing four times, slowly, the way he read a
material list.

*Sunagakure. Amegakure. Kusagakure. Takigakure. Otogakure — provisional.*

And then it stopped, because that was the end of it, and there were four
inches of empty paper underneath and then a clerk's initials and a date.

No Kiri.

He read it twice more to be sure, and then went outside and stood in the
dark against the wall of the Academy building with the crickets going in
the grass and the whole village lit up below him and orange in the heat,
and put his head back against the brick.

Kirigakure had not been invited, or had not come, or had not been asked,
or had said no; and he never did find out which, and it did not matter in
the slightest, and the entire thing had been decided in some office by
somebody who had never heard of him, for reasons that had nothing whatever
to do with him, months before he had thought to be frightened of it.

He laughed once, quietly, against the brickwork.

*Eleven days,* he thought. *Eleven days of a knot in my chest over a
piece of paper by a door.*

---

He went to the library the next afternoon and gave the schoolbook back.

Ochiai was at the desk, as he was every afternoon, seventy-five years old
in a brown cardigan in July with the elbows gone, mending a spine with a
brush and a pot of paste and a strip of linen, working with the slow,
absolutely steady hands of a man who has done a thing thirty thousand
times and no longer needs to look at it.

"*Elementary Mineralogy for the Middle School*," he said, checking the
card. "Fourth time out this year. All four of them you." He put it in the
returns box. "You do know it's for fifteen-year-olds."

"It's the best book I've ever read."

Ochiai stopped brushing paste for a second.

"Well," he said. "I shall tell it."

Sazare took out three more. He was allowed three at a time and by the
seventh month he had had thirty-one of the forty out at least once, and
had copied about nine thousand words of them into a stack of hospital
requisition forms with the backs blank, and had found — this was the thing
he had not expected and never got over — that the further in he went, the
less the books agreed with each other.

The 1961 one said growth was a matter of the solution. The 1979 one said
it was a matter of the surface. There was a monograph from the eastern
provinces, translated badly, that spent forty pages arguing with somebody
called Warashina who was not in the library and never would be, about
whether a fault in a crystal is a defect or a *feature of how it grew*,
and which came down, eventually, at the end of a very long paragraph, on
the side of the feature.

Nine years he had thought the four seconds was a fact about him.

It was in a book, in a paragraph, in an argument, that people had been
having with each other since before his mother was born, in a building
four streets from his room, with a chair in it, that was open until the
tenth hour.

"Ochiai-san."

The old man looked up over his glasses.

"Do people ever write *back*? To these?" Sazare turned the monograph
round. "This man's arguing with somebody. Did the other one answer?"

Ochiai looked at the spine for a moment.

"Somewhere," he said. "Not here. We're a village library, son; we get what
we're sent." He went back to his linen strip. "You'd want the university
at Tanzaku for that, and they don't lend, and they don't let you in
without a paper." He smoothed the strip down with his thumbnail, once,
along its whole length. "Mind you. They don't check very hard *who* wrote
the paper."

Sazare Ruri stood at a desk in a civilian library with three books under
his arm and did not say anything at all for about four seconds.

"That's the second best thing anybody's said to me this year," he said.

"What was the first?"

"*Then bang all you like.*"

"I've no idea what that means," said Ochiai, stamping his card.

---

Kanzaki Uta wanted forty seeded blanks by the eighteenth and she wanted
them for the exams, and she told him so in the shed at the back of the
yard on a Thursday with the doors open and the heat coming off the yard
bricks in a wall.

"Perimeter seals. There'll be nine hundred of them up between now and the
finals and the ones on the gates get walked past by about eleven thousand
people, half of them foreign, and I am not having a blank crack in a
frame because somebody's elbow went into it."

"Forty's twenty-eight nights," said Sazare.

"I know what forty is."

"I've got nineteen days."

"I *know* what forty is," said Kanzaki Uta, and pushed her glasses up on
her forehead, and looked at him. "Do what you can. Twenty-five gets me the
gates. The rest can be plain and I'll put them somewhere with a wall
behind them."

She had not once, in eight months, told him what any of it was for beyond
the object in his hand. She did not tell him now either. It was the single
most restful professional relationship of his life.

He got her thirty-one, and it cost him most of the sleep he had.

---

And on the fourth night of that, at about the second hour of the morning,
with the lamp guttering and his hands gone and the persimmon black against
a sky that was already grey in the corner of the window, he did the thing
he had written down in the seventh month and had not had time for.

He put a seed on the tray.

And instead of putting his fingers on the granite beside it and starting a
bar off it, he put his fingers on the granite and started *another seed*
off it — a grain, off a grain, the new material arriving onto a template
that was already right, in a piece so small the whole of it was surface.

He had allowed ninety minutes and he had the timer glass turned.

It came up in fifty-one.

Sazare Ruri sat absolutely still on the floor of a rented room over a
persimmon tree with a grain of colourless stone the size of a rice-corn on
a tray in front of him and the sky going grey, and did not touch it, and
did not move, and did the arithmetic four separate times to be sure he had
not made it up.

*Ninety to fifty-one.*

*I did not get better at anything. My hands are worse tonight than they
have been all year. I did not concentrate harder and I did not find
anything out about myself.*

*I gave it a better place to land.*

*And the one it came off is not consumed, because I did not put it under
load — it is sitting on the tray, unchanged, and I can do it again
tomorrow, and the day after, and the one I made tonight will do it too.*

He put his hands over his face for a moment.

*Two of them make four. Four make eight.*

*It is not free. It is fifty-one minutes and a night and my hands, and it
will not go on halving, and somewhere down there is a floor and I will
find it in about a fortnight and it will probably be forty minutes and
that will be that.*

*But it moved. The seconds moved three weeks ago and the minutes moved
tonight, and I did not do either of them by getting stronger, which is
the only thing I have never been able to do.*

*I did both of them by finding out how it works.*

He wrote it up on a requisition form with the back blank, in the grey
light, with his hands shaking from four nights of no sleep: the times, the
weights, the method, the failure — because the third one he tried that
night came up faulted and he entered that too.

Then he wrote, at the bottom, underlined, and it was the nearest thing to
a creed he ever put on paper:

*I am not going to get stronger. Find out how it works.*

---

The roster for the finals went up on the second floor on the sixteenth and
he went and looked at it before his shift with the rest of them, in the
corridor, with the sun coming through the end window and the ward smelling
of the morning's carbolic and the trolley going somewhere down on the
first floor.

**Stadium aid post — 4 stations, 16 staff.** Two doctors, six nurses, four
orderlies, four runners. Kaburagi was on it. Nurse Yagi was on it. Ashida
Kōhei's wife was on it.

**Hospital, standing roster —** and there it was, six lines down, in the
clerk's hand:

*RURI, S. — 2nd flr, 06.00–16.00.*

He read it twice and then went and found Sister Kaburagi in the sluice.

"I'd like the stadium."

"No," said Kaburagi, without looking up from the bin she was hooking a
liner into.

"May I ask why?"

"You may." She straightened up. She was fifty-one and had a face like a
closed door with a window in it and forearms she had earned, and she gave
him the whole answer, standing there with a bin liner in one hand, because
she had never once in eight months made him ask twice for a reason. "One:
the stadium is outside the inner perimeter and you are a probationary
foreign national on a residence order that says you are not, and I don't
write that order and I can't bend it and neither can the Hokage on a
Tuesday. Two: there will be four hundred foreign shinobi in that bowl and
about nine of them will be from countries that would be very interested in
your hands, and putting you in a white apron in the front row of that is
the stupidest thing I could do with you this month."

"Right."

"Three," said Sister Kaburagi, "and this is the one that's actually true.
I'm putting sixteen of my best staff in a field station four miles from
the theatre for a day, for a *sports fixture*, and what I am left with in
this building is a skeleton and eleven people who can't walk." She hooked
the liner over the rim and snapped it down. "So no, you can't have the
stadium. I want you on the ward. I want somebody on that floor who knows
where everything is and can lift, and who is going to be *here*."

Sazare Ruri stood in a sluice room in a hospital in the Land of Fire and
was, for about a second and a half, so purely disappointed that it must
have been on his face.

"Yes, Sister."

"Don't sulk. It's a nice day out and you'll be indoors, which at your
complexion is a kindness." She picked up the bin. "And Ruri. Bring your
tools."

"Sister?"

"There's a window on the men's end that won't shut and I've been asking
maintenance since the fourth month," said Sister Kaburagi, "and everybody
in this village is going to be at that stadium, so it's the first quiet
day I've had since I was thirty."

---

He counted the tin on the morning of the finals because he counted the tin
every morning, the way other men check a pocket.

He did it at the low table with the shutter still closed and the room
already warm and the first of the light coming up the persimmon leaves
outside, and it took him about eleven seconds, and he did it with a
fingertip, moving them across the tin one at a time.

Nineteen.

Twelve made the hard way over eleven weeks at ninety minutes each, and
seven made in nine nights off the back of the twelve, and the last one
made at four in the morning on the Thursday in forty-four minutes, which
was the floor, and he had known it was the floor when it came up because
the third one that night had come up in forty-four as well.

Nineteen grains of colourless stone, four-tenths of a gram apiece, seven
and a half grams in total, all of it entered in a government ledger in
pencil in the sixth column.

Two seconds each. Nineteen times.

He put the lid on the tin and put the tin in the inside pocket of his coat
where it had lived since the fifth month, and rolled the mat, and washed
the cup and put it upside down on the shelf, and banked nothing because
there was nothing to bank, and picked up the tool roll.

Outside, the whole east side was going the other way — down the hill,
toward the south gate and the road out to the arena, in a river, in their
good clothes, with children on shoulders and paper flags: Ōtaki Sue in a
proper kimono with her hair done and a folding stool under her arm;
O-Shige with a basket; Kurihara Tetsu at a flat run; Nemoto on his stick,
last, sixty feet behind everybody, absolutely determined.

Sazare Ruri went up the hill to the hospital against the traffic, in the
sun, at the sixth hour, on the day of the third stage of the chūnin
selection examination, with nineteen grains in his coat and a screwdriver
in his hand for a window on the men's end.
