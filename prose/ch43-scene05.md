# Chapter 43, Scene 5 — The Trap

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the quartermaster's office. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,741 words.*

---

He designed it on the Saturday and it was the cleverest thing he had
ever built out of paper.

The problem was stated in four lines and he was rather proud of the
four lines. A materials-test schedule had left Konoha General and had
arrived, in some form, four hundred miles away, in the hands of a man
whose name nobody had. The hospital produced somewhere above nine
hundred sheets a week across eleven departments. Every one of those
sheets went through between four and nine pairs of hands and the
routing was recorded for none of them.

So: mark the paper.

Not with a seal, and not with anything a careful man would look for.
With an *error*.

He wrote it out for Kanzaki on the Monday with the whole scheme on one
sheet, because Kanzaki did not read documents longer than one sheet and
had told him so on the first day he had ever walked into that office.

Eleven versions of the same schedule. Identical in every respect except
one figure, and the figure chosen so that it changed nothing — a
tare weight in the third column, the sort of number that gets carried
forward and never used, differing by four grams across the eleven. Each
version goes to one department. Nobody is told. Nine months later,
whatever surfaces anywhere in the world, you read the tare weight and
you know which of eleven doors it went out of.

"It's four grams," he said. "It cannot affect a patient. It cannot
affect a dose. It cannot affect anything at all, because that column is
a provenance field and the only person in this building who reads a
provenance field is you and you'd be told in advance."

Kanzaki read the sheet.

She read it twice, which she did not do, and then she put it flat on
the desk and put both hands on either side of it, and Sazare had known
her for three years and had seen that exactly once before.

"No," she said.

---

"You haven't said why."

"I'm going to. Sit down, this is going to take longer than you want."
She did not sit. "How many people work in this hospital?"

"Four hundred and eleven, including the laundry."

"How many of them are stealing paper?"

"One. Possibly none — it may be a courier or a contractor or the
sealing office, which is why the eleven doors—"

"One," said Kanzaki. "At the outside, one. And your scheme tests four
hundred and eleven, without telling any of them, for nine months."

"It doesn't test them. It marks the paper."

"It marks the paper so that when the paper turns up, you can name the
door." She tapped the sheet. "There are between four and nine people
behind every door. When it turns up, you won't have a thief, you'll
have a door and a list of names, and then somebody in that office up
the hill will start on the list, and every single person on it will
find out that for nine months, the sheets they were handed had a
deliberate lie in them, put there by the second-floor fabricator, with
their department's number on it."

"The lie is four grams in a column nobody reads."

"The lie is that you knew and they didn't." She let that sit. "That's
the whole of it, and the four grams is a detail, and you're using the
detail because it's the part you can defend."

He did not say anything for a moment.

"Say the rest of it," he said. "You've got more."

"I have considerably more." Kanzaki pulled her stool round and sat on
it, which meant it was going to be bad. "Two years and one month ago,
you stood in a rear yard and let a woman put a stone in a man with a
bleeding graft, and the thing you got right — the thing that made
Yagi go to the wall for you and made Kaburagi stop calling you *the
fabricator* — was that the man knew everything. Everything, including
that it might do nothing, including that he could stop it at any
minute. You built a doctrine on that. It's got your name on it in
three files. It says: nobody in this building has a thing done to them
without knowing."

"That's patients."

"Is it?" she said. "Read it back to me and tell me where it says
patients."

He did not need to read it back. He knew where it said patients, which
was nowhere, because he had written it at eighteen in a rear yard about
a man with a graft and it had not occurred to him that the sentence
would ever be pointed anywhere else.

"You'd be running a nine-month covert trial," Kanzaki said, "on four
hundred and eleven people who did not consent, in a building where your
entire standing rests on a document that says you don't do that. And
you'd be doing it for an extremely good reason. They always are. I have
been a quartermaster for thirty-one years and I have never once been
handed a scheme like this by somebody with a bad reason."

---

He went at it for a while, because he could not do otherwise, and it
was not a good quarter of an hour for him.

He said it was proportionate and she agreed it was proportionate and
said proportionality was an argument about the size of a thing and not
about whether you were allowed to do it. He said the alternative was
nothing and she agreed the alternative was nothing and said that
sometimes the alternative was nothing. He said that the man four
hundred miles away had not asked anybody's consent and she said that
was correct and asked him whether he had noticed what he had just used
as a standard.

He stopped on that one.

"Ibiki will authorise it," he said, eventually.

"Ibiki will authorise it tomorrow morning and be pleased with you,"
said Kanzaki. "Ibiki's job is the four hundred miles. Mine is this
yard, and Kaburagi's is that ward, and there is no rule anywhere that
says the three of us have to reach the same answer, and the day you
start thinking that the man with the widest remit has the most correct
answer is the day you become useful to him and stop being useful to
anybody else."

"So it goes nowhere."

"I didn't say that." She stood up and pushed the stool back under the
bench. "I said no. I've got no authority to say anything else and you
know it — I can't stop you, I can decline to sign, which I do, and then
you take it upstairs, which you should." She squared the sheet on the
desk and pushed it back across to him. "Take it upstairs. Put my
objection in it. In full, in my words, not your summary of them,
because your summary will be fair and it will be four lines shorter
than it needs to be."

"You want me to argue against myself."

"I want you to hand her the whole of it. And then I want you to abide
by what she says, which is the part you'll find difficult, and which is
also the entire reason anybody in this village lets you build
anything."

---

He got to the door and stopped, because there was a thing he had been
carrying since the yard and it was going to get worse if he took it
home.

"You told her about the governor," he said.

"I told Hatake about the governor, yes."

"Why."

Kanzaki looked up.

"Because he asked me what you'd been building and I said a limit that
goes on before the thing it limits, and he went quiet for about four
seconds and then said *say that again*." She went back to the day book.
"I have watched you for three years, Sazare, and you have got extremely
good at one thing, which is being honest on paper after the fact. You
are not yet any good at all at the other one."

"Which is."

"Being honest with somebody who can still stop you." She did not look
up. "Paper can't stop you. That's why you like it."

---

He wrote it up on the walk home and finished it at the table.

*Objection sustained, entirely, on grounds I did not construct and
could not answer, from a woman with no authority over me who declined
to sign and told me to take it over her head.*

*The four grams was real and true and it was also the part of the
scheme I put in front of her, and I put it in front of her because it
was the defensible part, and I did that without deciding to do it,
which is the bit I want on the record.*

*I did not build a covert trial and then find a justification. I built
a covert trial and my hands assembled a presentation for it on the way
to her office, out of the true material, arranged so that the
uncomfortable half was in the third paragraph.*

*I have priced things for eleven years. I know exactly what that
arrangement is called when a man does it with a stone.*

He sat back and then wrote the honest half underneath, and it took him
three attempts.

*I do not think the scheme is wrong. I still don't. I have gone through
her objection nine times and I cannot break it and I also cannot make
myself agree with it, and those are two different states and I am going
to write both down rather than pick the one that resolves.*

*What I am certain of is the second thing, which is that I would have
run it, and I would have run it without telling four hundred and eleven
people, and the document with my name on it in three files would have
gone on saying what it says.*

*Take it upstairs. Her objection first, in her words, at the top, above
mine.*

*And then abide by it, which she has correctly identified as the part I
am going to find difficult, and which she said out loud to my face
while I could still do something about it, which is the thing I have
never once managed to do for anybody.*
