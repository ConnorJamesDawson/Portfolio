# Chapter 17, Scene 2 — The Grammar

*Act 2, part D. Kasado, on the strait. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

Kasado sat at the narrow end of a strait where two coasts came within
nine hundred yards of each other, and everything about the place was
about that nine hundred yards.

There was a north quay and a south quay and a chandlery and a
lamp-oil agency and four boatyards, and a great deal of official
signage about tolls that nobody read, and a slipway where the current
came round the point so hard that a badly loaded boat went sideways
before it went anywhere, and three inns, and — this was the thing you
noticed about Kasado if you had ever been anywhere — an enormous number
of people who were not from Kasado.

They were on the quay in the mornings. They were sitting on their bundles
outside the toll shed. They were in the yards of two of the three inns
with their children asleep against them, and they had the particular
posture of people waiting for a thing they have paid for and are no
longer certain of.

Sazare had seen it before. He had *been* it, at thirteen, on a jetty in
the fog, with everything he owned in one hand.

He came in on the fourth day of the fortnight with a sub-contract in his
pocket from a smith at Ihoya — forty tally-frames and a barrel of hinge
plates, a fortnight's overflow, entirely genuine, arranged three weeks
earlier for exactly this purpose — and took a bench in the back of the
second boatyard and was, within about six hours, a completely
uninteresting fact about the town.

He had four days of survey.

He used three of them.

---

The description he had been given was a woman of about fifty, heavy
build, grey, who kept the chandlery on the north quay and walked the
tideline at low water most mornings, and who was called nothing at all,
because he had told a fat man in a counting-room not to tell him.

He found her inside two hours and did not once look at her face for
longer than a stranger would.

The chandlery was the correct sort of place. It was a real business —
rope, tar, oil, salt beef, a wall of shackles, a counter worn hollow —
and it did a real trade, and there was a girl of about seventeen behind
the counter who was not her daughter, and a man in his sixties in the
back who mended nets and did not appear to be paid, and a great many
people came in and bought nothing.

That was the first thing. He noted it and did not lay it beside anything.

The second thing was the ledger. She kept two, in plain sight, on the
same shelf, and he watched her work them for twenty minutes from across
the quay through an open door: the fat one for the chandlery, and a thin
one that came out for about one customer in nine, and the pen stopped and
did not stop in a rhythm he knew before he knew that he knew it, the way
you recognise a footstep on a stair.

*Anzu,* said something in him. *That's Anzu's rhythm. That's the pen
going while she decides what to write down and what to have never been
told.*

He put that in the tray. Loose. Not beside anything.

The third thing was the walk. She went out at low water and along the
tideline eastward under the toll shed and round to the shingle at the
point, six mornings out of seven, alone, at first light, for forty
minutes, and came back with a basket that sometimes had weed in it and
sometimes did not.

A woman of fifty, alone, at first light, on a shingle spit with no
sightline from the town, six mornings out of seven.

He had it by the end of the first day. It was the easiest job he had ever
looked at.

---

On the second evening he walked the boatyards, because he walked
everything, and on the wall of the third boat-shed there was a rope-board.

It was an ordinary rope-board: a plank with pegs on it and rope-ends
hanging, the kind that has been on the outside of every boat-shed since
boats were invented, and it was weathered grey and nobody in the world
looked at it.

There was a chip of stone wedged flush in the joint at the second peg.

Cloudy. Worthless. About the size of a thumbnail. The sort of thing you
would take for a bit of grit that had got itself jammed, if you took it
for anything at all, which nobody ever had, in the entire history of
Kasado.

Sazare stood in front of a boat-shed wall in the last of the light with
his hands at his sides and his ears roaring.

He did not touch it. He did not need to touch it. He could read it from
four feet away: the wedge-set, flush, the long axis lying up-and-left,
one small scratch across the near face made with a nail.

*Ours. Safe. Knock.*

---

It was not Ryōsuke's hand. He established that in the first two seconds
and it did not help him at all, because that was not the point and had
never been the point.

The point was that there is only one way to do this.

You cannot invent a system for telling a frightened stranger that a door
is safe. You inherit it, from somebody who inherited it, and the marks
change and the hands change and the pegs change and the *grammar does
not*, because the grammar is four hundred years old and it works and
nobody who has ever needed it has had the leisure to improve on it. A
chip means a thing. A peg means a place. A scratch across the face means
*and there is somebody in there*. He had been taught it at eight years
old on a step in the dark by a girl of fifteen who had made it a game,
and had spent two hours a week for four years getting quick at it, and it
had gone into him the way a first language goes into anybody, which is to
say permanently and underneath.

He had spent nine days at Kanezaki successfully not-assembling a man's
entire life.

He was given four seconds here, and he did not get to use one of them,
because it was not assembly. There was nothing to assemble. It was
*reading*, and reading is not a thing a literate person can decline to
do.

---

He went and stood on the north quay in the dark for a long time.

Then he did the thing he had been very carefully not doing for three
days, and he did it deliberately, with his eyes open, knowing exactly
what it was: he went back through the survey and laid every single part
of it out in a row.

*A chandlery that does a real trade and a great many people come in and
buy nothing.*

*A thin ledger for one customer in nine, and a pen that stops while a
woman decides what to have never been told.*

*A girl of seventeen behind the counter who is not her daughter.*

*A man of sixty in the back mending nets who is not paid.*

*Two inns full of people sitting on bundles who have paid somebody for a
crossing and are not sure of it, and one inn that has none.*

*A strait nine hundred yards wide with a current at the point, and a
ferry that Chōsei — comfortable, cheerful, in a warm room, with his
sons in the trade — had called a swindle to my face and laughed about.*

*Forty ryō a head.* He did not know that number. He knew that number the
way you know the price of bread in a town you have never been to: it was
the number, it had been the number on every coast in the four years since
the fog came down, and he had heard it said aloud in a boat once by a man
who was not going to be able to pay it.

*And a woman who has a mark on a peg and a second ledger and no boat of
her own.*

Sazare stood on a quay in the border country and finished it, and it took
him less than the four seconds, and it came out clean and complete and
without any possibility of error whatsoever:

She moves them for nothing.

That is the whole of it. That is the entire commercial dispute. There are
men on this strait who charge forty ryō a head to put frightened people
in a boat and some of those people are landed and some of them are not,
and there is a fat woman on the north quay with a chip on a peg and a
line of skiffs she does not own, and she takes them across for whatever
they have, and sometimes for nothing, and she has been doing it for long
enough that two inns are full and the third one is empty.

And she is bad for business.

And a man in a counting-room with a finger like a sausage has offered
four hundred ryō for her, in a fortnight, *made to look like anything
else at all*, because the buyers have to go on standing on that quay
afterwards and looking those people in the face.

---

He sat down on a bollard, which he had no memory of deciding to do, and
was still sitting on it a considerable time later when the tide turned.

The thing he could not get round — and he tried, he went at it from four
directions, because going at things from four directions was all he had
ever been — was that he already knew everything about her.

Not her name. He had been very careful about her name and the care had
been worth precisely nothing.

He knew what her life was. He knew she did not sleep much. He knew that
the second ledger had names in it that were not true and that she had
written every one of them herself and would burn the book before she let
it be read, and that the burning was planned, and that she had thought
about where. He knew that she had a list in her head of who owed what and
that she would never call any of it in. He knew that when a boat did not
come back she went and stood somewhere on her own for a while and then
went back to the counter, because there was a queue, because there was
always a queue. He knew she had been asked, more than once, by somebody
who loved her, to stop.

He knew all of it because he had lived in a house with it for four years.

His sister had come in at the third hour with her hands cold and had
eaten standing up and had said *no* and *no* and *yeah* to a boy on a
step, and had lied to her own mother's face at a kitchen table for
thirteen months, and had built a thing out of chips and pegs and other
people's back doors that had moved — he had never known the number and
never would — some quantity of frightened strangers out of a district
that no longer existed.

And she had been bad for business too. That was the thing. That was
exactly the thing. His whole family had gone into a square in front of
four hundred people because a resistance is *inconvenient* to somebody,
and the somebody had not even been ideological about it, and here he sat
on a bollard at sixteen with two hundred ryō in his coat, hired.

---

He walked back to the boatyard at about the second hour and lay on the
bench in the back with his coat over him.

He did not decide anything. That is the honest account of the night and
he never told it any other way, even to himself, because he had made a
rule about preferring true things and the true thing was this: he did not
lie there wrestling with it. He did not weigh it. He did not compose
arguments for either side and set them against each other the way he had
set arguments against each other his whole life, about lattices and notes
and roads and men.

He lay on a bench in a boatyard at Kasado and looked at the roof and
thought, over and over, with a kind of flat administrative persistence,
about the money.

Four hundred was two years.

Two years was the man who could tell him in one sentence.

The sentence was the four seconds, and the four seconds was the reason
there was a piece of him in the ground on a hill above Kanmuri Street,
and the reason a bar in a culvert had lasted no time at all, and the
reason that when the white faces came back — and they would come back,
prices like that always find feet, and feet come back — he would have
nothing whatever to meet them with except a stick of oak he no longer
owned and a habit of putting rungs in walls that a woman with a bad
temper had told him to stop.

Two hundred in his coat. Two hundred on completion.

*You'll want it later. They all want it later. At three in the morning,
about the sixth week.*

It was the third hour, and it was the eighth day, and he had wanted it
early, and he had not been told, and he had gone and found it out for
himself off a chip of grit on a peg because he was literate in the one
language his family had ever managed to leave him.

The tide came in under the boatyard and moved the skiffs on their
painters, and somewhere on the north quay a woman of about fifty who had
no name as far as he was concerned was probably awake, because they
never sleep much.

At about the fourth hour Sazare turned over and faced the wall and
worked out how he was going to do it.
