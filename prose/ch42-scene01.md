# Chapter 42, Scene 1 — Nine Seconds

*Act 4, part D. Konoha General, the fourth-floor landing. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,609 words.*

---

The reason was in the notebook and he had written it eleven days
earlier and had been unable to get out from under it since.

*Two. There are two people alive who can hold both halves at once.*

*I would like there to be more than two.*

He had audited it the way he audited everything, and the audit had
produced exactly one candidate, and the candidate was fourteen years
old and worked on the third floor.

Haruno Sakura had been in that building as long as he had. She was
Tsunade's, formally, in the way he was not; she had the medical half
coming at her at a rate he could not have survived and she had been
holding a scalpel of chakra for eleven weeks with a control that made
Yagi go quiet; and she was the only other person in Konoha who had been
picked out of a crowd by the same woman for the same reason and had to
work out what to do about it afterwards.

He had shared corridors with her for two years.

He had had, by his own count, forty-one conversations with her, of
which thirty-eight were handovers, two were the stairs in the second
month, and one was an argument about a dressing that he had won and
been wrong about.

*Chapter two,* he thought, going up the stairs on the fourth of the
fourth month with a bed state under his arm he did not need to deliver
in person. *Of Speech without Object.*

*State: cannot do at all.*

---

The fourth-floor landing had a window that faced east and got the
morning before any other part of the building, and at half past seven
in the fourth month it came through in a long warm bar that lit the
whole stairwell and made the dust visible and made the paint — a green
put on in a hurry the summer after the invasion, over a green that had
been there thirty years — show every place where the new coat had gone
thin over the old. Somewhere below, a trolley went over the join in the
corridor tiles with the same double knock it had made every morning for
two years. The building smelled of hot water and the particular clean
scorch of the sterilising room at the start of a day.

Sakura came up the stairs at a pace that had nothing left over.

She had the satchel on, the strap knotted twice near the buckle to take
up nine inches it had been built with for somebody taller, and the
knots were old and had been retied enough times that the webbing had
gone furred where they sat. Ink down the outside of her right middle
finger. Her nails were cut to nothing, and the pad of her thumb had
begun — he saw it and could not unsee it — to go flat and shining in
exactly the place Tsunade's had, which was eleven years of a technique
arriving early in somebody who had been doing it for eleven weeks.

Her forehead was bare and she carried it the way a person carries a
thing they have decided in public not to mind.

"Ruri-san."

"Good morning," said Sazare.

And then he did not say anything else.

He had planned that far and no further, because the book said —
explicitly, on page nineteen, in a paragraph he had read enough times
to recite — that the whole of chapter two consisted in *going in
without a cargo*, and that the commonest error of the studious was to
prepare a subject and call it conversation.

So he had prepared nothing.

The bar of light lay across the landing. The trolley knocked twice
below. Sakura stopped on the third stair from the top with one hand on
the rail and waited, in a perfectly ordinary way, for the rest of it.

There was no rest of it.

He was aware of the silence arriving, and then of it going on, and then
of it becoming a thing with a shape, and somewhere in the fourth second
his chest did something he had last felt in a corridor with twenty-two
people in it, and by the seventh second the only fact available to him
in the entire world was that he was standing on a landing producing
nothing.

At nine seconds he broke.

"The humidity's up," he said. "Four days running. The plaster store
wants turning before the end of the week or the bottom of the third
pallet will set in the sacks."

Sakura looked at him.

"...Right," she said. "I'll tell Emiko-san."

"It isn't urgent."

"No, I—" She came up the last two stairs. "I'll tell her anyway."

---

She got about four paces along the corridor.

Then she stopped, and stood for a moment with her back to him, and he
watched her make a decision in the set of her shoulders — he could not
read what it was, only that it was one — and turn round.

"Can I ask you something," she said, "and you're allowed to say no."

"Yes."

"Was that the book?"

Sazare did not answer.

"Because you came up four flights with a bed state that goes in the box
at the bottom," said Sakura, "and you said good morning, and then you
said nothing for about ten seconds, which you have never done in two
years, and then you told me about *plaster*." She had gone a bit pink
across the bridge of the nose and was pressing on regardless, which he
had noticed her do before and had catalogued incorrectly at the time as
nerves. "And in the second month you handed me a book and asked me four
questions on a staircase. So."

"Nine," said Sazare.

"What?"

"Nine seconds. Not ten. I counted."

"You *counted*—" Sakura put a hand over her face. "Right. Yes. That was
the book."

"It was the other book."

She took the hand down.

"The other book."

"There are two." He said it flatly, because there was no version of it
that was not flat, and because the alternative was to arrange it, and
arranging it was how a true thing became a manoeuvre. "The one I handed
you in the second month is about courtship and it is in a drawer and I
have not opened it in nine weeks. The one I was doing just then is
older and it is about friendship, and it has six chapters, and I have
had it since the ninth month of last year, and chapter two is called
*Of Speech without Object*, and I have not been able to do it once."

Sakura was quiet for a second.

"That's the friendship one," she said. "Not the other one."

"Yes."

"Okay." She adjusted the strap. "I just wanted it said out loud, so
that neither of us has to spend the next three weeks working out which
one it is."

"That's the correct instinct and I hadn't got to it."

"No, you hadn't." She said it without any heat at all. "You were going
to do the whole thing perfectly politely and never mention it, and I'd
have spent a fortnight deciding whether I was imagining it, and I'd
have got it wrong in one direction or the other, and then it'd have
been strange for a year. That's how it goes. I've watched it happen to
about four people."

"You're fourteen."

"I've watched it happen to about four people," said Sakura, "*because*
I'm fourteen and I work with adults and nobody shuts up in front of me
because nobody thinks I'm in the room."

Sazare wrote that down.

He got the notebook out and wrote it down standing on the landing in
the bar of light, and she let him, and when he looked up she had the
expression of somebody deciding whether to be insulted and coming down,
narrowly, on the other side.

"Why me," she said.

"Because you're the only person in this building I've had forty-one
conversations with and I can account for thirty-eight of them as
handovers."

"That's not an answer, that's a total."

He put the notebook away.

"Because there are two people alive who can hold the whole of what I
do," said Sazare, "and one of them is the Hokage and the other one is a
stranger, and I would like there to be three, and I looked at everybody
in this village and it's you, and I have no idea how to get from a
handover to that, and the only instruction manual I own says the way is
to be able to stand on a landing and say nothing about plaster."

Sakura looked at him for a while.

"Right," she said. "Well. You're terrible at it."

"I know."

"No — I mean you are *genuinely* terrible at it, it's not modesty, I
want to be clear about the scale." She hitched the satchel. "But you'd
rather be practised on than not, wouldn't you. That's what you're
asking."

"I don't know what I'm asking."

"Then I'll say it, because I do." She said it fast, and she did not
look at him while she said it, and he understood afterwards that this
was the part that had cost her something. "I'd rather be the person
somebody practises on than the person nobody practises on. Everyone in
this hospital is extremely nice to me and it is all handovers. All of
it. Two years."

Then she looked up.

"Half seven, this landing," said Sakura. "It's the only ten minutes
either of us has. Bring the book."
