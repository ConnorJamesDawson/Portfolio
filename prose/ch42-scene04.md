# Chapter 42, Scene 4 — Falling

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, training ground eleven. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,610 words.*

---

Kakashi came for him on the seventeenth, at the hospital gate, at forty
minutes past the hour he had named.

"Your knee healed in the eleventh month of the year before last," he
said, by way of the whole of a greeting. "That was fifteen months ago.
I've let it run because you had four things happening at once and
because a man who is being useful is very hard to argue with, and I've
decided this week that I've been wrong about that since roughly the
spring."

"I've been busy."

"I know exactly how busy you've been. I've read the ledger you file
with Kaburagi and I've read the one you file with Kanzaki, and between
them I can account for about ninety hours a week." Kakashi was already
walking. "Come on."

---

Training ground eleven was the small one, the one behind the
memorial-stone track, hemmed on three sides by the second growth that
had come up hard and fast in the two years since the invasion took the
old trees off that slope. It was late morning and the fourth month and
the ground had dried out on top and was still wet nine inches down, so
that it took a print and then held it, and the whole of the clearing
smelled of that — turned wet earth, and the sap of young growth, and
underneath it the cold mineral smell of the stream on the far side of
the trees.

The sky had gone that flat high white that is not cloud and is not
clear.

Kakashi walked him to the near post, stood him on a line scuffed into
the dirt, pointed at the far post, and said: "Go."

Sazare went.

He came back at the far side of forty seconds and his lungs were doing
something he had not felt since the invasion and he put a hand on the
post.

"Fifty-one," said Kakashi.

"...Seconds."

"Seconds. Post to post, that circuit, four laps." He put the timer
away. "A genin scores about thirty-four. A tired genin scores thirty-
eight. Sakura scored thirty-one at twelve years old, which is a low
number for a girl who was not, at twelve, especially fast."

Sazare waited.

Kakashi did not say anything else.

He got his breath back over the course of a minute and a half and
worked out, somewhere in the middle of it, that the silence was not a
pause and was not a kindness withheld — that Kakashi had given him the
number, and given him a scale for the number, and had then declined to
tell him what to feel about it, and that this was precisely and exactly
what he himself did to every single person he handed a fault report to,
and that he had never once considered how it landed on the other side
of the bench.

"Is that bad," he said.

"It's a number," said Kakashi.

"That's what I say."

"I know," said Kakashi. "That's why I said it."

---

They did falling for three hours.

Not throws. Not forms. Falling: from standing, from a crouch, from a
walk, backwards off a low bank, sideways onto the wet ground with the
arm out and then with the arm in and then with the arm out again
because he had got it wrong in a new way.

He was bad at it in a manner that surprised him, and the surprise was
itself the most useful thing that happened all morning, because he had
assumed — without ever writing the assumption down, which was how the
bad ones survived — that being good with his hands would generalise.

It did not generalise at all.

"You're catching yourself," said Kakashi, at about the fortieth
attempt. "Every time. You get four-fifths of the way into the fall and
then some part of you says no and you put a limb out and you arrive on
the ground in three separate pieces at three separate times, which is
how people break wrists."

"I know I'm doing it. I can't stop doing it."

"No. You can't." Kakashi hadn't taken the book out once, which Sazare
had noticed at the second hour and had not known what to do with. "Do
you want to know why, or would you rather find out in nine months?"

"Now."

"You have never once in your life let go of a position on purpose."

Sazare stood in the wet grass with his sleeve black to the elbow.

"Everything you're good at is holding," Kakashi said. "You hold a line
in a ledger. You hold a course in a stone. You held a hospital corridor
for eleven minutes with twenty-two people coming through it and you
would not give up a single foot of it, and that is not a criticism,
that is the reason you're alive and so are about ninety other people.
But every single technique in this profession above the level of a
thrown knife is a *controlled loss of position*. That's all a
substitution is. That's all a feint is. That's all falling is. You give
up where you are, on purpose, at a moment you chose, in order to arrive
somewhere better than where you were."

"And I can't."

"You can't yet. You've had eleven years of a life where every foot of
ground you gave up was taken off you." Kakashi said it without any
weight at all, in the register of a man discussing a camber. "And I'd
point out — since you like the ones that connect — that this is the
same fault as the field you couldn't turn off. Stopping is a loss of
position. You've been calling it a design problem for two years because
that's the half you could work on."

---

He made a table at the second hour.

He did it in a gap while Kakashi was doing something to a target at the
far end of the clearing, and he did it fast and neatly and with a
column for the four failure modes and a column for the count of each,
and he was eleven entries in when a gloved hand came over his shoulder
and took the notebook out of his hands.

"No," said Kakashi.

"It's a—"

"I know what it is. It's the best notebook I have ever seen and I have
read three of your reports for pleasure, which I would prefer you
didn't repeat." He put it in his own back pouch. "You'll have it at
the end. Not because writing is bad. Because you are using it to be
good at something while you are still bad at it, and the only way
anybody has ever learned to fall is to be bad at falling in front of
another person for about nine hours."

"That's the same problem."

"What is?"

"Being bad at it in front of somebody." Sazare heard it come out and
did not stop it, which he noted at the time as either progress or
fatigue. "I've been doing an experiment for a fortnight where I stand
on a landing at half seven in the morning and fail at a conversation in
front of a fourteen-year-old, and she scored me a two on the fourth
day, and I've thought about the two more than I've thought about
anything else this month, and I can't work out why, because it's the
smallest number anybody's ever given me and nobody died."

Kakashi was quiet for a second.

"Because nobody's ever seen you be bad at anything," he said. "You
arrived here at sixteen with eleven years of being the most competent
person in every room you'd ever stood in, and every one of those rooms
was one you'd walked into after you were already good. This is the
first time in your adult life you have been the worst person present
and had to come back the next day."

"That's accurate."

"It usually is, that one. It's the commonest failure in prodigies and
it kills more careers than injury." He nodded at the wet ground. "Get
up. Backwards, off the bank, arm in."

---

He went home at the fourth hour with his coat unwearable and both hips
already going the colour they were going to go, and Kakashi handed the
notebook back at the gate without comment, and Sazare got four paces
before he turned round.

"Fifty-one seconds," he said. "What's the number in nine months, if I
come every week."

"Forty-one."

"That's not thirty-four."

"No," said Kakashi. "You're eighteen and you started at eleven years
behind, and you will very likely never see thirty-four, and I could
have not told you that." He had the book out at last, and did not open
it. "I've decided you're one of the ones you tell. Same week, same
time. Bring a coat you don't like."

He was gone up the road before Sazare had worked out what to say, which
was becoming a fixed feature of their acquaintance and had stopped
being annoying somewhere around the second year.

*Fifty-one,* he wrote that night, standing up, because sitting down had
become a project.

*Genin: thirty-four. Ceiling, honest estimate, offered without being
asked for: forty-one.*

*I have written the number down and I am now going to write the other
thing down, because the ledger gets both halves.*

*The number is not the finding. The finding is that a man watched me be
worse at something than anybody he has ever trained, and told me the
true ceiling to my face, and then said "same week, same time."*

*Nobody has ever done both of those in the same conversation.*
