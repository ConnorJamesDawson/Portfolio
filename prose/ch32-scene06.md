# Chapter 32, Scene 6 — A Column With No Units

*Act 4, part C. Konoha, the room over the persimmon tree. Sazare Ruri,
age seventeen. POV: Sazare. ~1,320 words.*

---

He started the new page on the Wednesday night, because there was
nowhere else to put any of it and because a thing that is not written
down is, in his experience, a thing that gets remembered wrong within a
year.

The notebook had eleven columns' worth of habits in it by then and
every single one of them had a unit. Grams. Seconds. Degrees. Pounds
of load at failure. Minutes per grain, ryō per month, millilitres in
the tube and two left behind in it. He had never in his life kept a
record of anything he could not put a number beside, and had never
noticed that this was a choice rather than a property of the world.

He ruled the page the way he ruled every page.

Then he sat and looked at it for eleven minutes, because he did not
know what the second column was for.

In the end he wrote:

*ATTEMPTS*

and underneath, in the six lines it took:

*1. Tomoe. Described a bandage. She thought she was being told off.*
*2. O-Shige. Described a board. Her husband cut it. Accidental.*
*3. Mitarashi. Described a coat. Her mother's. Accidental.*
*4. Mori. Described a pouch strap. She has gone home and thought
about her pouch.*
*5. Ōtaki Sue. Her handwriting. Written, not said. First one that
worked and it worked because I could not speak it.*
*6. Handa. Said out loud. Took eleven days, four assemblies and being
refused a bench hook to get to it.*

He looked at the six of them.

Then he wrote the finding underneath, in the way he wrote every
finding, without softening either half:

*Six attempts. Two successes. Both of the successes were about a
person and not an object, and both of them cost me something, and in
both cases the thing they cost me was the same thing: the other person
now knows what I want.*

*Ōtaki Sue knows I have thought something admiring about her twice a
week for twelve months.*

*Handa knows he was one of four.*

*Neither of them can give that back. There is no version of this where
I get it back.*

*That is not a fault in the method. That appears to be the method.*

---

He could not put a unit on it.

He tried, because he could not stop himself trying, and got as far as
counting attempts and successes before he understood that the count was
not measuring the thing — six and two were facts about his mouth, not
about what had happened in a room with a slate in it.

He sat with the pencil for a while.

Then he wrote, in the second column, at the head, where the unit went:

*—*

and left it.

It was the first column he had ever ruled that had nothing to
calibrate against, and he found that he minded less than he had
expected to, and that what he actually minded was something else
entirely, which took him another twenty minutes to locate.

*I have been in this village for twelve months.*

*I have made, by my own count: one friend I chose (Handa, and he
chose me first and had to force the last four feet of it), one I
acquired by accruing him without noticing (Ashida, who has filed my
movements for a year and a half and asked me about my knee every
single Tuesday), one who intercepts me at a wall on a fortnightly
schedule she pretends is coincidence, one who taught me to look at
people and then left through a gate, one old woman two floors down, one
market trader, one soldier on a stick who bolts the door for the whole
street, and a boy who carries a tool roll like a log.*

*That is eight people.*

*In Kiri I had four in eleven years and two of them were related to me.*

*I did not do any of this on purpose. Not one of those eight is the
result of a decision I made. Every single one of them happened to me
while I was doing something else.*

He looked at that.

*Which means either I am extremely lucky, or the thing I have been
calling being careful is a thing that stops working the moment you
stay anywhere longer than a season.*

---

There was a fourth line he did not write for a long time.

He got up twice. He put the kettle on and did not make anything with
it. He went and looked at the shelf where the empty picture frame stood
beside the forearm plate, wrapped in its untreated cotton, which he
had never once unwrapped since it came back from evidence.

Then he sat down and wrote it, because leaving it out of the ledger
would have been the first dishonest entry in four years.

*The other thing I have not written down.*

*She had the join cleaned.*

*I said one sentence about a piece of jewellery in the autumn — as a
fault, which is the only way I have ever known how to say anything — and
she had four hours of work done on an eighty-year-old mount by
somebody she argued with for twenty minutes, and she did not tell me,
and she has not mentioned it, and she is not going to.*

*I do not know what to do with that.*

*I have no category for it. It is not gratitude, because I did not do
her a service. It is not politeness. It is not the thing Handa wanted
from me, because she has not said anything at all.*

*She simply took the one true thing I was capable of giving her, and
acted on it, and let me find out on my own.*

*Which is, as far as I can tell, the only way anybody has ever
successfully given me anything.*

He stopped there.

He did not write anything further about it, then or for a considerable
time, and he closed the notebook and put the pencil in the fold and sat
in the dark with the persimmon black against the window.

---

There was one more thing and it happened four days later and he did not
write it down at all.

He was in the administration building on an errand about a supply
requisition — Kanzaki's requisition, which she had sent him to chase
personally, on the theory that a clerk will do for a technician in
person what he will not do for a quartermaster on paper — and he was
standing at a counter waiting for a man to come back from a filing
room.

There was a tray on the desk beside him with the morning's summaries
in it, face up, the way trays are.

The third one down had a tab on it that said *WATER — DIGEST*, and
under the tab, in the corner, a date from that week.

Sazare read the tab.

He did not read anything else, because it was not his and because
reading it would have been a thing he could be asked about later, and
he had been very careful for twelve months about the difference
between what was in front of him and what was his.

The clerk came back with the requisition.

"Anything else?"

There was, in fact, something else. It had been there for nineteen
months. It had been sitting in trays two floors below his own desk the
entire time, and every single person in that building had assumed —
reasonably, from every piece of evidence he had ever given them, from a
headband tied to a medical bag and a ward roster and a man who never
once mentioned the place he came from — that he did not want it.

"No," said Sazare Ruri. "Thank you."

He took the requisition, and signed for it, and went back to the
hospital.
