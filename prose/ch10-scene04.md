# Chapter 10, Scene 4 — Not Yet

*Act 2, part B. Sazare Ruri, age fourteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,100 words.*

---

In the spring Tōan declared the arm finished, in the manner of a tradesman
signing off a job: he ran the whole battery on it in one morning — the stones,
the knots, the spring-band, grip against the scale hooks, and last a raw egg
to be lifted, carried the length of the porch in the bad hand's fingertips,
and set down unbroken — and then he wrote the final row of numbers in the
book, and ruled a line under them, and shut it.

"Strength's back to what your age owes you. The last two fingers will tire
first in cold weather for the rest of your life — that's the tendon's seam,
it's sound, it's just honest. Feeling's whole." He put the book on the shelf
with the patient books, where Sazare had never once seen him file anything
that had a name on it. "The scar you keep. I could fade it over another
year. I'm not going to."

"Why not?"

"Three reasons. It costs sessions we can spend better. Faded scars read as
vanity or as hiding, and both get remembered, and the whole of you is built
to be forgotten." He took the kettle off. "And a man should be able to read
his own ledger. That one's yours. It says paid."

Sazare looked at his forearm — at the long pale seam and the two round
stations of it, the writing his own blood had done on him in a fifth of a
second, fifteen months of other people's care wrapped around it since — and
found that he did not want it faded, and that until that moment he had not
known he did not want it.

"There's a last exercise," said Tōan. "It's not in the book."

He went out to the porch, and came back with a wooden tray, and set it on the
bench in the good light.

Grey sand, levelled, damp. He had got the grade right, or nearly — river
sand, washed; a little coarse, his mother would have sieved it twice — and
the sight of it standing there on a treatment bench in a foreign country
emptied every thought out of Sazare's head through the floor.

"Your trade's your own business," said Tōan, in exactly the voice he used
for dosages. "And I told you a year ago: nothing mends on an empty stomach,
and nothing mends unused either. That's true of tendon. It's true all the
way in. There's no patient in this house until the fourth hour."

And he took the kettle and went out to the herb beds, and was audibly,
tactfully, entirely gone.

---

The house made its noises. The stove ticked. Up the valley the sawmill sent
down its long two-note conversation with itself, and the river ran warm
under all of it, the way it ran under everything in that country.

Sazare sat down on the bench in front of the tray.

He looked at the sand for a long time. It was just past the fourth hour of
the morning; the light was the good working light; there was warmth coming
up through the boards from the channel the house was built over. Every
condition was kind. He noted that, and noted the kindness of it, and noted
that Tōan had chosen the day the way he chose everything, and none of the
noting moved his hands at all.

He got them up in the end. Both of them, out and open, heels turned slightly
in — the start position, the one his body had kept all this time underneath
everything, the one it made in his sleep — and held them over the tray, a
hand's width off the sand.

Found where it was moving. That was there too, instantly, the way the sea is
there when you walk back to it: the room's tremor, the sand's damp settle,
his own pulse ticking in the new-mended wrist. All of it laid itself out for
him the way it had since he was seven, asking, the way it had always asked:
*and now nothing. Now hold. Now not yet.*

His right arm, from the elbow down, went to ice.

Not pain. Refusal. Somewhere below decision, below even fear as he
understood fear, the whole limb declared — in the fixed way the body
declares, like a law posted nowhere and enforced absolutely — that the last
time chakra had gone down this road it had come out through three doors of
its own making with the family's whole art behind it and a man had stopped
being alive, and that this road was therefore shut, and would stay shut, and
that no argument now reaching it from the upper country of his head had
standing here.

Four seconds. Perhaps five.

He took his hands back and sat on them, on the bench, in the good light,
which is what he had done in a salt shed at twelve, and he was still sitting
like that — not crying; breathing carefully around a shut place — when Tōan
came back at the fourth hour and looked at the untouched sand and at the
boy sitting on his own hands, and nodded once, with no disappointment
anywhere in him, as at a number that had stalled and would be approached
lower down.

"Right," he said. "Not yet." He picked up the tray. "There's wood needs
splitting. Two-handed, now, so mind your feet."

And that was all. The tray went back to the porch. It did not go away —
Sazare saw it there every day thereafter, levelled and damp, maintained
like the herb beds, an offer with no date on it — and neither of them spoke
of it again that year.

---

The wages came at the start of summer and were an ambush.

Tōan put coin on the kitchen table on the last evening of the month, in a
short stack, and pushed it across, and went on eating.

"What's this?"

"Assistant's pay. Back-dated to the timber camp. You've been working this
house for a year — the woodpile's yours, the beds are yours, half the
boiling and carrying's yours, and since the camp you've been in the room
for everything but the women's cases." He reached for the pickles. "I don't
keep apprentices for free. It's bad for them and worse for the trade."

"You feed me. The rice—"

"The rice is the rice," said Tōan, with finality, as though citing statute.

Sazare looked at the stack. It was not large. It was, he understood after a
moment's tradesman's arithmetic — board deducted, honestly, line by line —
exactly right, which from Tōan was a form of tenderness that closing the
whole distance would not have improved on.

He thought of a boy sorting net weights on the other side of a wall of
planks. *The pay's short a cut and the cut goes home.*

"Can you get money to the coast?" he said. "To the exchange. To Anzu."

Tōan went on eating for a moment.

"Hachirō's down on the first of the month," he said. "It rides in the
lamp-oil manifest. Has done for years — you'd not be the first this table's
sent wages down that road." He glanced up, once, brief as the working
light. "The cut's a tenth, for them that are earning. You're not being
asked, mind. Nobody at that exchange would take a tenth off you."

"They didn't ask," said Sazare. "That's why."

He counted it out on the boards — a tenth, and then, because the arithmetic
of it had a taste he wanted more of, a tenth again — pushed the two small
piles to the middle of the table, and sat back, fourteen years old, propertied,
solvent, employed, his whole worldly surplus riding down a mountain next month
in a lamp-oil ledger to people who had written *not fit for work* in his name
and fed him anyway.

It was the first money of his life that anyone had paid him.

It was the first thing since the earring that he had made himself.

"Sazare," said Tōan — the name, plainly, for the first time in fourteen
months of *lad* and *the arm* and *you* — and lifted his cup about an inch
off the table, which was, in the whole time anyone in that valley had known
him, the entire recorded extent of the man's ceremony.

"Tōan," said Sazare, and lifted his.

Outside, the steam went up off the whole breathing country, into the clear
dark, and up the road behind the house the tray of sand sat level on the
porch rail, damp, tended, patient, with no date on it.

Not yet.
