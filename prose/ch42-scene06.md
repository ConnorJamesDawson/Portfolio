# Chapter 42, Scene 6 — The Governor

*Act 4, part D. Konoha General, the rear yard and Kanzaki's bench.
Sazare Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,683 words.*

---

It arrived on the twentieth, in the rear yard, out of a sentence
Kakashi had said in the wet grass three days earlier and had not
considered important.

*A controlled loss of position.*

He had been turning it over for three days as a thing about feet. It
stopped being about feet at about the second hour of a Tuesday
afternoon, standing at the bench with a failed piece in front of him
that had gone the way unseeded stock always went, which was to grit —
and the whole of it came at once, in the order it had always been in,
and he had simply never read it in that order.

*Courses nest. Fine damps coarse. The deepest course conditions
everything laid inside it.*

*Seeded stock fails to pieces. Unseeded stock fails to grit.*

*And every piece is a seed, and every seed carries the deepest course
it was grown around.*

He put the failed piece down.

Then he wrote one line, and it was the only line he wrote for the next
forty minutes.

*Lay the ceiling first.*

---

He ran it nine times over the following eleven days and took it to
Kanzaki on the first of the fifth month.

She heard it out with her hands folded, which was the worst audience in
the village and the only one he wanted.

"Say the mechanism," she said. "Not the intention. I have had four
people in this yard in nine years explain an intention to me and call
it a design."

"The deepest course goes down before anything else. It isn't a payload
and it doesn't do anything on its own — it's a limit. A statement about
the maximum the piece is permitted to deliver, laid as the first
instruction, so that every course after it is nested inside it and
inherits it, the way a fine course inherits the orientation of the
coarse one under it."

"And it holds when the thing breaks."

"That's the part that makes it worth doing." He turned his hand over.
"Seeded stock fails to pieces and every piece is a seed. That has been
true since I was nine and it is the reason the invasion produced
nineteen grains that were nineteen ignitions rather than nineteen
fragments. If the ceiling is the deepest course, then every piece it
fails into carries the ceiling with it, because the piece is a seed and
what a seed carries is the deepest thing in it."

Kanzaki was quiet.

"So it cannot be escalated by being destroyed," she said.

"It cannot be escalated by being destroyed. Which is the entire
objection Hatake put to me in the forecourt fifteen months ago and I
have been calling it a design problem ever since because that was the
half I could work on."

"What does it cost?"

"Thirty-one per cent."

She looked up at that, because it was a number and she was a
quartermaster and numbers were the only thing in the world she took
personally.

"Say it properly."

"A piece with a ceiling course laid first will carry about a third less
than the same piece without one. I've run it nine times over eleven
days on matched stock and the spread is tight — twenty-eight to
thirty-four, mean thirty-one." He put the sheet in front of her. "The
ceiling occupies depth. Depth is capacity. There isn't a clever way
round it and I've looked for one for a fortnight and I've stopped
looking."

"You are proposing," said Kanzaki, "to make everything you build from
now on a third worse."

"Yes."

"Deliberately."

"Yes."

"And you want me to write that in the ledger."

"I want you to write it in the ledger," said Sazare, "in the column you
added for the crystal doctrine three months before I gave you a reason
to need one, and I want the thirty-one per cent in it in figures, so
that in nine years somebody reads it and knows exactly what it cost and
that it was paid on purpose."

---

She did not write it.

She sat back on the stool with her arms folded and looked at him for a
while with the particular expression she used on a consignment whose
paperwork was in order and whose weight was not.

"No," she said. "Not yet. There's a hole in it and I'd rather you found
it than a board did."

"Where."

"Who takes it off."

Sazare opened his mouth and did not have anything.

"You laid the ceiling," said Kanzaki. "You can lay a course. Therefore
you can lay a course over the top of it, or grow a fresh piece without
one, or take a piece down and grow it again, which you have done three
hundred and eleven times and entered every single one of them in my
book. A limit that the man who made it can un-make at will is not a
limit. It's an intention with an extra step, and an intention is what
the four men said."

"It's not the same—"

"Then tell me why, and do not tell me that you wouldn't."

He stood in the yard.

The laundry lines went across above the kitchen roof, the same as
always, and there was a smell of scorched linen coming out of the vent
and the sound of the wringer going and stopping and going, and he stood
there and did the thing he had been doing since he was seven, which was
to take the object apart in his head and look at where the load
actually went.

"Because of where it sits," he said, slowly. "It isn't a component. It
isn't a governor bolted to the outside of a mechanism — that's the
picture I had in my head for a fortnight and it's wrong. It's the
deepest course, so it's the thing everything else is oriented to. You
cannot lift it out. The only way to remove the ceiling from a finished
piece is to take the whole piece down to stock and start again, and at
that point you haven't defeated the limit, you've destroyed the object
and made a new one, and the new object is a new decision that goes in a
book with a date on it."

Kanzaki uncapped the pen.

"That," she said, "is a design."

"It's still me deciding."

"Everything is you deciding. That was never avoidable and anybody who
tells you it is, is selling something." She was writing now, fast, in
the small hand she used for entries she intended to be read. "What you
have done is make the deciding *expensive* and make it *leave a mark*.
That is the whole of what any audit has ever been able to do to any man
in the history of the trade, and I have spent thirty-one years of my
life on it, and I would like it noted that you got there in a fortnight
by talking to a carpenter and a man in a mask."

"That isn't what happened."

"It is *precisely* what happened." She turned the ledger round. "Sign
it. And put the thirty-one per cent in yourself, in your own hand, so
it isn't my figure."

---

He walked home the long way again and did not slow down going past the
market, and got to the persimmon before he understood what he had
actually done that afternoon.

*It is the same move,* he wrote.

*Hatake spent three hours in a wet field making me fall over, and told
me that everything above the level of a thrown knife is a controlled
loss of position — you give up where you are, on purpose, at a moment
you chose, in order to arrive somewhere better than where you were. I
have been unable to do this with my feet forty times in one morning.*

*I did it this afternoon with a third of everything I can make.*

*I gave up thirty-one per cent on purpose, at a moment I chose, and I
did not have to be talked into it and I did not hesitate over it, and
the hesitation is the entire problem in the wet field.*

*So it is not that I cannot give up a position. It is that I can give
one up when I can see what it buys, and I cannot give one up on a
promise that I will arrive somewhere better, because I have never once
in my life arrived somewhere better by letting go of something.*

*That is a different problem and a smaller one and I would like it
written down that it took a fourteen-year-old, a carpenter, a
quartermaster and a jōnin about three weeks to reduce it from the first
to the second.*

He read it back.

Then he added the flattering half, because the ledger got both or it
got neither, and because he had begun — recently, unevenly, and against
a lifetime of practice — to be able to write the flattering half first
without it curdling.

*The piece will be a third worse.*

*It will also be the first thing I have ever built that cannot be made
to do more than I said it would, including by me, including if
somebody breaks it, including if somebody takes it off my body.*

He stopped on that last clause.

He had not planned it. It had come out with the rest of the sentence
and it did not belong to the rest of the sentence, and he went back
through the afternoon looking for where he had picked it up and could
not find it, which happened to him perhaps twice a year and had never
yet been nothing.

*Taken off my body,* he wrote underneath, in the margin. *I own a
notebook, a tin of grit and a coat. Nobody wants any of them.*

*Query: why did I write a clause about something being taken off me?*

He left the query standing, because an unanswered question in his own
hand was worth more than a tidy page, and turned the lamp down.
