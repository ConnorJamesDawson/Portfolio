# Chapter 12, Scene 1 — The Door Man

*Act 2, part C. Sazare Ruri, ages fourteen–fifteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,300 words.*

---

The man who kept the door at the new bathhouse was called Sakai, and the town
knew four things about him: that he came from the Land of Iron, that he had
been there eleven years, that he drank with the seriousness other men brought
to religion, and that the one time three quarry men had decided as a group
that an old drunk doorkeeper was no obstacle to an after-hours soak, all
three had woken up the next morning in the woodshed, stacked, in order of
size, with their boots placed neatly beside them and no memory that survived
telling.

He was fifty-odd and built long, with grey coming through a queue he still
tied in the Iron style, and hands that Sazare had noticed the first month
and filed with everything else he filed: hands that never fidgeted. In a
whole town of men whose fingers rolled coins and picked splinters and drummed
on bath-house rails, Sakai's hands lay on his knees like tools set down at
the end of work.

He carried no sword. There was a bokken by the door stool, weathered black
at the grip, and in his room above the woodshed — the town knew this the way
towns know everything — a long wrapped thing that leaned in the corner and
had never once been seen open.

Sazare watched him for two months before he asked. This was partly prudence
and partly, he admitted to himself, the collection: Sakai was a new kind of
thing, and Sazare studied him the way he studied hands — the way he moved
around the door, which was hardly at all; the way drunk men's aggression
seemed to lose its footing on him, like a wave on a sloped sea-wall; the
way he sat, which was the way Tōan stood at a bench, which was the way
Sazare's mother had sat at a frame.

Stillness that was made of attention. He knew the family it belonged to now.
He had been collecting its members all his life.

---

He asked on a wet evening in his second summer at Yumoto, at the door, with the steam coming off the bath-house eaves and the lamps just lit.

"Will you teach me the sword?"

Sakai looked at him for rather a long time. He had known who Sazare was for
a year in the way the whole town knew — the doctor's lad, the one who mended
the smith's stone — and had never once shown the least interest, which had
been a point in his favour in the collection.

"No," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because you're a tradesman's parcel, and I keep a door." He settled his
back against the post. "And because boys who want the sword want one of
three things — to be feared, to be avenged, or to be somebody — and I've
buried students of all three sorts, and I drink now, which is a better
trade. Go home."

Sazare stood in the rain and thought about it, because it deserved thinking
about, and because *avenged* had gone through him like a nail and he wanted
to be honest about whether it had gone through the middle.

"I don't want any of those," he said, eventually. "I want — there are
people who will come for me one day. Not soon, maybe. But they'll be better
than me, because everyone is better than me, and everything I know how to
do needs my hands free and eleven seconds and somewhere I've already been.
I want the eleven seconds. That's all. I want to not die long enough to
leave."

The rain came off the eaves.

"Say the last part again," said Sakai.

"I want to not die long enough to leave."

Sakai looked at him — top to bottom, one pass, the assessing look, the
fourth or fifth of Sazare's life — and something in the set of his
shoulders changed, the way weather changes before anything visible does
it.

"That," he said, "is the first sensible sentence I have heard from a boy
with empty hands in thirty years." He picked up the bokken and held it out,
grip first, level. "Take it. Don't swing it. God's sake — *don't swing
it.* Just hold it and stand there."

Sazare took it and held it and stood there.

Sakai walked around him once, in the rain, unhurried, and took the bokken
back.

"Terrible," he said, with what might have been satisfaction. "Completely
terrible. Nothing to unteach. Come at first light. Bring money — a jug a
lesson, and I don't haggle with children — and bring white oak from the
doctor's pile, a straight billet, this long." He held his hands apart.
"You'll make your own. You'll cut it wrong twice."

"How do you know I'll cut it wrong twice?"

"Because everyone cuts it wrong twice," said Sakai. "The wood's the first
teacher. I'm the second. Go home."

---

He cut it wrong twice.

The first bokken came out over-thick at the grip because he had left wood
for error, tradesman's habit, and Sakai weighed it in one hand and handed it
back and said "you've made a club for a bigger coward"; the second came out
true to the pattern and dead as a fence post, because he had followed the
pattern, and Sakai flexed it once against the floor and said "and now a
fence post," and Sazare took the third billet away and sat with it on the
porch for a long evening, turning it, reading the grain the way he read
stone — where it was dense, where it would want to give, where the life of
it ran — and cut to what the wood said instead of to the pattern, and
brought back something very slightly irregular that sat in the hand like a
handshake.

Sakai swung it twice, put it down, and said nothing at all, which by then
Sazare had learned was the grade above *fence post* and the grade below a
thing that had never yet been awarded.

The lessons were at first light, in the yard behind the woodshed, before
the baths opened and after Sakai had — this was the arrangement's engine,
and neither of them ever said it aloud — before Sakai had started drinking,
so that the one hour of his day that mattered was also the one hour he was
entirely himself.

And the lessons were, for four months, two things only.

Distance, and the cut.

"Maai," said Sakai, on the first morning, and drew a line in the wet grit
with his heel. "Distance. It's the whole art. Everything else is argument
about it." He stood across the line. "You know it already — you just keep
the knowledge in the wrong room. You told me it yourself: everything you
can do needs somewhere you've already been. A swordsman's *somewhere* is
this." He moved, fractionally, and was somehow a different proposition
entirely. "The two feet in front of me are a country I've already been to.
I know the roads. I know what everything costs there. You're going to
learn one country, two feet wide, and you're going to know it better than
men who've conquered ten."

And the cut: one cut, downward, ten thousand times.

Not three cuts. Not the crosses and thrusts Sazare had half-expected off
the training-yard glimpses of another life. One cut, from the raise to the
stop, done slowly, done wrong, corrected by half-inches, done again —
reps, rows, counts, a book Sakai kept in his head instead of on a shelf,
and within a fortnight Sazare had understood with a kind of interior laugh
that he had been here before; that this was the stones and the knots and
the spring-band; that the sword, as taught by this ruined precise man in
the hour before drink, was *rehabilitation* — the building of a limb that
had never existed — and that every teacher he had ever had, in every
country, in every trade, all of them fallen out of some other life, taught
exactly one syllabus in the end.

Slow. Correct. Again. The body learns what is true for it by it being true
for a long time.

"You're smiling," said Sakai, suspicious, in the third week. "Why are you
smiling. The cut's wrong."

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be *straighter*."

He stopped smiling, mostly, and got straighter, slowly, and paid his jug a
lesson every morning with the mended-jewellery money, and if either of them
noticed that Sakai had begun, some mornings, to let the first drink wait
until the boy had gone — a quarter-hour, then a half — neither of them
said one word about that either.
