# Chapter 22, Scene 4 — Item Six

*Act 4, part A. The Hokage's office. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~2,500 words.*

---

The hearing was on the sixteenth day at the fourth hour of the afternoon
and he was item six.

He found that out standing in the corridor outside, because there was a
slate on the wall by the door with the afternoon's business chalked on it
in a clerk's hand, and it went:

*1. Border returns, NW sector*
*2. Academy intake, spring — numbers*
*3. Kusa transit permits (3)*
*4. Rice contract, Tanaka*
*5. Roof*
*6. Amnesty application — Ruri*
*7. Any other*

He stood in front of it for about four minutes.

He had spent sixteen days in a cell and eleven days on a road and four
hundred miles and a year and a half and the whole of everything getting to
that corridor; and he was after a roof and before *any other*; and he
found, standing there, that the thing he actually felt was not humiliation
at all.

It was relief.

*Item six is survivable*, he thought. *Item six is a Thursday. Nobody has
ever put me on a list where I was one of seven things in my whole life.*

---

The office was not what he expected either, and by then he had stopped
expecting things and was simply making a note each time.

It was a big round room at the top of the building with windows all round
the far side and light coming in flat and gold off the whole village
below, and the roofs going away red-brown in every direction to the trees;
and the four faces on the cliff were straight ahead through the middle
window, at eye level, close enough that Sazare could see the tool marks.

It smelled of pipe smoke, paper, and floor polish.

There was a desk with three separate stacks on it, each about nine inches
high, and a full in-tray and an empty out-tray, and a teacup with a
saucer, and a pen stand with four pens in it and one of them broken.

There were five people in the room.

**An old man behind the desk**, in white and red, in the hat, with a long
pipe in one hand.

**A woman clerk of about forty** at a side table with a ledger open and a
box of forms.

**Ibiki Morino**, standing, arms folded, saying nothing.

**Kanzaki Uta**, sitting, with a leather satchel on her knees.

**And, leaning against the window frame at the far end with his hands in
his pockets and his book nowhere in sight, Hatake Kakashi**, who caught
Sazare's eye for about a quarter of a second and then went back to looking
out of the window.

*I can be in the room when they read it,* he had said, at a causeway head,
in the sun. *Which is worth more than the paper, and is about the end of
what I've got.*

He had come.

Sazare had spent sixteen days assuming that had been a kindness said to a
boy on a road, and had put it in the tray, and now stood on a polished
floor in a round room and had to spend about three seconds getting his
face back.

---

Hiruzen Sarutobi was sixty-eight years old and looked it.

He was not large. That was the first thing and it kept being surprising:
a smallish old man with a white beard cut close round the jaw and deep
lines coming down either side of his mouth and heavy lids, and hands that
had the look Sazare knew from Tōan's — the loosening of the skin over the
tendons that starts somewhere in the sixties and does not stop.

He had spots on the backs of them. He had a burn scar on the first two
fingers of the right hand about thirty years old.

And his eyes, when he put the pipe down and looked up, were extremely
clear and did not go over Sazare the way Toriyama's had, or the way
Kakashi's had in a doorway, or the way Ibiki's did.

They just looked at him.

"Sit down," said the Hokage. "You'll be here about half an hour and there's
no ceremony to any of it and I'd rather you weren't standing up for the
whole thing."

Sazare sat down.

"You've been read," said Hiruzen. "By which I mean I have read all of it,
this morning, from the beginning — Hatake's report, Morino's nine days,
Yamanaka's finding, the assessment, and Kanzaki's annex, which is four
pages longer than anybody else's and is the reason we are having this
meeting today rather than in the spring." A small dry look at the woman
with the satchel, who did not react at all. "So you will not have to say
any of it again. I want you to understand that before we start, because I
am told you have said it a great many times."

"Thank you."

"Now." The old man put both hands flat on the desk. "I am going to grant
your application, with conditions, and then I am going to read you the
conditions and you are going to be very disappointed in about ninety
seconds, and I would rather do it in that order than the other way about."

---

The conditions were these, and the clerk read them, and the Hokage said
the reason for each one himself, which Sazare had not expected and did not
understand the significance of for a considerable time.

**One. No combat training of any kind, with any person, in any discipline,
for the duration of the probationary period.**

"That is the hard one and it is not negotiable," said Hiruzen, "and the
reason is not that we think you dangerous. Assessment says you are not,
and I have read it, and frankly it made rather sad reading. The reason is
that this village cannot be the place that trained you. If in four years'
time you are standing somewhere doing something regrettable, every
government on this continent will ask one question, and it will not be
*who was he*, it will be *who taught him*, and the answer must be *nobody
here*."

**Two. Residence within the village boundary. No exit without written
permission. Assigned liaison, to whom you report your movements daily.**

"Which is a minder, and we will not pretend otherwise, and he will be
perfectly civil and will be extremely bored."

**Three. No forehead protector. No rank, no registration number, no
mission assignment. You are a resident foreign national and nothing else.**

"That one will hurt more than you think it will and rather later than you
expect."

**Four. Every article you fabricate is entered, numbered, and accounted
for. Every one. Including the ones that fail, including the ones you throw
away, including anything you make for your own amusement on a Sunday.**

"That is Kanzaki's clause and she wrote it herself and she will enforce it
without mercy, and I would not cross her."

**Five. Residency is conditional on production. A monthly quota, set at
review, delivered to the quartermaster's fabrication branch. Failure to
meet quota for two consecutive months is grounds for reconsideration of
status.**

"And that," said Hiruzen Sarutobi, "is the one I dislike, so I shall give
you the true reason for it rather than the official one. A man who is here
on sufferance and has nothing to do becomes a problem for everybody
including himself. A man with a quota has somewhere to be on Tuesday. It
is not generous. It is not intended to be. It is simply the only thing
that has ever worked."

The clerk turned the form round on the side table and put a pen beside it.

---

Sazare Ruri sat in a chair in a round room at the top of a tower and
looked at a piece of paper.

And the whole of it arrived at once, the way things did with him: not as
an idea but as an *object*, laid out flat on a bench where he could see
the joins.

*They are keeping me alive because I am productive.*

*That is the arrangement. Those are the terms. A monthly figure, delivered
to a counter, entered in a ledger, audited item by item including the
failures — and in exchange the state does not do to me the things it is
otherwise entitled to do, and my continued existence in this place is
reviewed against the number.*

*That is the Ruri contract. That is the exact contract, in the same words,
that my family were on for four hundred years, that my grandmother thought
was a privilege and my father thought was a career and my mother thought
was a wall round the district; and it is the contract that killed every
one of them, because a household that is kept for its output is a
household that is nothing else when the output stops being wanted.*

*I have walked four hundred miles.*

*I have crossed three countries and buried two people and put a woman in
the sea and left a piece of myself in a grave on a hill, and I have arrived
at the far side of the world, in the safest place there is, and asked, out
loud, in daylight, with my hands out from my sides, to be put back on it.*

*And this time I am going to sign it.*

Nobody in that room was being cruel. That was the part that went all the
way through. There was a woman at a side table who had been perfectly
pleasant to him; a small grey person who had fought for four pages to get
him this hearing in the eleventh month rather than the third; a man at the
window who had walked eleven days and come to a Thursday meeting he did
not have to attend; and an old man behind a desk who was giving him the
true reasons instead of the official ones, one at a time, to his face.

Every single one of them was doing their job correctly and with better
manners than anybody in the Land of Water had ever bothered with.

Sazare picked up the pen and signed it.

---

"Now," said Hiruzen, when the clerk had taken the form away. "Two things,
and then you can go and be shown where you sleep.

"The first is administrative and you will not like it. The monitoring
arrangement is not standard. Ordinarily a probationary resident is
reported on through the district office, monthly, and the file goes into
general circulation." He picked his pipe up and looked at it rather than
lighting it. "In your case the liaison reports to me. Personally. Daily,
in writing, to this desk, and I have had that put on the order in those
words rather than left as a courtesy, because orders in writing are read by
people and courtesies are not.

"That will mean, in practice, that you are watched considerably more
closely than anybody else in your position, by name, at the direct
instruction of the Hokage, and that everyone in this village who reads
personnel orders will know it inside a fortnight."

Sazare sat with his hands on his knees.

"All right," he said, and heard how flat it came out.

"Mm," said Hiruzen. "You have understood that as a measure of how little I
trust you."

"Yes."

"Good," said the old man, and put the pipe in his mouth and did not
explain, and looked at him with those extremely clear eyes for about two
seconds longer than the moment needed; and Ibiki Morino, by the wall,
shifted his weight very slightly, and Kakashi at the window did not move
at all.

---

"The second thing is not administrative," said Hiruzen, "and it is the
only question I am going to ask you, and you can decline it.

"What did your mother make?"

Sazare looked up.

"I'm sorry?"

"Your mother." The old man gestured with the pipe stem at the stack of
paper on his desk. "There are four separate documents on this desk
describing what you can do to a human being. Nobody has written down one
line about what your family actually *made*, because it did not seem
relevant to any of them, and I have been reading forms for forty years and
have learned to be suspicious of the parts nobody thinks are relevant."

And Sazare — who had answered questions for nine days from a man with a
ruined head, and had had a stranger walk through the worst four minutes of
his life, and had stood in a shed and watched *NIL* written down four
times — found that this was the one that got him.

"Ornament," he said.

"Go on."

"Earrings. Hair-pins. Drop pendants, mostly — that was the house
speciality, a tapered drop with a facet cut on the shoulder, and my
mother's were the best in the district because she got the shoulder right
and nobody else did. Beads. Inlay for boxes. Belt fittings for people who
wanted to be seen to afford them." He heard his own voice steady out as it
went, the way it always did once his hands would have been moving. "She
grew about nine thousand pieces in her working life, near enough, and
about a third of them went to the guild afterwards to be inscribed and
came out as ninja goods and the rest went out on trestles in a market to
women who wanted something nice.

"She was forty-one. She could hold a piece under a lattice for about six
hours without stopping, and I have never met anybody else who could, and I
don't think she ever once knew it was unusual."

The office was quiet. Down in the village somebody was hammering.

"Thank you," said Hiruzen Sarutobi. "That is the most useful thing in the
file."

---

At the door, with the clerk waiting to take him down, the old man said:

"One more. This is not a condition and it is not in the terms and I am
telling you rather than writing it anywhere.

"You may not train. That is absolute and if I hear of you being taught a
single technique by a single person I will end this arrangement the same
afternoon and I will be sorry about it and I will do it anyway.

"There is nothing whatever in those terms about *reading*."

Sazare stopped.

"The Academy building on the east side has a public reading room on the
second floor," said Hiruzen. "It is open to any resident of this village
six days a week from the second hour to the tenth, it is free, and it is
mostly full of eleven-year-olds doing their homework badly. I would not
have thought to mention it — except that you have asked four questions in
this office this afternoon and three of them were about how things work,
and one of them was about the roof."

He put the pipe back in his mouth.

"Go and read," said the Third Hokage. "Nobody has to give you permission."
