# Chapter 8, Scene 3 — Fourteen Hours in One

*Act 1, beats 22–23. Sazare Ruri, age thirteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,800 words.*

---

A growing-frame is a bed of sand with a copper lattice over it and it takes four
hours, and the reason it takes four hours is not that crystal is slow.

Crystal is not slow. Crystal will come out of a medium as fast as you let it. The
four hours are the *holding back* — the whole discipline, the thing his mother did
with her forearms not moving and the thing he did with a bowl, is a person
standing between a bloodline and its own speed and saying *not yet, not yet, not
yet* for two hundred and forty minutes, because a thing that comes fast comes
cloudy and a thing that comes all at once does not come at all.

Everybody had told him that. Nobody had ever told him the other half of it, which
was what happened if you stopped saying *not yet*.

His family had known. Of course they had known. It was in a lacquer box on the
second shelf and he had never been tall enough.

---

There was no sand.

That was the entire problem and he had about a quarter of a second to solve it,
and he solved it the way you solve things at thirteen, which is by not
understanding that it is a problem.

He had grown crystal out of a wooden tray of grey river sand nine hundred times
because a wooden tray of grey river sand was what there was in the north room. He
had never once asked why sand. He had asked why crystal clouded and why the frames
hummed and why nobody could tell him what the marks said, and he had never asked
the only question that mattered, which was *what is the sand for* — and if he had
asked, somebody would have told him that it is a medium, and that a medium is
anything wet with something in it, and that the reason you use river sand is that
it is cheap and it is level and it is *not part of you*.

His hands were up and open with the heels turned in.

The nearest medium was his own blood.

---

It came out of the palm of his right hand.

It came out of the palm and through the back of the hand at the same time, because
it did not know which way out was, and it went through the small bones on the way,
and it kept going.

It came out fast and it came out clear.

There was a sound. He heard it perfectly and would hear it at intervals for the
rest of his life, and it was not a scream or a crack; it was the dry high note of
nine frames humming in a cold room, all at once, out of one hand, at a volume that
made the people on either side of him flinch away.

The spike went eleven yards.

It was as thick as a wrist at the base and it tapered, and it was blue, and it was
absolutely clear all the way through — not one flaw in it, not one bubble, four
hundred years of a family's craft delivered in about a fifth of a second by a
thirteen-year-old who had never made anything better than an earring — and it went
over the barrier and over the heads of the front three rows and it took the
executioner just under the collarbone and out through the back and about four feet
beyond, and it stopped him mid-step.

He did not fall over. That was the thing. The crystal was in the platform behind
him and he was on it, and he stayed up.

---

Sazare's arm came apart from the wrist to just below the elbow.

He did not know that at the time. He knew that something had happened that had no
category — not pain, not yet, just a total loss of information from the elbow
down, and the smell of hot metal, and the fact that his hand was no longer a shape
he recognised in the corner of his eye.

The forearm had split along the line of the bone where it had gone out. There were
three separate exits and one of them had taken the tendon with it.

He would lose about a third of the movement in that hand and all of the strength
in the last two fingers, and he would keep the arm, which was more than any medic
who ever looked at it afterwards could account for.

---

For about a second and a half the entire square did nothing at all.

Then, from about four places at once around the edge of it, the insurgents moved.

---

He had it wrong for years. He thought — because he was thirteen and because it was
the only shape available to him — that he had done something and that they had
come, and it took a long time and somebody being cruel to him on purpose before he
understood the actual sequence.

There were nineteen of them in that square. They had been in it since the second
hour. Four on the ice-house wall dressed as roofers, two at the chestnut stall,
five in the crowd at the top of the square, and eight in the alley behind the
platform with the barrier at their back.

They had been waiting for the signal, which was not going to come for another six
minutes.

The plan was for the executioner. Not to save him — to *use* him. He was the point
at which every guard in that square would be facing one way, at one man, at one
moment, and the eight in the alley would come over the back of the platform in
the four seconds while everybody's eyes were on the sword.

Sazare had put a spike through him a hundred and forty seconds early.

---

What happened then took about ninety seconds and he saw perhaps a fifth of it.

He was on his knees. The crowd went away from him in every direction at once,
which is what four hundred people do, and it took the two women who had been
talking about the marriage with it, and a great deal of the barrier.

He saw the four on the ice-house wall come down. He saw one of them hit the
platform and go straight through the officials, and he saw the old one, the one
who had come out too early and been uncomfortable in the sun, go over the front
edge of it into the crowd.

He saw eight men come over the back of the platform, and they were four seconds
too early or a hundred and forty seconds too late, and it did not matter, because
what mattered was the other thing.

What mattered was the guards.

---

There were twenty-two men on that barrier and every one of them had a standing
order for exactly this, and it was not complicated, and it was not cruelty, and
Sazare would spend a great deal of his life explaining that to himself in various
rooms.

If a sentence is interrupted, you carry out the sentence.

You do not defend the prisoners. You do not withdraw them. The entire purpose of
producing nine people on a platform in a public square is that they are produced,
and if somebody comes over the wall to take them off you, then the one outcome
that cannot be permitted is that they are taken off you.

There were four men on the platform already, because there were always four men on
the platform.

They were closer than the eight in the alley by about nine feet.

---

He saw it.

He was on his knees eleven yards away with his right arm in pieces and he had a
clear line and he saw all of it and he never once afterwards claimed that he had
not.

He saw his uncle Iwao go first, because Iwao was standing between his niece and
the nearest man and had been for fourteen months, and it worked for about one
second, which is the longest it was ever going to work.

He saw his mother.

He saw his father go down half a step later, still looking at the crowd.

And he saw Kohaku — who had her hands tied in front of her and not behind, because
the steps were steep — get one arm up.

She got it up. That was the thing he could never put anywhere. She was
twenty-three days off starvation and had been in a cell for fourteen months and she
saw it coming and she got both bound hands up and turned her shoulder into it, and
she was fast, she was so fast, she was faster than the man was —

and then she was not.

---

The eight from the alley reached the platform about four seconds later.

They killed the four men on it in about six, which was very fast, and one of them
stood there for a moment afterwards with his hands out looking at the nine people
on the boards as though there were still something to be done about it.

Then somebody shouted something and they went.

---

Six insurgents died in the square. Two more died on the north cutting inside the
hour, and one was taken alive at the west quay in the evening, which the whole
district heard about by the following afternoon.

Nine prisoners had been produced.

Nine prisoners had been sentenced.

The sentence had been carried out.

It was, from the point of view of the man with the folder, an untidy morning that
had concluded correctly, and Sazare read the notice about it eleven days later on
the same board, in the same hand, under a notice about the summer mooring fees.
