# Chapter 29, Scene 4 — The Call

*Act 4, part C. Konoha General. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare.
~1,091 words.*

---

Yagi loaded the pattern on the morning of the thirtieth day, with
Fujita's signature witnessing and Urushibara's seal already cut into
the chip the night before, and the whole procedure took considerably
less time than the twenty-nine days that had gone into making it
possible.

She worked the way she worked every dressing change, without ceremony,
narrating each step for the chart rather than for anybody in the room:
pressure, direction, the particular rhythm her chakra fell into when
it was doing this specific job on this specific vessel, held steady
for the eight seconds it always took, and then, in the last half
second, redirected — not into Daichi, but into the chip Sazare held
against the dressing's edge, where the store took it whole and settled
into the granite the way water settles into a level bowl.

"That's it," she said. "It'll hold that exactly, and nothing else,
until it fires or it degrades. Whichever comes first."

"How long before it degrades."

"Urushibara says a week, conservatively. I'd plan for less and be
pleased if we got more."

She pressed the tab into place under the fresh dressing, checked it
lifted cleanly with two fingers, and pressed it flat again.

"Pull that if you want it gone," she told Daichi. "For any reason. You
don't need one."

"I know."

"I'm saying it again because I'm going to keep saying it every time I
change this dressing, and if that gets tedious, good — it means you've
stopped needing to hear it."

---

Sazare kept his own observations that week from the second-floor desk,
close enough to feel the chip without needing to be in the room, the
way he could feel any of his own work now without looking directly at
it — a faint, steady presence at the edge of attention, the kind of
thing a person stops noticing consciously and would notice instantly
if it went quiet.

It did not go quiet.

He checked it perhaps thirty times over five days, mostly without
meaning to, the way his hand went to a tool in his pocket before he'd
decided he needed one. Each time it answered the same: intact, full,
waiting, a small closed door with somebody standing patiently on the
other side of it.

The fifth day was a bad one for reasons that had nothing to do with
Daichi. Two infections in the same afternoon, both requiring Fujita
and both requiring him at once, and a delivery from the western
outpost that put six new casualties on the ward inside an hour with
half the staff already committed elsewhere. Yagi was three floors down
in the surgical wing before noon and did not come up again until well
past the hour Daichi's dressing was due.

Sazare was at the desk with the observation boards when the call came,
not from Daichi's bell but from the auxiliary two beds down, who put
her head round the screen with the particular urgency of somebody who
had seen blood and knew exactly whose bed it belonged to.

"He's bleeding again. He was helping somebody with a stretcher — I
told him not to — and now the dressing's soaked through."

"Where's Yagi."

"Surgical. She won't be up for ten minutes at least."

Sazare reached for the chip.

It answered the way it always did, immediate and familiar, his own
signature meeting the route he had grown into it rather than the
patient's own — not the store itself, which belonged to Daichi and
would never answer anyone else, but the thin thread of recognition
laid down the day he'd grown the crystal, the same thread that had
let him find a fragment on a rooftop in a burning street and know, at
twenty yards, that it was still his to reach.

He sent the activation pulse down it. Small. Nothing like the four
seconds a wall had ever cost him. A key turning, not a door being
built.

The route answered, and beyond it, faint and immediate at once the
way a sound arrives before the mind has finished placing its
direction, he felt the store discharge — not into himself, not
anywhere he could follow it, simply *gone*, spent, the door on the
other side finally opening onto whatever it had been holding for five
days.

He had no way to know, from the desk, whether it had worked.

He went anyway, as fast as the leg allowed, which was not fast, and
reached the bed forty seconds after the auxiliary's first shout to
find her with both hands already pressed to the dressing, Daichi pale
and still and swearing quietly through his teeth, and the bleeding —
when she lifted her hands for the half second it took to check —
slowed to something that looked, unmistakably, like a wound already
partway toward stopping rather than one still actively losing ground.

"It's not as bad," the auxiliary said, sounding faintly surprised at
her own observation. "I don't know why. I didn't do anything
different."

"You didn't need to," Sazare said. "Keep the pressure on. It's already
done the part it was going to do."

Yagi arrived four minutes later, having run the last flight, and had
the dressing off and the vessel properly resealed inside ninety
seconds of arriving, working with the same unhurried economy she
brought to everything urgent, which Sazare had learned, over four
months, to read as focus rather than calm.

"How long was it bleeding before she called it," Yagi asked, not
looking up.

"Under a minute, by the auxiliary's account."

"And the discharge."

"Fired within four seconds of the call reaching me. I felt it go."

Yagi finished the seal, checked the tracking of the graft's edge with
two fingers, and sat back on her heels.

"It's slower than it would have been with a hand actually on it," she
said. "It bought time. It didn't stop the bleeding on its own — I
still had to come and finish the job. But it bought the exact eight
seconds it was built to buy, at the exact moment nobody in this
building had eight seconds free to give him, and that is not a small
thing to have proven."

Daichi, still pale, still swearing intermittently under his breath at
a volume that suggested he had stopped caring whether anyone heard,
opened one eye.

"Eight seconds again," he said.

"Yes."

"Feels like considerably longer when you're the one bleeding."

"I imagine it does."

"Still counts, though."

"Yes," Sazare said. "It still counts."
