# Chapter 58, Scene 6 — You Did Tell Me

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1830 words.*

---

He got the lamp on and a felt down and sat on the yard stone, and she
came off the corner of the bench onto the felt, and for a few minutes
neither of them said anything, which was ordinary.

Then he said:

"On Monday morning I found out what happened to my hand."

"Your hand," said Katsuyu.

"The fifteenth of the eighth month. The petrification."

She did not say anything.

"A man told me on a roof. He said it wasn't a crystal accident. He said
it was the ceiling of the art — that if you take in more than you can
hold and can't spend it and it doesn't go away, you go to stone from the
point of contact outward, painlessly, and that is how sages die, and
that is the only way they die, and that most of the people who ever went
near it are statues."

He looked at the felt.

"He said I should be one."

"Yes," said Katsuyu.

---

He had known she would say it and it went through him anyway.

"You knew."

"I knew on the first evening," said Katsuyu. "Before you sat down. You
came out of the shed with the lamp in your left hand and I saw the palm
and I knew what it was, and I have known every Friday since."

"You never said."

"No."

"Katsuyu—"

"I am going to answer you," she said, "and my first answer will be true
and insufficient and I will give it to you anyway, because it is part of
it and because leaving it out would be a kind of arranging.

"I have no word for it.

"There is no name for that in anything I have. We do not name it. It is
not that the name is secret — there has never been one. It is what
happens, in the way that falling is what happens, and no one at
Shikkotsu has ever needed to say a word about it to anybody because
everyone there can see it."

"That's not enough."

"No," said Katsuyu. "It is not. Ask the rest."

---

*Right. Ask it properly and do not dress it.*

"You have been teaching me since the eighth month," said Sazare. "Nineteen
evenings. Seventy-two corrections, and I have got every one of them
written down.

"And on the first of those evenings you were sitting four feet from a
seventeen-year-old with a patch on his palm that you knew was the mark
of a thing that kills everybody it happens to, and you started teaching
him.

"And in three months you did not say one word about it, and I found out
from a stranger on a roof, and I have got to ask you why and I do not
want to."

Katsuyu was quiet.

"I did tell you," she said.

"You didn't."

"Ruri. Listen to me and then decide.

"What have I taught you?"

---

He opened his mouth to answer and then did not.

*Say it in order. Nineteen evenings. What is in the book.*

*The faults in the hands. The take, which is a third over. The grip,
which is twice the necessary effort. The close, which is two acts and
not one and which I have no report of.*

*And then the exercise.*

*Two, for seventeen weeks. Three at the fifth of the ninth. Four. Five.
Six. Eight. Nine. Eleven. Fifteen.*

*And that is all of it.*

*That is the entire content of nineteen evenings.*

He sat on the yard stone in the cold with the lamp between them and got
there, and it was the worst four seconds he had had in a week.

"Capacity," he said.

"Yes."

"You have never taught me one thing about power."

"No."

"Not once. In three months. Not one application, not one use, not one
thing about making anything bigger or harder or faster — I have got
about forty pages and there is not a single line in any of it about what
to *do* with it."

"No," said Katsuyu.

"That was the warning."

"That was the warning," she said, "and it was every evening, and it was
the whole of the curriculum, and there has not been one minute of it
that was about anything else."

---

He put his hands over his face for a moment, which was a thing he had
seen other people do.

"I logged it as an exercise," he said.

"I know."

"I put the number in a column. Nineteen times. At the bottom of the
page, in the ordinary place, under the day's work — two, and then three,
and then nine, and then fifteen — and I have got a whole set of pages
where the only thing about the most important thing in my life is a
figure in a column with nothing under it."

"I know that as well," said Katsuyu. "I have watched you write it."

"Then why didn't you—"

He stopped.

"Because you assumed I could see it," he said.

"Yes."

"Because everyone at Shikkotsu can."

"Yes," said Katsuyu. "And that is my error and it is mine entirely, and I
want it stated properly and not softened, because you have just done a
difficult thing standing up in the cold and I am not going to do a
lesser one sitting down.

"I have no model of a creature that would need it said.

"I am four hundred years old and I have never in my life met a thing
that could do what you do and not know what it was, and when I found
one I did not recognise the situation, because there has never been one.

"You were doing it. I could see you doing it. I corrected the faults in
the doing and I built you the only thing that would keep you alive, week
by week, and I did all of that without once considering that the person
in front of me did not know the shape of the room he was standing in.

"That is not a decision I made. It is a thing I failed to think of, and
that is worse, and I have understood it in the last three minutes and I
would like to say that I am sorry, and I have said that four times in
four hundred years and I do not think I say it well."

---

He sat and looked at the felt.

*Both of us came up here to pay something and neither of us owed it and
we have both been carrying it for six days.*

*And hers is real and mine wasn't.*

"You did the one thing that mattered," he said.

"I do not think that follows."

"It follows. I've got the arithmetic and it's not generous, it's just
right.

"You could have told me in the eighth month and it would have taken you
about forty seconds and I would have written it down and it would have
gone in a book with a bracket round it.

"Instead of which you spent nineteen evenings building me the margin,
and I have gone from two to fifteen, and I did not know what I was
doing and it worked anyway, and it worked because it does not require me
to know.

"If you had explained it to me I would have understood it perfectly and
had two."

Katsuyu did not say anything.

"That's not a kindness," said Sazare. "I don't do those. It's the
finding."

---

"There's a second thing," he said, "and I was going to keep it until
Monday but I've been sitting here for four minutes not doing it very
well."

"You have been holding your left hand still on purpose since you sat
down," said Katsuyu, "which you do when there is something you have
decided not to raise, and you have done it on four occasions and raised
it every time."

"...Yes. Right.

"The exercise has broken. I did it on Tuesday for the first time in six
days and I could not get past six, and it isn't the six days. It's that
I know what it is now. There's a thing in the middle and the thing in
the middle is me watching, and it needs me to go still, and I can't go
still while I'm watching myself go still."

"No."

"You're not surprised."

"I am not surprised," said Katsuyu, "and I would have been very
surprised by the opposite, and I will tell you what to do about it and
it will take one sentence and you will not like it."

"Go on."

"Do it every day and be bad at it for some weeks."

---

He sat there.

"That's it?"

"That is it. There is no method. The watching wears out — it is not
patient, it is not interested in a thing that has happened four hundred
times, and it will get bored before you do. Six will be nine and nine
will be fifteen and it will take some weeks and there is nothing else in
it at all."

"Some weeks."

"Some weeks."

"I've never—" He stopped. "I have never in my life had a fault where the
answer was *wait*."

"No," said Katsuyu. "You have not. And that is the second thing I did not
think of, and I am beginning to suspect there is a great deal in this
that I have not thought of, and I find I do not mind that either."

---

"There's a word for it," he said.

"For what?"

"For what I am. What we do. He gave me one on Monday and I've been
carrying it around for four days like something in a bag."

"Say it."

"Senjutsu," said Sazare. "And the people who do it are sages."

Katsuyu was still for a moment.

"That is the human word," she said. "Yes. I have heard it. Tsunade has
used it perhaps twice in front of me and I did not attend to it because
it did not seem to be about anything.

"You should know that it is not our word and we have not got one, and if
you go to Shikkotsu and use it they will understand you and think it a
strange thing to have named."

"Have you taught it before?"

"No."

He looked up.

"Never," said Katsuyu. "Not in four hundred years. There have been
humans on that mountain and there have been humans on our contract and
there has been one on it for nineteen years and she has never once asked.

"I have never taught anybody anything, Ruri. That is not modesty. I did
not know I could until the second Friday and I did not know that I liked
it until the sixth, and I have not said either of those things out loud
before this evening."

The lamp buzzed on the stool.

"You are the only one," she said. "You have been the only one since the
eighth month. I had assumed you knew that as well."
