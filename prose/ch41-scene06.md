# Chapter 41, Scene 6 — Not Answering

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the west market and the room over the persimmon
tree. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,855 words.*

---

He wrote the letter on the Tuesday.

He wrote it four times, which he noticed on the fourth and did not stop
for, and it was the best technical writing he had ever done. Eleven
hundred words. The four media and the reject rates. The source column
and the fourteen months of his own handwriting. The seventy per cent
and why it was not a loss. The sentence about the handing over, which
came out on the second draft exactly as it had come out in the office
and which he could not improve in two more attempts.

No greeting. He had no name to put in one.

No signature either, which he sat with for a while, because the
symmetry of it was pleasing and pleasing was not a reason.

He read the whole of it back at about the eleventh hour with the lamp
low and the persimmon making its four-in-the-morning noises against the
gutter two months early, and it was correct, and it was complete, and
it was the clearest account of the finding that existed anywhere
including inside his own head.

Then he folded it, and put it in the drawer, and did not send it.

---

He did the accounting the next day because he did not trust the
decision until he had.

He wrote it out as a ledger, because a ledger was the only form he had
ever been able to make himself be honest in, and he headed the two
columns the way he always did.

*What telling Tsunade cost.*

*One night of the Hokage's sleep. One morning of Shizune's, at second
hand, plus a stack of forty signatures that went on the floor and did
not get signed and will now be signed late by a woman who had them in
order.*

*A study design, which will go to a review board of eleven people, four
of whom will object to it correctly and two of whom will object to it
for reasons of standing, and it will take between four and nine months
and it will come back changed.*

*Six practitioners required to work in silence for the length of the
trial, which is a thing I am asking of six people who did not ask to be
asked.*

*A sentence I am not permitted to write, ever, that I would have
written by the end of the month.*

*My name on a piece of policy in a village I have lived in for two
years, which is a thing that can be looked at by anybody, and which
will be.*

He looked at that column for a long time. It was long, and every line
in it was a real cost to a real person with a name, and none of it was
a complaint, because a cost is what you get when you hand a finding to
somebody who is actually able to do something with it. That was not a
flaw in Tsunade. That was the entire definition of Tsunade. A thing
given to the Hokage becomes a process, because the Hokage is a process
with a face on it, and the alternative to the process is that the
finding sits in a drawer being true and helping nobody.

Then he headed the second column.

*What telling him would cost.*

He sat with the pencil over it.

*Nothing.*

*He has no review board. He has no ward and no six practitioners and no
eleven people, and he cannot make me do a study or stop me writing a
sentence, and if I sent him eleven hundred words on Thursday the only
thing that would happen anywhere in the world is that on some other
Thursday I would get eleven hundred words back.*

*He would simply be interested.*

*It would cost nothing at all.*

He put the pencil down.

*And that is the sales pitch.*

*I have spent eleven years pricing things. Every single object I have
ever valued, I valued by what it cost to make and what it cost the man
holding it to give it up, and I have never once been wrong about a
stone or a setting or a hallmark, and here is a transaction where the
entire cost side of the ledger reads zero.*

*Nothing costs nothing. A thing that appears to cost nothing is a thing
whose cost has not been entered yet, and the only reason I cannot see
the entry is that I am not being shown it, which is not the same as it
not existing, and I have made exactly that mistake once before in my
life over a man with a very good coat.*

He wrote the last line of the honest half and did not enjoy it.

*None of the above is why I am not sending it.*

*I am not sending it because I read it back at the eleventh hour and
what I felt was not caution. It was relief. I had found somebody to
tell. And the letter said it would think less of me for answering
quickly, and I have been walking around for two months believing that
line was a courtesy, and it is not a courtesy, it is a lock that opens
outward — the longer I do not answer, the more I feel I have earned the
answering.*

*I can see the mechanism. I can draw it. I am still in it.*

---

He went to the west market on the second Thursday anyway.

It was the seventeenth, clear and hard, one of those late-frost
mornings where the sky goes an enormous washed-out blue with nothing in
it at all and the shadows on the north side of the street keep the
white until noon. The market ran along the wall under the old cedars,
and the fish came in on carts at five and were gone by ten, and by nine
the whole of that end smelled of ice-water and cut cedar and the sour
bright edge of the brine barrels.

The man who brought the fish was between fifty and sixty. Brown to the
elbow on both arms and pale above, which meant thirty summers of
sleeves rolled to the same place. He wore a padded jacket that had been
blue once, mended at the right shoulder in a grey thread that did not
match and had been chosen by somebody who owned grey thread rather than
somebody who owned blue. Boots wrong for the trade, army-cut, the left
heel worn down the outside — a man who had been given boots at some
point in his life and had never bought another pair because these had
not finished yet.

His hands were the answer and Sazare had them inside four seconds.

Nicks across the backs of the fingers, dozens, all old, all healed, all
running the same way. The thumb pad flat and shining. Nails cut to
nothing and the quick of them stained. A callus on the outside edge of
the right index where a knife had sat against it for three decades.

Not a shinobi's hands. Not anywhere near a shinobi's hands. The
callusing was in every place a fish knife puts it and in none of the
places anything else does, and the wear on the boots said standing and
walking and had nothing whatsoever in it of running or of landing.

Sazare bought two mackerel.

The man wrapped them, told him they were better the next day than they
were today and to keep them cold and not in the window, took his coin,
counted the change into his palm without looking at it, and said the
same four words to him that he said to the woman behind him.

That was all of it.

He walked back up the hill with the fish under his arm and did not look
round once, and by the time he reached the persimmon he had it.

*He is a fishmonger.*

*He has been a fishmonger for thirty years and he has never held a
weapon and he does not know a single thing, and the letter told me that
in advance and told me the truth, and I went and checked anyway.*

*Checking was the part to look at.*

*I did not go to that market to catch anybody. I went because standing
four feet from the only physical object in this village that connects
to that letter was the closest I could get to answering it without
answering it.*

*I have found a way to reach for it that doesn't leave a receipt.*

*So the correct entry is not that I did not send the letter. The
correct entry is that I did not send the letter and then spent forty
minutes and eleven coins getting as near to sending it as the geometry
allowed, and I would have written the first version of this entry as a
success.*

He cooked the mackerel badly, on the wrong heat, which he had done
every time for two years and had never once bothered to correct
because nobody else ate at that table.

---

He counted it out at the low table afterwards, because it was the sort
of thing that only stayed honest if it went on paper.

*Who can hear the whole of it.*

*Kanzaki has the material half and does not have the medical half and
would not want it. Yagi has the medical half and takes my word for the
material half, which is generous and is not the same as understanding
it. Emiko can hear any of it and cannot use it. Ashida would listen for
two hours out of loyalty and would be bored inside four minutes and
would not say so.*

*Two.*

*There are two people alive who can hold both halves at once. One of
them is the Hokage of this village, who gave me four hours she did not
have, and stopped me from writing a sentence that would have hurt
people for a century, and will make me pay for the finding in reviews
and signatures and other people's time — which is the price of it
being real.*

*The other one reads my post.*

*I would like there to be more than two. That is not a complaint about
either of them. It is the actual shape of the problem and I have been
calling it other things for two months.*

He put the pencil down, and then picked it up again, because the
flattering half had gone in first for once and the ledger got both or
it got neither.

*Not this Thursday.*

*I have not written "not ever" and I want that noted, because I looked
at "not ever" for some time this evening and I could not make myself
write it down, and a man who cannot write a sentence is telling himself
something.*

The drawer stayed shut. The letter stayed in it.

He went to bed at the second hour with the room smelling of burnt fish
and got about four hours, and dreamed, as far as he could recall
afterwards, of absolutely nothing.
