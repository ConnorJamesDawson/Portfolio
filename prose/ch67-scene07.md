# Chapter 67, Scene 7 — The Front of Four Books

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard and the north
fields. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1070 words.*

---

Sunday was for the gear, and the gear took twenty minutes, and the
rest of the day took its own shape around the fact of Monday the way
the water had taken its shape around her.

Tenzō came by the yard at ten, off-clock — clause four governed live
working and Sunday had none — and stood his eight feet with a paper
bag of chestnuts he had roasted himself and shared out without
comment, and watched Sazare strip and re-roll the tool roll twice.

"First training posting?" said Tenzō, who knew the answer from the
file.

"First anything posting. I was never a genin. There is a rank in my
record now and nobody, including the records office, can say what
it is." He squared the roll. "Four mornings a week, about a year.
He said he would build the style as well as the man, because nobody
has been one before."

"Mm," said Tenzō. He ate a chestnut with the deliberation of a man
deciding how much to say, and decided, and said it mildly, at the
mildness he kept for the true things: "I was built by people who
started from the man they wanted and worked backwards. It makes
weapons. Jiraiya-sama starts from the man he's got and works
*outwards*. It makes people who happen to be dangerous. I've read
every file that touches him and I have never found one he ruined."
He held out the bag. "You'll keep the bench. That's the part I came
to say. Whatever the mornings make of you, the afternoons will
still know the way here."

Sazare took a chestnut.

"That is the most words you have said in six days."

"I ration," said Tenzō. "It's the arrangement."

---

He did the books in the evening, all four, because the creed was in
the front of all four and he wanted to read it in his own
sixteen-year-old hand one more time while it was still the standing
text.

*I am not going to get stronger. Find out how it works.*

Fifteen, when he wrote it first, on a boat, with a hand that had
just healed wrong. It had been a lifeboat then, and later a bench
lamp, and later still a boundary wall, and he knew — had known
since a bathhouse in December, since *that's not the creed* — that
on some morning in the next year a man with white hair was going to
walk him out into a field and take a bar to it, and that the man
was right to, and that it was going to cost.

He did not revise it. Revision was not his to begin tonight;
tonight was for reading the survey stakes before the ground was
opened.

But he wrote, underneath it, in the fourth book only, in the small
exact hand of the evening entries:

*Amendment pending. The author at fifteen did not know that a
structure can be asked to hold more than itself. He built for one
occupant. The load case has changed: there is a sound now, and the
sound has to be protected by somebody, and the somebody is going to
have to be strong enough for the job whether the creed approves of
him or not.*

*Find out how it works — that stands, that is the whole of me, that
goes on the last page of the last book whenever the last book gets
written.*

*But the first half is under review, effective tomorrow, six sharp,
the north fields.*

*Bring the question. Bring the sword. Do not bring the protocol —
the world has never once honoured it, and he is going to be the
fifth deep end in three months, and the finding from the other four
is unanimous: I do not learn to swim until the water closes.*

---

Monday came up iron-grey and hard-frozen, the good kind of cold,
the kind that made every sound in the village arrive signed.

She was awake when he left, which was not the arrangement, and made
no pretence of it being coincidence — she stood at the top of the
stairs in the grey light in her robe with her arms folded, the
whole tower asleep under her, and looked at him the way she had
looked at the ledger at Tarugi, once, completely, everything on the
page at one pass.

"Noon," she said. "Every training day. Whatever state he sends you
back in, the noons are mine. That's not a request, it's in the
diary, and the diary doesn't lie."

"Noon," he agreed.

"And Sazare." She said the name the way she signed things she meant
to be permanent. "Don't let the old fool see the counting. He'll
want to know what you're counting, and it's none of his."

*She saw the counting.*

*Of course she saw the counting. File under: she sees everything.
Amend nothing. That she watched me file her laugh and said nothing
for a day and a half and is sending me to a field with it as a
parting gift is itself going in the book, tonight, whatever state
he sends me back in.*

"Nothing I count is his," said Sazare, "and one thing I count is
going to outlast every list I have ever kept."

He went down the outside stair into the iron morning with the sword
on his back and the capped question in his coat, and the gate of
the north fields stood open when he reached it, frost on the rails,
breath standing in the air.

Jiraiya was already there, coat off in the cold, two wooden swords
speared upright in the hard ground like a proposition, and next to
them, sitting on a folded cloth with the patience of a small
mountain, a toad the colour of old iron, watching him arrive with
its arms folded.

"Morning, kid," said Jiraiya. "Class is us three. Fukasaku-sama's
got the first answer, I've got the field, and you've got about
eleven questions and one you've written down and capped, because
you always have." He picked up the nearer sword and threw it,
flat and easy, and Sazare caught it out of the air with the hand
that had held a blade for two hundred hours. "Uncap it after.
First we find out about the hesitation."

The frost held every sound. Somewhere behind him the tower's
second-floor window caught the first of the sun.

*Six sharp, the north fields. The fifth deep end.*

*In.*
