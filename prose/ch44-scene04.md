# Chapter 44, Scene 4 — Six in the Room

*Act 4, part D. Konoha General, the second-floor treatment room. Sazare
Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,536 words.*

---

He did not book the committee room.

He booked the second-floor treatment room, which had six chairs in it
because it had been a six-bed bay before the invasion and nobody had
ever got round to changing the furniture, and he booked it for the
evening of the eighteenth, after the day shift, and he put the chairs
in a ring rather than at a table because there was no table.

Then he wrote nothing.

That was the difficult part and he had thought about it for four days.
Every instinct he owned said arrive with a document. A protocol, ruled,
collated, tables at the back, eleven copies. He had done that in the
fourth month and lost a room in nine words, and he had spent since
working out that the failure had not been the leading — the leading was
the symptom — and that the actual fault was that he had walked into a
room with a finished object and asked eleven people to admire it.

So he brought a blank sheet and a pencil and nothing else, and it felt
like walking into a fight without a coat.

---

They came in over about ten minutes.

Ōkubo first, early, in the same pressed coat with the frayed cuff, who
sat down and said "Right" and nothing else for twenty minutes. Natsumi
from the gate detail, off shift, still in the jacket. Nemoto, who had
argued with Handa across a room full of six turned tables in the first
month and had a laugh like a dropped tray. Ariake Fumi, fifty-one, from
obstetrics, who Sazare had met four times and had never once seen sit
down. Tsuchida, twenty-three, second year, terrified.

And Sakura, last, who came in at exactly the appointed minute and took
the chair nearest the door and did not look at Ōkubo, who was head of
general surgery and outranked everybody in the room by about thirty
years.

"Right," said Ōkubo. "Where's the paper."

"There isn't one."

"There's always a paper."

"There was a paper. It was a bad paper and Sister Kaburagi took it
apart in ninety seconds without a note in front of her, and she was
correct." Sazare stayed standing, because sitting would have made it a
meeting. "The objection was that I wrote a consent form for the patient
and nobody wrote one for you. I've had four weeks to write it and I
can't, because I've never stood where you stand. So I'm not going to
write it. You are, tonight, in this room, and I'm going to hold the
pencil."

Nobody said anything.

"That's the whole of the opening," said Sazare. "I've stopped."

Ariake Fumi sat down.

---

It took two hours and forty minutes and it was, without any comparison,
the best document he had ever been part of.

They gave him things he could not have got to.

Nemoto, in the first fifteen minutes, with his arms folded: "You've got
us silent for nine months. Nobody's silent for nine months. Nobody in
this building can go nine months without saying *you're doing well* to
somebody who's frightened, and if you make it a rule then the first
time one of us breaks it we'll lie about it on the sheet, and then
your data's rubbish and you'll never know."

"So what's the rule."

"There isn't one. That's the point." Nemoto shrugged. "Give us a
stopping rule *we* hold. Any of us breaks silence any time, for any
reason, and doesn't have to say why, and it goes on the sheet as a
break and not as a failure. Then nobody lies."

Sazare wrote it down and then stopped writing.

"That's better than my design," he said.

"Well. Yes."

"No, I mean it's *better as a study*. If the break rate is on the sheet
then the break rate is data. I've been treating your discomfort as a
cost to be minimised and it's actually the most interesting number in
the whole trial, and I would never have got there, because I'd have
built it to keep you comfortable and lost the variable."

Ōkubo made a noise that in a smaller man would have been a laugh.

---

Ariake Fumi gave him the one that stopped the room, at about the
ninetieth minute, in the voice of a woman who had thought about it since
the second minute and had been deciding whether to say it.

"It isn't the silence," she said.

"Go on."

"The silence is nine months and then it's over and I'll forget it." She
had her hands in her lap and was looking at the middle of the ring.
"What I'll have is that I'll know which arm I was in. And in about four
years I'll be standing over somebody who's gone badly and I'll think:
was that one of mine. Was that the one where I stood there and said
nothing, and did it matter, and I will never be able to find out,
because you can't run it twice on the same woman."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Tsuchida, who had not spoken in two hours, said, "Can you — is there a
way to—"

"No," said Sazare.

Six people looked at him.

"There isn't a way," he said. "I've been sitting here for about ninety
seconds trying to build one and every version I get is a version where
I tell her something that isn't true in order to make this evening
easier, and I'm not doing that. She's right. It's not fixable. If you
go in the silent arm you will carry a specific unanswerable question
about specific people for the rest of your working life, and the only
honest thing I can do about it is put that sentence in the consent form
in those words and make you sign underneath it."

"That," said Ōkubo, "will lose you two of the six."

"Then I'll have four and I'll say so on the paper."

"It'll lose you the board as well. Kaburagi will read that line and—"

"Kaburagi will read that line," said Sazare, "and know that somebody in
this room said it out loud, because I could not have written it. That's
the only thing that gets it past her and it's also true, and I'd like
those to be the same thing for once."

Ariake Fumi looked at him for a long moment.

"Put it in," she said. "In my words. Not tidied."

---

It went past eleven.

Sakura said almost nothing for the first two hours, which he understood
by about the fortieth minute — she was fourteen in a room with the head
of general surgery, and every single thing she said was going to be
heard as Tsunade's apprentice saying it, and she had worked that out
before she came through the door and had decided to sit on it.

She broke at the ninth item, over the matched-injuries criterion, and
she broke correctly, and what came out was four sentences of extremely
precise objection about wound age that stopped Ōkubo mid-word.

There was a pause of about two seconds.

"Say the second half again," said Ōkubo.

She said it again.

"She's right," he said, to the room. "Twenty-two years and I've been
matching on presentation and not on age of injury, and if we do it
Haruno's way the arms are actually comparable and if we do it mine
they're not." He wrote something on his own knee. "I'd like that
minuted as coming from her and not from me. It'll get argued with less
if it comes from me, and I'm not doing that."

Sakura went a colour and said nothing at all.

---

Sazare walked her down as far as the second floor afterwards, because
they were going the same way, and neither of them said anything until
the turn.

"Six out of ten," said Sakura.

"For what?"

"Tonight. Criterion one, you went in without a cargo, which for you is
enormous, three out of three. Criterion two, you left a gap the size of
a door for two and a half hours, three out of three." She shifted her
satchel. "Criterion three, nought. You followed everything anybody gave
you and you never once went back to what you had ready, and that's — I
haven't got a box for that, it's off the top of the sheet, so I gave
you nought because I need a new sheet."

"That's four."

"Criterion four is where you lost it," said Sakura. "You didn't stop
when it was finished. It was finished at half past ten and you kept it
open for another forty minutes because you didn't want it to be over."

Sazare thought about that on the stairs.

"That's accurate," he said.

"I know."

"I've never had that before. A room where the thing gets better because
of who's in it." He stopped at the turn. "I've had rooms where I was
right and rooms where I was told, and I've never had one where I put a
blank sheet down and got back something I couldn't have made."

"That's just a good meeting, Ruri-san."

"Yes," he said. "I'm eighteen and it's the first one."
