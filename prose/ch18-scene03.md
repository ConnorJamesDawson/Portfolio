# Chapter 18, Scene 3 — Not Late

*Act 3, part A. Nami and the north shore. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~2,300 words.*

---

The word came into the town on a Thursday afternoon, up the plank walk,
the way everything came into Nami — sideways, from a boy who had it from
his uncle who had it from a carter — and by the time it reached the net
loft it had been through six mouths and had acquired a giant.

There had been a fight on the coast road north. Konoha ninja, four of
them, bringing the old bridge-builder home. A man had come at them out of the water with a
sword the size of a door. He had been killed. His body had been taken away
by a boy in a white mask who had come down out of a tree, and the Konoha
ninja had let him take it, because that was the arrangement, apparently,
between villages.

Sazare was sitting cross-legged on the loft floor with a lamp bracket in
pieces on a cloth in front of him and a few thousand ryō's worth of somebody
else's crockery stacked against the wall, and he put the bracket down.

He knew four things about that story inside about six seconds, and three
of them nobody else in the Land of Waves knew at all.

The first was that a sword the size of a door was not a figure of speech.

The second was that a Kirigakure retrieval man does not carry a body away
to be dealt with elsewhere. He deals with it there. On the ground, at the
site, immediately, because the whole point of the office is that nothing
goes anywhere, and Sazare knew that the way he knew the price of bread,
which is to say from a fat sweat-swollen book in an inn doorway in the
rain and from four minutes lying in six inches of run-off with a
professional standing eight feet away.

The third was that a masked boy coming down out of a tree at exactly the
right moment, in a foreign country, to remove exactly one specific body
from four Konoha shinobi and take it away intact, was not a hunter-nin.
He was the same thing a mender's card in a window was.

And the fourth was that a man who has to be carried is a man who is going
to be lying somewhere for a fortnight.

---

He was two days working out what he actually wanted, and he never got a
better answer than the one he started with, which was: *I don't know. I
want to look at him.*

He was honest about it, in the loft, at night, with the tide knocking
under the floor. It was not revenge; there was nothing to revenge. It was
not gratitude either, or not much — a man had told a seven-year-old to run
in a street eleven years ago and had almost certainly forgotten it before
he reached the corner.

It was that in nine years he had met four adults who had lived the life he
was now living, and every one of them had been a preview.

Tōan, who did not lie and had lost everything for it and lived alone up a
valley dosing quarrymen.

Sakai, who kept a door and drank his wages and cut a paper strip out of
the air once a year to prove to nobody at all that one room in the ruin
was intact.

Umeya, sixty, spectacles, thirty-one years of other people's worst
afternoons, saying *I've had four of your sort and I'd rather have had
none.*

Chōsei, fat and cheerful, with two sons in the trade.

And out there on some island, with a hole in him, was the one who had gone
furthest down the road — a man who had done every single thing Sazare was
now doing, for twenty years, at the top of the profession, and had a page
of his own in the book, and had never stopped.

He wanted to know what that looked like at the end. That was all it was.
He wanted to see the last milestone on his own road before he had walked
to it.

---

He did not look for a man. He looked for the shopping.

That was the whole method and it took him six days and it worked, and it
worked because the Land of Waves was the easiest country on earth to do it
in: there was no money, therefore there was almost no trade, therefore
every single purchase in that town was an event that somebody remembered.

A man lying up with a chest wound for a fortnight needs a household. The
household needs water, fuel, food, and cloth, and the cloth is the tell,
because you cannot dress a wound like that twice a day for two weeks on
what is already in a house.

He did not ask a single direct question. He mended things and let people
talk at him, which they did, constantly, because he was sixteen and quiet
and had a leg and had fixed the school's window catch for nothing.

In six days he was given, without asking:

That the herbalist at the top of the hill — a bent old man with a shop the
size of a cupboard and a smell in it like a hedge on fire — had sold, in
one go, the whole of his knitbone, most of his safflower, and every scrap
of dried yarrow he had, to a customer who had known exactly what to ask
for and had not haggled.

That the same customer had been back twice.

That the draper on the plank walk, who had not sold a bolt of anything
since the spring, had sold four yards of unbleached cotton and then, six
days later, six more.

That the boy at the rice store had been given a whole silver piece, an
actual silver piece, in Nami, in the eleventh month, and had had to go to
three houses to get it changed.

And that the customer — this was the part that came up over and over,
because it was the only interesting thing that had happened in Nami all
season, and because everybody who mentioned it did so with the same
faintly baffled fondness — was very young, and very polite, and pretty
enough that two separate people described them to him as a girl and one as
a boy, and had a soft accent that nobody could place, and always said
thank you twice.

Sazare, sitting on the loft floor at night with a candle and a scrap of
board, wrote none of it down and laid all of it out, and did the sum.

Knitbone, safflower and yarrow is a bruise-and-bleeding decoction. Tōan
had made it in a copper pan up a valley in the Land of Hot Water and had
made Sazare grind it a hundred times and had said, with the pan going,
*this is for the ones where the bleeding's inside and you can't get at it,
and it does about a fifth of what people think it does, and the other four
fifths is time and somebody sitting up.*

Four yards, then six. The six was the second week, when the dressings
start getting changed more often because the wound has started to work
rather than because it is fresh.

Three shops, all of them in the upper town, none on the water side. A
person who has come in by boat lands at the quay and shops at the quay. A
person shopping the *top* of the hill has come over the ridge, on foot,
from the north shore.

And the load. Six yards of cotton, a bag of rice, herbs, oil: call it a
big load for a slight person, and a slight person carries a big load about
four miles before they stop enjoying it, and comes into town in the
morning and out again before dark.

He drew the circle in his head. Four miles, north shore, over the ridge,
with water and shelter and no neighbours.

There were eleven places in that arc it could be. He walked six of them in
two days, in the rain, with his knee going, and found four empty
fishermen's huts and a burnt-out farm.

The seventh was the one.

---

It stood on a spit at the end of a shingle beach on the north shore, and
he saw it from the ridge at about the fourth hour of the afternoon through
a gap in the pines with the rain just stopping and the sun coming out
underneath the cloud the way it does at the end of a wet day, low and
yellow and horizontal, so that the whole spit lit up gold against a sky
still the colour of a bruise behind it.

It was a house-and-a-half: a squat single-storey building with a
steep-pitched thatch gone black and mossy, and a lean-to on the seaward
end, and a wooden veranda along the front that had lost about a third of
its boards. Behind it the pines came down close. In front of it, forty
yards of grey shingle and then the water, flat calm, with the low sun
laying a road of light across it and the islands standing out in the
distance like the backs of animals.

There was a stack of firewood against the gable, and it was a *big* stack,
and it was new, and it had been split by somebody with a good eye and
stacked in courses with the bark side up to shed rain.

There was smoke coming out of the roof.

And on the shingle, forty feet down from the veranda, there was a boy
kneeling at the tideline, doing something with his hands in a wide flat
basket.

Sazare lay down in the wet bracken on the ridge with his chin on his
forearms and watched him for two and a half hours until the light went.

The boy was slight and not tall and wore a plain dark travelling kimono,
much washed, with the sleeves tied back, and his hair was long and black
and tied at the nape, and he moved — this was the thing Sazare noticed and
could not stop noticing — he moved *economically*. Every time he went from
the tideline to the house he came back with something. He never made an
empty journey. He carried water in two buckets rather than one, and set
them down together, and did not stagger.

Once he stopped, halfway up the shingle, and stood for a moment looking
out at the islands with a bucket in each hand.

Once he crouched and picked something up off the beach and turned it over
and put it in his sleeve, which was the single most familiar thing Sazare
had ever watched a stranger do.

At dusk he went in and the door shut and a lamp went on behind the paper
of the window, and against it, for about four seconds, an enormous shadow
sat up.

---

Sazare walked back over the ridge in the dark with the rain coming on
again and his knee clicking on every downhill step, and did not go near
the place for another day, because he did not know how to.

There was no version of *walk up to that door* that ended in a
conversation. He was a stranger in a country with one road. If he came up
that beach in daylight he was a witness; if he came at night he was a
threat; and both of those, in that house, with that man in it, ended the
same way, because a man lying up with a chest wound cannot afford to work
out which sort of stranger you are.

He solved it the next morning, sitting at the window over the mud with
his tea going cold and the mist burning off the shallows in long white
bars, and the solution was so obvious and so entirely his that he laughed
out loud at it, alone, in an empty room, for the first time in about nine
months.

The veranda had lost a third of its boards.

He went up the hill to O-Kiyo's brother-in-law who had a saw pit and no
work, and bought — with actual money, four thousand ryō, more than he had
earned in a month in that town — six planks of larch, eight feet, sawn and
squared, and borrowed a two-wheeled handcart with a broken axle-cap that
he mended in nine minutes on the spot.

And on a bright cold morning with the sea flat and the sun coming up over
the ridge behind him, Sazare Ruri walked four miles round the north shore
pulling a cart full of decking, down a shingle beach, in plain view, at
walking pace, from four hundred yards out, whistling; and stopped at the
bottom of the steps of a house with smoke coming out of the roof; and put
the cart handles down.
