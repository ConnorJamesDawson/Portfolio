# Chapter 44, Scene 1 — Nine Visits

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,412 words.*

---

He found it on the second of the month, doing the reconcile.

The reconcile was the last hour of the last working day before Kanzaki
closed a month, and it existed because Kanzaki did not trust anybody's
arithmetic including her own, and it consisted of the two of them
sitting at opposite ends of the awning bench reading their books aloud
at each other line by line until the two agreed. Nine hundred entries.
Four hours. He had come to like it more than almost anything else he
did, which he had never said, because saying it would have made her
stop.

The fifth month had come in hot. The tar on the office roof had gone
soft at the seams by eleven and the whole yard smelled of it, and the
pigeon behind the wall had a second pigeon now and the two of them were
producing something considerably worse than four notes. Kanzaki had
moved her stool into the shade at the start of the week and had not
mentioned doing it.

"Twelfth," she said. "Aggregate, four kilos, second issue."

"Four kilos, second issue."

"Thirteenth. Nothing."

"Nothing."

"Fourteenth. Certification closes. Kurihara signs off the batch,
nineteen stones, all four rows and the short fifth, and I book her
final hours and pay her out of the seal office allocation."

Sazare found it, ticked it, and said, "Fourteenth. Certification
closes."

And then, because the entry after it was in his own hand and he read
his own hand faster than he read hers, he was four lines further down
before the thing arrived.

---

He went back.

*17th. Kurihara S., yard, 14:00–16:20. No work order.*

*19th. Kurihara S., yard, 15:10–17:00. No work order.*

*23rd. Kurihara S., yard, afternoon. No work order.*

He turned the page.

*26th. 27th. 30th. 2nd. 4th. 7th.*

Nine.

He counted them twice, because he did not believe the first count, and
then a third time going backwards from the seventh of the fifth month
to the seventeenth of the fourth, and got nine each time, which was the
correct behaviour and did not help.

The certification had closed on the fourteenth. Every stone signed,
every hour booked, every yen paid out of an allocation that did not
have another yen in it. There was no work order after the fourteenth
because there was no work after the fourteenth. He had checked the
allocation himself in the third week because he had assumed there had
been an error.

There had been no error. She had not been paid for any of it.

"Sazare."

"Sorry."

"You've gone quiet, which you do about four times a year, and the last
time you did it in this yard you'd found the seventh-application
fault." Kanzaki did not look up from her book. "Is my ledger wrong?"

"No."

"Is yours?"

He looked at the nine entries.

"No," he said. "Mine's correct. It's just got something in it I can't
account for."

---

He did the work on it that evening, properly, because a number with no
cause was the exact species of object he had been dealing with since he
was seven and there was a procedure for it.

He listed the possible causes and tested each one.

*She has outstanding work in the yard.* No. Batch closed, signed,
countersigned, paid. Checked against three books.

*She is using the yard's equipment because she has none of her own.*
No. She has a bench at the sealing office with a better lens than
Kanzaki's, which he knew because she had complained about Kanzaki's
lens on the first day and then gone on using it.

*She is working for Kanzaki on something unrelated.* No. Kanzaki would
have booked it. Kanzaki had booked a dead man's funeral clerk to the
nearest quarter-hour.

*She has business in the lane and the yard is a shortcut.* No. The yard
is not a shortcut to anywhere. It has one gate.

*I have asked her to come.* He sat with that one for some time and went
through every exchange he could reconstruct, which was most of them,
because he wrote up anything technical the same evening. He had asked
her four questions about retaining figures on the nineteenth, accepted
three corrections on the twenty-third, and given her the reordering
notebook back — she had returned it — on the twenty-sixth.

He had not once said *come back*.

*She has asked to come.* No. She had never announced a visit. She
simply arrived, at some point in the afternoon, and worked at the far
bench on her own things, and corrected him when he was wrong, which was
on average twice a session and had gone down to about one and a half.

He stopped there, because the next entries on the list were of a kind
he had no procedure for, and looked at the page for a while with the
lamp low and the fifth month sitting warm and still against the window.

Then he wrote, in the notebook, under nothing:

*Nine visits in twenty days, none booked, none paid, none requested by
either party, no work order, no equipment need, no route of
convenience.*

*The certification closed on the fourteenth.*

*I have three hundred and eleven entries on what a medium does and I
have one entry type in this book with no unit at the head of it, and
this is the eighth item to go under it, and it is the first one that is
about a living person doing something on purpose.*

He put the pencil down.

Then he picked it up and added the line that was actually true, which
took him rather longer than the other four:

*I noticed on the second of the fifth month, doing a reconcile, in
somebody else's handwriting.*

*I have read eleven people off their boots. I read a fishmonger's
thirty years off the backs of his fingers in four seconds and wrote it
down and was pleased about it.*

*She has been standing at the far bench of a yard I work in, nine
times, for eighteen days, and I found out about it from a ledger.*

---

Kanzaki said one thing about it, on the ninth, and picked her moment
the way she picked everything.

He was at the awning bench with the log open at the fifth month and
Kurihara was at the far bench under the sky with her back to both of
them and a taper going in full sun for reasons she had explained twice
and he had not entirely followed, and Kanzaki came past with the day
book and put it down and did not sit.

"You've started leaving that end clear," she said.

"What?"

"The far bench. Third one, under the sky." She nodded at it without
looking at it. "For two years you have used that bench for stock, and
about four weeks ago you stopped, and you have been carrying aggregate
past it to the one by the wall, which is nine feet further and in the
shade, and shade is the wrong place to keep aggregate."

Sazare looked at the far bench.

He had no memory of deciding anything about the far bench.

"When did I start doing that."

"You're asking me the date."

"Yes."

"I don't know the date, I'm not your ledger," said Kanzaki. "I noticed
in the fourth week and I've said nothing for a fortnight, and I'm
saying it now because you've been doing arithmetic at that log for
eleven days with a face on you and I'd rather you had the whole set of
numbers than most of them."

She picked the day book back up.

"That's the entire remark," she said. "I'm not going to make it into
anything and neither should you, and if you ask me a follow-up question
I shall be extremely busy."

She went inside.

Sazare sat at the awning bench for a moment with a log in front of him
in which every entry for two years was in his own hand, and one fact
about his own conduct that was not in it anywhere, and which had
apparently been visible from across a yard for four weeks to a woman of
sixty-two who was not even looking.

At the far bench, Kurihara said, without turning round, "Your third
stroke's closing again."

"On which."

"The one in your left hand. You've been holding it for six minutes."

He looked down at it.

It was closing.
