# Chapter 21, Scene 2 — What Nobody Handed Him

*Act 3, part C. The headland. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare.
~2,500 words.*

---

The graves were on the headland north of the bridge, on the seaward
shoulder of it where the grass gives out and the rock starts, about ninety
feet above the water and open to everything.

He went up on the fifth day, in the afternoon, alone, and nobody in the
Land of Waves knew he was going and nobody saw him go.

It was a hard walk and the wind made it harder. The path went up through
gorse and then through nothing, and by the top there was no shelter at all
and the north-easter came across that headland with the whole channel
behind it, flattening the grass in long running combs and making his eyes
stream. The sea below was the colour of a slate roof with the white going
across it in ranks, and out past the point the swell was coming round the
headland and hitting the rock and going up forty feet in slow white
columns that fell back and did it again.

The air smelled of salt and crushed grass and, faintly, of the gorse,
which flowers in every month there is.

Two mounds, side by side, cut into the turf and mounded back over, about
four feet apart. Somebody had squared the edges with a spade and somebody
had put stones round them, unevenly, in a hurry, and the turf on top had
gone yellow at the edges the way cut turf does in five days.

And standing at the head of them, driven into the ground about a foot and
a half, upright and enormous and entirely unattended, was the sword.

---

He stopped about twenty feet short and stood in the wind and looked at it
for a long time before he went any closer.

He had thought about this. He had thought about it, honestly, on and off,
since the morning of the snow, in a way he had not permitted himself to
call thinking about it: a set of small practical questions, entertained
one at a time, each of them innocent on its own — *how far is it to the
top; is there a path; would a handcart go up; what does that thing
weigh* — which had sat in the tray for four days without ever once being
laid beside each other, because the moment they were laid beside each
other they made a plan.

He walked up to it and laid them beside each other.

Then he went and looked at the mounds first, because he was not going to
be a person who came up a hill and went to the sword.

There was nothing to see. Two rectangles of turf. The stones. A dead
flower-head somebody had put on the smaller one, gone brown, wedged under
a stone so the wind would not take it; and it had not taken it, though it
had had five days to try, which meant whoever put it there had known what
the wind on that headland was like and had thought about it for a second
longer than a person does.

Haku's mask was on the larger mound, propped against a stone.

It had been white. It was cracked from the left eye-slit down through the
cheek and out at the jaw, one clean fracture, and a triangular piece about
the size of a thumbnail had gone out of it altogether and was nowhere on
that hillside. The wave-mark on the forehead was still perfect. The
cord-holes were still whole, and the cord was still through them, knotted
once each side by somebody who tied a good knot.

The wind had shifted it since it was put down. It had slid on the turf and
was lying half-turned, looking sideways along the ground toward the
gorse.

Sazare crouched down and picked it up.

It weighed nothing. That was the first thing and it was absurd and it went
through him like a nail: the object he had spent four years being
frightened of, the thing that had come out of a drain at him at Tomari and
had stood over eleven people's worth of nightmares, weighed about as much
as a wooden bowl and was made of fired clay two millimetres thick with a
glaze on it.

He could mend it. He knew that in the time it took to turn it over. He had
the materials in his roll and he could do a clay repair in an afternoon
that would be invisible at four feet, and the missing piece could be made
up with a fill and toned, and it was a straightforward job that he had
done on nine cheaper objects in that town in six weeks.

He set it back against the stone, square, facing out to sea, and did not
mend it.

He was not able to give a proper account of that one either, and he tried,
on the way down, and got as far as: *he wore it to be nobody, and I saw
his face for two seconds on a beach when the smile went off it, and I am
not going to put the other one back on him.*

---

Then he went to the sword.

Up close, in daylight, out of that dark room, it was worse and better.

It stood taller than he did by a head. The blade was a single slab of
steel about eight inches across at the widest and the thickness of a
thumb, driven point-down into the turf and rock so that it went in a foot
and a half and stood dead vertical, and it did not move in that wind, in
any degree, at all.

There was salt on it already. Five days on a headland in a north-easter
and there was a bloom of it right up the seaward face, dulling the grey to
a kind of frosted matt, and in three places along the back edge where the
steel had been ground thin there were the first orange freckles.

He put his hand flat on it.

It was cold — colder than the air, the way big masses of metal are — and
under the salt it was extraordinary. The grind was not flat. He had
thought it was flat in the dark; it was not; there was a very slight hollow
running the length of both faces, three or four thousandths, put there by
somebody who understood that you cannot make a blade that size light and
so you make it *fast* instead by giving it somewhere for the air and the
meat to go. The half-moon bite at the top had a finished edge on it that
went round the curve without a single flat, which is a thing you cannot do
with a wheel and must do with a file and a great deal of patience. The
hole through the base was drilled, not punched, and reamed after.

Nobody knew who had made it. Nobody had known for two hundred years. And
whoever it was had taken a piece of steel the size of a door plank and had
sat down with a file and gone round a curve.

Sazare stood on that headland with his hand on it and wanted it so badly
that his teeth ached.

---

He got it out of the ground.

That was the part he never told anybody about and it took him about
eleven minutes.

He did it the way he did everything: not by lifting, because he weighed
eight and a half stone and could no more have lifted that thing straight
up than he could have lifted a cart. He got a flat stone and a piece of
driftwood off the top of the beach and made a fulcrum against the base of
the blade and levered it, an inch at a time, side to side, working the
socket in the turf the way you work a fence post, until the whole of it
came loose and leaned; and then he got his shoulder under the guardless
base of it and walked it over onto the grass and let it down flat.

It went down with a sound he felt through his boots.

Then he sat on the turf beside it in the wind, breathing hard, with his
knee shrieking and both hands shaking, and looked at four and a half feet
of the finest object he had ever touched lying in the grass on a headland
with nobody within a mile.

And there it was, laid out flat, the whole of the plan, in the open at
last where he could look at it properly:

He could not carry it. He could get it to the bottom of the hill on a
sledge made of the driftwood and the quilt in about four hours. He could
build a cart for it in a day and a half out of the second boatyard's
scrap and he had mended enough for that yard that they would not ask him
what it was for. Sada would take him and a long crate off that island for
eleven thousand ryō and would not look inside it, because she had not
looked inside anything in thirty years.

He could be off the thin coast with it inside a week.

*They get handed down. Holder to holder. You get it off the last man.*

And the last man was four feet away under a rectangle of yellowing turf,
and there was nobody standing on that headland to hand it to anybody, and
there never would be, and every single person who had any claim on the
thing at all was dead in the Land of Water or dead in the ground under his
boots.

He sat with his hand on it for a long time.

---

The argument he made to himself was short, because he had already made all
of it and had been making it for eleven days in pieces without letting the
pieces touch.

*Nobody handed it to me.*

That was the whole of it. He turned it over four ways on that headland and
it did not develop and it did not need to.

His clan had lived four hundred years on a thing they had not been handed
and had not asked for and had certainly not earned: the protection of a
state, which they had accepted every single day of their protected lives,
and which had been paid for out of a bill that was presented to somebody
else — the hunters they armed, the people those hunters caught, four
hundred years of a family being *comfortable* on the strength of an
arrangement nobody in it had ever once looked straight at. And when the
bill finally came round to them they had gone to a platform in the fog and
been astonished.

Kiri had taken his four names and his file and his mother's trade and his
sister's road and the whole of what he was and had put a number on it in a
book, and had never asked him about any part of it, and had priced him
alive and undamaged with particular care taken of the hands.

And his sister — and this was the one that had cost him four years and a
morning on a beach in the fog — his sister had taken four years of him,
carefully, cleverly, with real love, and had never once asked; and he had
found out on a shingle bank with the sea going out through the stones that
the not-asking was the whole of the wound and always had been, and that he
would have given her every single hour of it gladly if she had ever put
her hand out and said *may I*.

Sazare Ruri sat on a headland at sixteen with his hand on a blade that
nobody in the world was watching.

*Nobody handed it to me.*

*And I have spent my entire life finding out what the difference is
between that and taking it, and it turns out to be the only thing I
actually know.*

He got up.

---

He put it back in exactly the hole it had come out of, which took him
another quarter of an hour and was substantially harder than getting it
out, and involved the driftwood and a great deal of unedifying language;
and when it was in he packed the socket with turf and small stones and
trod it in all round, twice, and then stood back and sighted it against
the horizon from two directions and knocked it a half-inch with the flat
of his hand to bring it true.

It stood dead vertical.

Then he did the only thing he had.

He got the small flask of oil out of the medical roll — the one Tōan had
given him for instruments, four years old, a third full — and a clean
scrap of the shirt he had run out of on the bridge, and he oiled that
blade from the ground to the top on both faces, and along the back, and
round the inside of the half-moon, and got into the drilled hole with a
finger and a rag corner and did that too.

It took him nearly an hour. He had to do the top by standing on a stone.

The salt bloom came off. The freckles along the back edge would not, and
were going to spread, and by the spring there would be a red line the
whole length of it and in five years it would be pitted right through the
thin part and in twenty it would be a bar of rust standing on a headland
with the turf grown up round the base, and he knew every part of that
while he was doing it and did it anyway, thoroughly, working the rag along
the grind in the direction of the grain, on a cliff, in a north-easter,
for a dead man he had met twice.

It would make no difference whatever.

It was the entire content of what he had to give anybody, and he had given
it to a lantern and a lid and a veranda and a frame with dust in the
split, and he stood on that headland at the end of it with his hands
black and his knee finished and the wind taking the water out of his eyes,
and did not say one word aloud, because there was nobody to say it to and
he had never in his life been able to talk to the dead.

Then he wiped his hands, and put the flask away, and went down off the
headland and did not go back up it again.
