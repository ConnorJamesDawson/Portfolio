# Chapter 21, Scene 1 — The Pan

*Act 3, part C. The north shore. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare.
~2,050 words.*

---

He went round the shore on the second morning, because he had put it off
on the first and had spent the whole of that day being no use to anybody
and had known exactly why.

The weather had turned properly by then. The snow had come to nothing —
four minutes of it and a wet road — but it had brought the wind round
behind it, and the second morning came up hard and blue and cold with a
north-easter coming down the channel and the sea running white out past
the point, and the pines going over on the ridge with a noise like
somebody tearing cloth a long way off.

The tide was making. He had to go the high way over the shoulder rather
than round the beach, which added a mile and cost his knee about half an
hour, and he was glad of both.

The house came into sight from the top of the ridge at about the ninth
hour: the black thatch, the lean-to, the veranda with the six new boards
in it, and the woodpile against the gable with the bark side up.

There was no smoke.

He stood on the ridge and looked at that for a while, and it was the
smoke, in the end, and not the graves and not the bridge; it was a roof
with nothing coming out of it, at nine in the morning, in a north-easter,
in the eleventh month.

---

The door was not locked. There was no lock.

Inside, the room had gone cold all the way through in a day and a half —
that particular dead chill a house gets when the fire has been out longer
than the walls can carry, which is different from the cold outside and
worse — and the light came through the two paper windows flat and grey and
lay in two long bars across an earth floor that had been swept.

That was the thing that undid him and he had not been braced for it.

The floor had been swept. The hearth was banked, properly, with the ash
drawn up over the last of the embers in a low mound the way you do it when
you intend to be back in a few hours and want to save yourself the
trouble of a strike. The quilt was folded in three at the foot of the mat.
The mat was rolled. There were two cups upside down on the ledge beside
the pan, both dry, and a cloth over them.

A man and a boy had got up in the dark on the Friday morning, and had made
tea, and had washed the cups, and had swept the floor and banked the fire
and rolled the beds and put a cloth over the crockery against the dust;
and one of them had known for four days that he was not coming back and
had done all of it anyway, exactly, in order, because a well-kept tool
does not leave a mess.

Sazare stood in the doorway with his hand on the frame for some
considerable time.

---

He did the errand the way it had been given to him, which was as a list.

**The pan.** It was iron, about ten inches, with a handle that had been
riveted twice and a base worn silver-bright in a ring where three years of
fires had had at it, and the inside of it was — he turned it to the light
and had to stop again — the inside of it was *perfect*. Not clean. Any
fool can clean a pan. It was the deep even black-brown of iron that has
been scoured with salt and sand and dried on the heat and wiped with fat,
every single time, for three years, by somebody who had never once let it
go a day, and there was not a speck of rust anywhere on it, on a coast
where everything rusts.

And on it, the lid, with a bronze mushroom handle on a new stud made out
of the shank of a bent nail, sitting down flush on a copper shoulder
washer.

He put the lid on the pan. It shut.

**The food.** Rice, about six days of it, in a lidded jar. A string of
dried fish. A crock of the pickled thing. Salt. A twist of tea. Oil in a
flask with a stopper he had watched Haku wedge with a scrap of cloth
because the stopper was proud.

**The jar of yarrow**, and beside it, which had not been on the list,
three more twists of the same size in the same paper: knitbone, safflower,
and the grey-leaved thing that had no name he knew.

**A quilt.** **Two knives.** **A lamp with oil in it.**

He laid them all out on the floor by the door in a row, and counted them
against the list, and found that he could account for every item Haku had
named and that there were four things in that house Haku had not named.

He looked at those four for a long time.

A whetstone, worn hollow.

A wooden comb with three teeth gone.

A packet of needles in a paper fold — sewing needles, ordinary, the sort
anybody has.

And on the shelf above the ledge, standing on end, wedged upright between
the wall and a stone, a single dried flower stem about nine inches long
with a papery grey-white head on it that had been picked when it was
fresh and had been in that house long enough to lose every particle of
colour it ever had.

There was nothing else on that shelf. There had never been anything else
on that shelf; the dust said so.

Sazare left all four of them exactly where they were.

He was not able, then or afterwards, to give a proper account of why. The
nearest he ever came to it was that a man who takes a pan is doing an
errand, and a man who takes a comb is going through somebody's things.

---

He took the four foot of larch out from under the veranda, because he had
been told to and because Haku had been right about the hornbeam, which had
opened a two-inch shake in the end grain overnight the way seasoned
hornbeam sometimes does out of pure spite.

Then he went round the outside of the house once, slowly, the way he went
round everything.

The woodpile was full. That was the last thing and it was the worst.
There was, against that gable, about five weeks of split wood, stacked in
courses with the bark side up to shed the rain, cut short enough for that
hearth and no shorter — five weeks of somebody going out with an axe on
the days a chest was knitting and there was nothing else useful to do —
and every stick of it was going to sit there through the winter and
through the spring and be rained on and go grey and be burned, eventually,
by somebody who had walked out from the town and found an empty house.

He stood in the north-easter with his hand on the top course.

Then he went back in and got the fire lit, out of the banked ash, which
took him two minutes because it had been banked by somebody who knew how;
and he put the pan on it with water in it and made tea with the twist of
tea, in that house, alone, with the wind coming off the channel and the
door shut; and he drank it sitting on the floor with his back against the
post where a very large man had sat with a cup in both hands and told him
there were only two doors.

It was a completely useless thing to do and he did it anyway and did not
try to justify it to himself even once.

Then he washed the cup and put it upside down on the ledge with the other
one, and put the cloth over both, and banked the fire again, and went out
and shut the door.

---

He carried it all back over the ridge in two trips because his knee would
not do it in one, and on the second trip the wind had got up further and
he had to go with his head down and the quilt in a bundle on his shoulder
acting like a sail the whole way.

He left the pan on O-Kiyo's step at the fourth hour when she was up at the
water queue.

He put it dead centre on the step where she would not be able to walk past
it, with the lid on and a fold of the paper under the handle to keep it
off the wet stone, and the jar of rice and the fish and the crock beside
it, and he did not leave a note, and he did not stay, and he did not say a
single word to her about any of it that day or ever.

She had that iron thing with the crack in it. She had been boiling
everything twice for two years because of it, which adds about forty
minutes to every meal a person eats and about nine hundred hours to a
life, and she had never once mentioned it to him, because it was not
broken, it just took twice as long, and there is no word for that and no
queue you can join.

He watched from the top of the plank walk, because he could not not, and
saw her come back with the water and stop.

She stood in front of that step for a while with the yoke still on her
shoulders.

Then she put the buckets down and picked the pan up in both hands and
turned it over, and took the lid off and put it back on and heard it shut,
and looked up and down the walk twice, at nobody; and took it inside.

---

The rest of it he gave away over the following two days without ceremony
and without saying where any of it came from.

The quilt went to the empty inn, where there were still four families
sleeping on boards. The lamp went to the widow at the third house on the
ridge road who had been going to bed at dark since the spring. The knives
went to the second boatyard, where the foreman took one look at the edges
and said *these are a bloody sight better than mine* and asked no
questions at all.

The medical twists he kept. He was clear with himself about that one: it
was not sentiment, it was that there were nine people in that town with
hands and ribs and a thigh who were going to need dressing for a fortnight
and he was the only person in the Land of Waves who knew what the
grey-leaved thing was for.

That was what he told himself.

He also, on the fourth night, sat on the mat in the loft with the twist of
grey leaves open on the cloth in front of him and looked at it for about
forty minutes without touching it, in the dark, with the tide going out
under the boards.

It had been picked by somebody kneeling on a bank above a town at first
light with a small knife, on a morning when he had four days to live and
knew it, and had gone on gathering herbs for a man's chest anyway, and had
stopped at the top of the good bank to wake a sleeping boy and tell him he
would catch cold.

Sazare folded the paper back up along its own creases, exactly, the way it
had been folded, and put it in the medical roll with the others.

Then he lay down and did not sleep, and at about the third hour he got up
and lit the lamp and got out the four foot of larch and started making a
plane body he did not need, because his hands wanted something and there
was nothing else in the room for them to do.
