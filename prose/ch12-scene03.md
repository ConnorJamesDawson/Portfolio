# Chapter 12, Scene 3 — The Note

*Act 2, part C. Sazare Ruri, age fifteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

The lattice question he took apart that spring, in the weeks after the first
piece, at night, on the scrap tray, in the manner of the only laboratory he had ever been trained in:
one change at a time, and write nothing down.

The question was old. It was, in fact, the second-oldest question he owned —
*what is the copper for* — and it had been answered his whole childhood the
way the whole trade answered everything, which was *because that's how it's
done*. But he had now grown twice in his life with no lattice at all: once
out of his own blood, in a fifth of a second, in a square; and once over
two careful nights with a bent stove wire that had been a lattice the way
a whittled stick is a spoon — a courtesy to the form, doing nothing.

So: no wire at all.

The first piece grown bare came up out of the sand like a bad secret —
alive, unmistakably alive, faster to take than any framed piece he had
ever fed, and *shapeless*: a blunt glassy fist of crystal, lobed and
witless, that had gone wherever the feed was richest, the way water goes.
He sat looking at it for a long time, turning it under the lamp.

It was not a failure. That was the thing. It was rude, and it was ugly,
and his uncle would have binned it on sight — but it was clear all
through, no bubble, no cloud, and the surface had the wet-glass truth of
his mother's best work. The *material* was right. Only the *form* had
gone wild.

Which meant the lattice had been doing two jobs all along, and nobody in
four hundred years had thought to give them separate names, because
nobody had ever had to do one without the other.

The first job was the shape — a boundary, a thing for the growth to
answer to, the way a trellis answers a vine.

And the second job was the *smoothing*. A frame's copper web spread the
feed, evened it, forgave the small stumbles of a tired hand at the third
hour — and this, he understood with a slow rising of the hairs on his
arms, was why the trade could exist at all; why his uncle, three frames
and lower grades, could sell his work; why *most Ruri* had ever been able
to grow anything worth a docket. The lattice was a crutch for the feed.
The clan had been leaning on copper for four centuries because the clan,
mostly, had needed to.

He did not need to.

He tested it the only honest way, bare tray, four nights running, feeding
with the full still-water attention that fourteen years of bowls and
frames and lamps had ground into him — and the feed held even without the
copper's forgiveness, grain by grain, level as a glass — and what came up
came up clear every time, and shapeless every time, and there it was, laid
out on the bench in a row of five ugly honest lumps: the inheritance,
divided. His mother's control had been doing the copper's second job all
her life without her ever calling it that. The lattice's first job — the
shape — was the only part he actually lacked.

And shape, it turned out, could be *suggested*.

A waxed thread strung an inch over the sand, and the growth followed it —
followed it the way frost follows a wire, a ridge of crystal walking the
thread's line from one end to the other, thin as a reed and true as the
thread was true. A nail stood upright in the bed grew a socket around
itself, released with a twist, a perfect blind hole. A chopstick laid flat
got a half-round channel. The growth answered *any* boundary it was
offered — offered, not imposed; the thread did not force the crystal any
more than a path forces a walker — and within two months he
could grow, slowly, on a good night, with everything in him bent to it, a
flat ribbon two fingers wide and a hand long, unlatticed, shaped by
nothing but a stretched string and intent.

It was the most interesting thing that had ever happened to him.

He recognised the feeling from the inside of the feeling, which was new —
being *in* the finding-out, hip-deep, hours going by unnoticed, the lamp
burned dry twice — and he thought, in the small hours, levelling the sand
for the next attempt: this. This was what the whole head had been *for*,
all along. Not the errands and not the hiding and not the seven kinds of
survival arithmetic.

Somebody should have given him this when he was seven, and the reason
nobody had was that nobody had known it was there to give, because in the
north room the question *why* had been a child's noise you waited out,
and here — in a foreign kitchen at three in the morning, on a seed-flat
of sieved sand, alone — it was the tool that opened the trade.

---

The note he found by accident, and it frightened him properly, and he
went at it anyway, because that was the order those two things always
came in with him.

He had taken to flicking the test pieces with a fingernail as he worked —
tradesman's habit, the way his mother had flicked the earring at the
lamp, listening for the dull spot that marked a hollow. Good pieces rang.
Each rang its own pitch, high and small, a fingernail's worth of the
frames' old hum — and one evening, two test ribbons lying a foot apart on
the bench, he flicked the first and heard, underneath its dying ring, the
second one *answer*.

Untouched. Singing. Faint as a held breath, the far ribbon had picked up
its sister's note out of the air and given it back.

He sat very still, the way he sat when it mattered.

Then he spent three weeks on it, methodically, with the shutters fast.

Twin pieces, grown the same night at the same pace, rang near enough
alike that one would always answer the other. Pieces grown apart answered
weakly, or not at all. And a piece's note, sounded *at* it, hard, close —
he built a rig for this, a struck bar and a bamboo horn, because he was
not going to put his mouth near the question until he understood it —
made the piece *tremble*; and a good slow piece trembled and rang and
took no harm; and a hurried piece, a piece with a join, a piece grown in
a temper —

The third such piece burst on the bench like a dropped cup, at four feet,
from nothing but its own note held against it for the space of three
heartbeats.

He cleaned up every splinter and sat on the porch in the cold for a long
time.

The frames hummed. All nine of them, every night of his childhood, none
of them quite in tune — and he heard his own voice, seven years old,
asking the first question he had ever asked in this story: *could you
make them agree?* And his mother, hands flat on the sand, not looking up:

*If you ever work out how, I'd like to hear it.*

He had it now. He had worked it out at fifteen in another country on a
scrap tray with a bamboo horn, and the answer was: you must never make
them agree. The disagreement was the safety. Nine frames tuned apart
could never take up one another's note, never build each other's tremble,
never sing one another to pieces in the night — four hundred years of
lives spent leaning over live crystal, and somewhere so far back that the
reason had rotted out of the practice, somebody had learned this the hard
way and set the frames apart on purpose, and by his mother's day it was
just the sound the house made instead of silence.

She had known the answer without knowing it was an answer. She'd have
liked to hear it.

He sat with that until the cold drove him in, and it hurt exactly the
way pressing the arm's scar hurt — a live seam under mended skin — and he
let it, because Tose's country had taught him the other thing, the thing
Kiri never taught: that the dead like to be spoken to, and that news is
news, whoever it reaches.

"You must never make them agree," he said, to the steam over the gorge,
to a woman with her sleeves pinned back five hundred miles east under a
lid of fog. "That's why. It's a good reason. It's the best reason I've
ever found for anything."

Then he went in, and levelled the sand, and — being what he was, because
curiosity ran in front of fear in him always and had never once looked
back — began to think, carefully, in the dark, about what it meant that
he could grow, from a thread's suggestion, in perfect silence, a ribbon
of crystal tuned deliberately to *agree with itself*: a thing whose whole
nature was to be still and clear and patient until, from anywhere, at any
distance, somebody sounded its one true note.

He put the thought away unfinished.

Some doors you note the position of. You do not have to use them.
