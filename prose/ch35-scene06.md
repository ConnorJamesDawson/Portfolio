# Chapter 35, Scene 6 — Same Night

*Act 4, part C. Konoha. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare.
~1,553 words.*

---

She was late, as promised, by twenty-five minutes, and came in shaking
the rain off a coat that was not the Hokage's coat, and sat down at the
corner table opposite him, and said:

"Bridge."

"The bridge again?"

"The *same bridge*. Two hours. There is a man on that Council who has
now spoken for a cumulative eleven hours about a bridge that will cost
less than the paper we have generated arguing about it, and today he
brought a *diagram*." She poured. "Don't. Don't say anything reasonable
about it, I've had six people be reasonable at me today."

"I wasn't going to be reasonable."

"What were you going to be?"

"I was going to say that if he's brought a diagram he's lost, because
people who are winning don't bring diagrams, they bring one number and
say it twice."

Tsunade looked at him for a second and then laughed the way she had
laughed the first time, head back, once.

"That is exactly correct and it took you four seconds and I have been
in the room since the second hour."

---

He was two-thirds of the way through telling her about the corridor
before he noticed.

It arrived sideways, the way things did — he was in the middle of a
sentence about Emiko taking nine seconds, and he registered, without
breaking the sentence, that his cup had been sitting untouched about
four inches from his right hand for the whole of the last eleven
minutes.

He stopped.

"What," said Tsunade.

"I haven't drunk any of it."

"I noticed that about a quarter of an hour ago and elected to say
nothing, which I'd like some credit for, because it was difficult."

He looked at the cup.

"The check's running," he said. "It's fully running. I've been sober
since the fourth hour and I've just told you about a corridor."

"Yes."

"That's not—" He stopped, and started again, badly, which he was
getting used to. "Four nights ago I worked out that the only reason I
could say anything was that a chemical had switched off the part of me
that stops it. I wrote it down. I concluded it was a fault I'd found a
way around rather than a solution, and I stand by that, and then I
spent five weeks in a corridor being unable to produce four sentences
sober, which appeared to confirm the whole of it."

"And now?"

"And now the check is running and I've been talking for twenty minutes
and it hasn't stopped anything, and I do not understand the
mechanism."

Tsunade picked up her own cup, and did not drink from it either, which
he noticed and did not comment on.

"You want the medical answer or the other one?"

"Both."

"The medical answer is that the drink didn't switch anything off, it
lowered a threshold, and thresholds move for about nine reasons and
alcohol's only one of them. Fatigue moves it. Habituation moves it.
Doing the thing once and surviving it moves it a very long way." She
turned the cup. "You said eleven unguarded things in this room four
nights ago and the world did not end. That's data, and you're a man who
updates on data, and you have been updating on it for four days without
noticing because you were busy in a corridor."

"That's not a mechanism, that's a description."

"It's a mechanism, you just don't like it, because it means the useful
variable was never the drink." She drank at last. "The other answer is
shorter and worse."

"Go on."

"It's the fourth time you've sat at this table with me and you've
stopped expecting it to go badly."

---

He sat with that for a while.

"I asked for next week," he said. "Under the lamp. Three drinks in."

"You did."

"I've thought about that a great deal. I wrote it down. I checked in
the morning whether I'd have asked sober and decided I would have, and
I've been about seventy per cent confident in that for a fortnight and
it's been bothering me the whole time."

"And?"

"And I don't know how to test it, because I can't un-ask it."

"No," Tsunade agreed. "You can't. Welcome to approximately everything
that matters — there's no control sample and you get one run and you
find out afterwards or not at all." She refilled her own and did not
touch his. "You'll have to do what everybody else does, which is decide
what you think and then behave as though it's true and see whether it
holds up over about nine years."

"That's an appalling methodology."

"It's the worst methodology I've ever encountered and it is the only
one available for anything worth knowing, and I have been extremely
angry about it since I was about twenty-four."

---

She asked about the form, eventually, and he told her, and she did not
say any of the four things he had been braced for.

"Three years," she said instead. "With the appeal."

"Probably."

"Do you know what you want them to say?"

"Yes."

"Say it, then."

Sazare turned his cup a quarter turn without lifting it.

"I want somebody in that section to read four lines in my handwriting
and write *established* on the bottom of it," he said, "and I do not
want the money, and I'd give the money to the reconstruction fund the
day it arrived. I want the word. I want there to be one document in the
world where a government that spent six years agreeing my family had
ended looks at the question properly and writes down that it hasn't."

"That's a clean want."

"It's not a want, it's a—"

"It's a want, Ruri, and you've just said it out loud in under nine
seconds and a fortnight ago you couldn't finish the word *clan* on a
piece of requisition paper." She put the bottle down. "I'd stop and
notice that, if I were you. You'll be back to auditing it by Thursday
and you'll find something wrong with it, and you'll be right, and it'll
still have happened."

---

She asked one more thing, at the end, on the way out, standing in the
doorway while the rain finished.

"What are you going to do with it?"

"With what?"

"The scroll." She said it entirely casually, which he did not believe
for a second. "Thirty-one feet of a bloodline nobody's ever
documented, sitting in a box on a shelf in a rented room over a
persimmon tree, in a village with the best archive on the continent and
a research directorate that would take it off you tomorrow, and an
eighteen-year-old who is the last person alive who can read the
annotations."

"I don't know."

"Good."

"Good?"

"You've had it seven weeks. If you knew, I'd be worried about you."
Tsunade put her collar up. "Everybody's going to ask you that. Kanzaki
will ask you inside a month, the archive will find out about it within
the year and they will be extremely polite and extremely persistent,
and somebody from research will eventually explain to you that it's a
national asset, and they will all have good arguments."

"And?"

"And it's the one thing in your entire life that nobody can decide for
you," said Tsunade, "including me, and I want that said out loud in a
doorway by the person with the authority to take it off you, so that
when somebody tells you otherwise in three years' time you'll remember
where you heard it first."

She went left. He went right.

---

He wrote it up at the bench, at the eleventh hour, with the window open
a hand's width because the rain had stopped and the room was warm.

*Fourth evening. Did not drink. Talked for two hours.*

*Finding: the drink was not the variable. Threshold, not switch. She
says the useful variable was doing it once and surviving it. I do not
like this answer because it cannot be measured and because it means
that the thing which changed was not chemical and not deliberate and
not mine.*

*Counter-finding, and I am writing this one down because I would
otherwise arrange not to think it: it is also possible that the
variable is her, and that I have been constructing an explanation with
a mechanism in it because the alternative explanation is that I talk
to one specific person and I do not know why.*

*I have no way to test this and will not have one.*

*Second item. She asked what I intend to do with the scroll and I said
I did not know, and she said good, and then told me — in a doorway,
without being asked, as the person with the authority to take it — that
it is mine to decide.*

*I do not know what to do with that either.*

He looked at the page.

Then he got the paulownia box down off the shelf for the first time in
seven weeks and put it on the bench beside the notebook, and did not
open it, and sat there with his hand flat on the lid for a while with
the persimmon moving outside the window.

*Not tonight,* he wrote, eventually.

*But I got it down off the shelf.*
