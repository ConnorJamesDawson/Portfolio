# Chapter 9, Scene 1 — The Edge

*Act 2, part A. Sazare Ruri, age thirteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,500 words.*

---

The woman with the bag did not ask him anything else, and some time after that
the fog began to thin, and Sazare understood dimly that this was because they
were leaving it behind, which was not a thing he had known fog could be.

He had known the fog his whole life as weather. It came up off the harbour and
it sat on the cutting and it took the boats mast-last, and it was as much a fact
of the world as the tide. It had not once occurred to him that it belonged to
the Land of Water the way the Land of Water belonged to it, and that a boat
going west far enough would come out from under it the way you come out from
under a roof.

They came out from under it a little before dawn.

He was watching backwards, at the wake, because watching backwards meant not
watching the fourteen other people in the boat, and so he saw it happen: the
grey behind them went from being everything to being a *thing* — a long low bank
of it, lying on the sea from one end of the world to the other, with a top edge.

The fog had a top edge. Above the top edge there were stars.

He looked at it for a long time.

Somewhere behind that bank was the whole of it — Nagihama and the cutting and
the third bridge and the compound with the bar on the gate, all of it sitting
under that lid the way he had sat under it for thirteen years without knowing it
was a lid. From the outside it looked small. That was the thing he could not
take in. From the outside the entire Land of Water was a smudge of weather lying
on the sea, and the sea went on past it in every direction, enormous and
uninterested, and the sky came all the way down to a horizon that was perfectly,
cleanly straight.

There had been a girl in his year who had argued that the continent had an edge.
She had produced a piece of reasoning so unanswerable that Mizuho had stopped
the lesson and made her say it again, and Sazare could no longer remember the
reasoning, only the fact of it, and the specific pleasure on her small serious
face at being made to repeat it.

He looked at the horizon, which had an edge, and was straight, and was the
first thing he had ever seen with no fog in front of it.

*Nao would have liked this*, he thought, and then had to stop thinking for a
while, and gripped the gunwale with his good hand until the wood hurt him.

---

The transfer happened in the middle of the day, in open water, and nobody
explained it to him because nobody explained anything to him.

A second boat came up on them from the south — a fat, filthy, working boat with
nets stacked amidships and a smell that arrived some time before it did — and
the two ran alongside, and lines went across, and then there was twenty minutes
of moving fourteen people and their bundles from one to the other in a swell,
which is a slow and undignified process involving a great deal of being grabbed
by the forearms.

The man who grabbed Sazare took him by the right forearm.

He did not mean anything by it. He was a fisherman of about fifty with hands
like tarred rope, and he took hold of what was offered the way he had taken
hold of ten thousand crates, and Sazare went white and made no sound at all,
and the boat-medic woman said something short and sharp from behind, and the
fisherman looked down at the strapping and the sling and said, "Ah. Sorry, lad,"
and took him by the collar instead, and swung him aboard like luggage.

They put the fourteen of them down among the nets. The first boat stood off,
came about, and went back the way it had come, towards the bank of fog on the
edge of the world, and Sazare watched it go and understood that it was going
back *in*, and that everybody on it was going back in, and that they did this
all the time — went in and out under the lid, in a fat boat that smelled of
herring, carrying people the way it carried fish.

"Under the nets if I say so," said the fisherman, to all of them, conversationally.
"Not before. Nothing shows from a distance like people hiding."

Nobody hid. They sat among the nets in the sun — there was *sun*, standing sun,
sun with weight to it, and half the fourteen had their faces turned up into it
like plants — and the boat wallowed west, and the baby slept, and the man who
had not once looked at Sazare in three days did not look at him some more.

---

His name was Bandō. Sazare had assembled this the way he assembled everything
now, out of the edges of other people's sentences.

He was a big man, forty perhaps, with a flat quiet face, and he had been one of
the eight who came over the back of the platform, and Tomi — the woman who had
put Sazare on the floor of the boat shed three times, patiently, and then stood
back with her hands loose and said *try again if you want* — had been his
sister.

He did not do anything. That was what made it unbearable. He did not glare, or
mutter, or arrange to be elsewhere; he simply organised the whole of himself, at
every hour of the day, in a swell, in the dark, so that no part of him was ever
turned towards Sazare, and he did it without apparent effort, the way a
compass needle organises itself away from south.

Sazare ate his share at the times when food came round, because the boat-medic
had told him in words of one syllable what she would do to him if he gave his
share away again, and he slept when his body took sleep by force, and twice in
four days he woke with his heart slamming and the sound of his own crystal in
his ears — the high dry note, nine frames in one hand — and both times it was
only the wind in the stays, and both times a man across the boat turned over and
sighed, and it was nobody, and the sky was full of foreign stars.

The dream was always the same and it was not, strictly, a dream of anything
happening.

It was the platform, and the executioner moving his mother along by a pace with
his hand flat, the way you move a chair — and in the dream Sazare understood,
with the complete lucidity of dreams, that if the arrangement could be got
right, if the order could be changed, then the thing itself would not happen;
and he tried to call out the right order, and had no voice; and the hand went on
flatly rearranging his family with infinite patience, forever, and none of the
arrangements was ever the right one, and that was the whole dream.

He did not tell anybody that. There was nobody whose job it was to be told.

---

Land came up on the fourth evening as a green line.

He thought it was a trick of the light. He had seen land from the sea his whole
life and land from the sea was grey — grey stone, grey water, a grey town
stitched along the waterline under a grey lid. This was green from the tideline
to the sky: hills behind hills behind hills, and every one of them furred with
forest the way a pelt is furred, and above two of the valleys there stood tall
white columns that he took for smoke and could not find the fires of.

"Steam," said the boat-medic, following his eyes. She had come to sit beside him
in the bow, which she did once a day, officially to look at the arm. "The whole
country leaks. Hot water out of the ground, everywhere, all year. You can boil
an egg in a puddle in some towns." She unwound the strapping a turn and looked
at what there was to look at, and did not let anything show on her face, which
he had learned meant she was being careful. "They bathe more than anybody in
the world and they're smug about it. You'll see."

"Why does it do that?"

"Do what?"

"The ground. Why is the water hot?"

She wound the strapping back up.

"Do you know," she said, "you're the first person to ask me that in about
thirty years, and I have no idea. Fire under it, I suppose. Something has to be
under it." She tied off the end, neatly. "There's a man where you're going who'll
either know or make something up. You can sort out which."

It was the first time anyone had said anything about where he was going, and
she said it the way you mention weather, and got up, and went back to the
stern.

Sazare sat in the bow with his arm in its sling and watched the green country
come up out of the sea, with its impossible standing columns of steam going
straight up into a clear evening, gold at the tops where the light caught them.

It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen and he had no one to say so to,
and so he said nothing, and looked at it until it was dark.
