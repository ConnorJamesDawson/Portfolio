# Chapter 48, Scene 3 — A Fish Knows Water

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the Hokage's tower. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen.
POV: Sazare. ~1,840 words.*

---

He got eleven questions in and had four lines of notes, and three of
those four were about what she could not tell him.

It was not that she was withholding. That was the first thing he
established and he established it inside about ninety seconds, because
he had spent eleven years being told things by people who did not want
to tell him things and he knew the shape of that from a great distance.

Katsuyu was trying. She was trying with an evident, mild, entirely
uncondescending patience, and she simply did not have it.

"Where is it," he said. "Physically. Is it in the ground, in the air,
in living things, in all three?"

"It is in everything."

"Evenly?"

A pause. "No."

"What makes it uneven?"

"...Some places have more."

"Which places?"

"The old places," said Katsuyu. "The undisturbed ones. Forgive me. I do
not think I am answering you well."

"You're answering me honestly, which is a different measurement." He
was writing left-handed and badly. "Can I try a different sort of
question? Is there a finite amount of it?"

"I do not understand the question."

"If I take a hundred kilos out of a place, is there a hundred kilos
less there afterwards?"

Another pause, longer.

"I do not know how to think about that," said Katsuyu. "It is not a
thing I have ever considered. It is — " and here she stopped, and
started again, in the tone of somebody genuinely hunting. "You are
asking me how much water there is in the sea. I am in the sea. I have
been in the sea for a very long time. I could not tell you where it
begins."

---

He got three facts in forty minutes and every one of them cost him nine
questions.

*One. It does not "do" anything to a body that cannot take it. There is
no attack, no hostility and no direction. Katsuyu was quite firm about
this and it is the only thing she has been firm about.*

*Two. It fills. That is her word and she used it four times and would
not be moved off it. A body that cannot take up natural energy does not
get converted into stone — it gets FILLED, and a thing that is
completely full has no room left in which to be alive. The stone is not
what my hand became. It is what my hand has no room to stop being.*

He put the pencil down when he wrote that, for a moment, and then
picked it up again.

*Three. It can come out. Slowly. Not by the body pushing — the body
cannot push, it is full — but by something else with room in it taking
some of it away. That is how it is normally undone, and it is normally
undone by the person themselves, because a person who is learning
senjutsu has, by definition, been practising having room.*

*I have never practised having room. I have practised not having any. I
cannot hold a sphere between my palms for a count of three because I
have spent eleven years handing everything I have to a material inside
one and a half seconds.*

*Exercise one is the thing. It was the thing in the fifth month and it
is the thing tonight and I did not need a stone hand to find that out
and I have got one anyway.*

---

"Can you take it," said Tsunade.

"Some," said Katsuyu. "Not enough, and not quickly. I have a great deal
of room and I am very slow. I could take perhaps a tenth of what is in
his hand between now and the morning, and by then it will have moved,
and I would be taking a tenth of a larger number."

"So it's me."

"It is you, Tsunade-sama, if it is anyone." Katsuyu settled slightly.
"Though I should say plainly that I do not know whether you can. It is
not medical work. There is nothing to make go faster. It is more like —
"

She stopped.

"Like carrying," she said, unhappily. "I am sorry. I am aware that this
is not useful. I have never had to say any of it before, because
everyone I have ever said anything to about it already knew, and the
ones who did not know were not asking."

"Who did know," said Sazare.

"The ones who learn come to us," said Katsuyu. "That is how it has
always been. They come to the forest and they stay a long time and they
find out by being there, and nobody explains it because there is
nothing to explain. Your grandfather was—"

"Katsuyu."

Tsunade said it in an entirely level voice from the other side of the
table.

"Not tonight," she said. "Please."

---

Sazare did not look up.

He was aware of the exact size of what had just gone past — he had it
complete, in about a second and a half, the whole of it: *your
grandfather*, present tense of the sentence broken off, a creature who
had been summoned by that family for a hundred years and who did not
know it was a live wire because nothing was a live wire to her.

He had it, and he had four questions constructed behind it, and every
one of them was a question he wanted enormously.

He wrote down the three facts he already had.

That was all. He put his pencil on the fourth line and wrote *3. It
can come out. Slowly. By something with room.* and then stopped
writing and looked at the page.

It took about eleven seconds and it was, he thought afterwards, the
single most difficult thing he had done that year and there was no
possible way to explain to anybody why.

*Do not ask.*

*Do not ask it now, do not ask it in four days, and do not construct a
route to it and walk somebody down the route in six months and call it
a conversation.*

*She said please.*

*I have known her two years and eleven months and I have heard her say
that word twice and both times it was to Shizune about a form.*

Across the table, Tsunade was looking at the lamp and not at either of
them.

"Go on," she said, after a moment. "You had a question."

"I've got about forty," said Sazare. "The one I want is: when you say
carrying — is it a rate problem or a capacity problem? If you'd got a
hundred times the room, would it come out a hundred times faster, or
does it come out at whatever speed it comes out at and the room only
tells you how much you can be holding at once?"

Tsunade turned her head.

"That's a good question," said Katsuyu, with what was unmistakably
relief. "I do not know the answer, but that is a good question and I
can tell you which half is wrong. It is not a rate. I have taken it
from things before — small amounts, from ground — and it came at the
same speed whether I was large or small. The size only changes how long
I can go on before I am full myself."

"So it's capacity, and the rate's fixed."

"I believe so."

"Then it's hours," said Sazare, "and it's not a question of who's
stronger, it's a question of who can stand there longest without
filling up."

He heard the shape of what he had just said about four seconds after
saying it and did not take it back, because taking it back would have
been worse.

---

There was a silence.

Tsunade got up and went to the cupboard and unlocked it and got out
nothing in particular, which he recognised, because he had done the
same thing with a lamp on a table in the fifth month.

"Right," she said, with her back to them. "Then that's the plan and
it's a bad plan and it's the only one. Katsuyu takes what she can. I
take the rest. Hours, apparently."

"Tsunade-sama."

"Mm."

"Before you begin," said Katsuyu, "there is something I should say, and
I have been deciding whether to say it for some time, and I have
concluded that I should say it now rather than after, because after
will be too late to be of any use."

"Go on."

"Your chakra is not steady," said Katsuyu.

Tsunade did not turn round.

"It has not been steady since you called me, which was fifty-one
minutes ago. It is not fatigue — I know what your fatigue is, I have
been in it. It is the other thing." The mantle shifted. "I have felt it
four times in forty-one years. I have never mentioned it on any of the
four occasions, because on all four of them you were working, and
because it did not seem to be mine to mention."

"And tonight it is?"

"Tonight you are about to stand and hold something for a number of
hours," said Katsuyu, "and I have watched you do that with steady
hands and I have watched you do it with unsteady ones, and the second
kind takes longer and costs more and you do not stop, and I am not able
to make you stop, and so all I can do is tell you before you start
rather than afterwards."

The lamp went on burning.

"I would also say," Katsuyu added, entirely mildly, "that I have been
here now for nearly an hour and nobody has told me what has happened,
and that on all four of the previous occasions I was told."

---

Tsunade shut the cupboard.

Sazare, sitting on a stool with a notebook on his knee and four lines
in it and a stone hand on a table, kept his eyes on the page and did
not move a muscle, because he had spent two years learning what a room
sounds like when the thing in it is not his.

"He's eighteen," said Tsunade, to the cupboard door. "He works in my
hospital. He got here at five past eleven with a rose in his pocket and
his hand already gone to the second knuckle and a pencil mark on the
first one so he'd have a fixed reference, and he has not once asked me
whether he is going to lose it."

"Ah," said Katsuyu.

"Don't."

"I have not said anything, Tsunade-sama."

"You've said *ah* twice tonight," said Tsunade, "and both times it was
the entire content of a paragraph, and I would remind you that I have
had you for forty-one years and I know exactly what your *ah* is worth."

"Then I shall not say it a third time," said Katsuyu peaceably, and
flowed forward across the scrubbed table towards the thing on the end
of Sazare's arm, "and we shall begin, and you will have the rest of the
night to be angry with me in."
