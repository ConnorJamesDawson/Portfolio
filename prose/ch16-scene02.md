# Chapter 16, Scene 2 — Nakabori

*Act 2, part D. Kanezaki. POV: Nakabori Sōhachi, factor, age fifty-one.
~2,000 words.*

---

The name was Nakabori Sōhachi and he had been a factor on that river for
twenty-six years, and the thing he was proudest of, and said so at any
table that would hold still, was that in twenty-six years he had never
once been out on a tally.

Not once. Not a bale, not a bundle, not a quarter-picul of salt. Other
men signed for what the barge said it was carrying. Nakabori counted, and
counted again in front of the bargemaster, and had made himself
comprehensively disliked along ninety miles of water by doing it, and did
not care, because a factor who signs for what he is told is a factor who
is stealing from his principals whether he knows it or not, and Nakabori
did not steal.

He had done other things. He was quite clear about that in his own mind
and had never bothered to be otherwise.

In the spring of the year the freight allocations were cut, he had gone
to the customs office and given them the Fusabe brothers.

It had been an entirely deliberate act and he had thought about it for
four days beforehand. The Fusabe had been running eleven per cent of
their lumber under a bonded manifest that did not cover it, everybody on
the river knew, everybody on the river also ran a little of something,
and the difference was that the Fusabe had used the margin it gave them
to underbid him on the Kanmuri contract two years running and had been
loud about it at the guild dinner.

So he had written it out — dates, hulls, quantities, in the exact and
merciless hand of a man who had never been out on a tally — and three
men had gone to the house of correction at Ise for four years apiece.

The middle brother had died there in the second winter of something that
went round the block.

Nakabori had heard about it at the counter, from a bargemaster, in the
middle of a discussion about demurrage, and had said *ah, that's hard*,
and had gone on with the discussion, and had thought about it that night
for perhaps eleven minutes and then not again, except sometimes, and not
usefully.

He would have done it again. That was the plain truth of it. He had told
himself the arithmetic often enough that it came out smooth: they broke
the bond, the bond is the whole of the trade, a man who breaks the bond
takes bread off forty households to put it on his own. All of which was
correct. None of which was why he had done it.

He had done it because they had been loud at the guild dinner.

---

The town said other things about him, and the town was wrong about
almost all of them.

The town said he had put the Shimoya girl on a boat, which he had not; he
had lent her mother the fare and had been repaid in eleven instalments
and had kept the tally of that too. The town said the fire at the second
salt shed had been his, which was slander of an actionable kind that he
had never bothered to action, because a man who sues about gossip has
made it a fact. The town said, in a general way that could not be pinned
to any one mouth, that Nakabori had *had people done*, and this had
attached itself to him about six years ago and had never come off, and
was entirely untrue, and had been very useful to him at the counter, and
he had therefore never once denied it.

He was aware, as an intelligent man is aware of the weather, that being
thought worse than you are is a thing you pay for eventually.

He had not expected the bill that autumn, and had he been asked to guess
at its size he would have guessed at a lost contract.

---

His hip had gone in the third month and had never properly come back, and
so the route home had changed, and the changed route was the small
private grief of his year.

Kanmuri Street was the sensible way. Kanmuri Street was also the guild
hall, the three chandlers, and the corner where the Fusabe's mother still
kept her tea stall, and she was eighty-one and had not spoken to him in
four years and stood up when he passed, every time, and looked at him,
and sat down again.

So he went along the water.

It was longer and it was unlit and it was hard on the hip over the
cobbles, and it had the enormous advantage of containing nobody at all
after the sixth hour. He had got to like it, in the way you get to like a
thing you have been driven to. The river at night did not have opinions.
You could stand at the bend where the row jogged out and watch the
lighters go down with their stern lamps and think about your daughter's
marriage portion, which was the only thing Nakabori Sōhachi thought about
now with any pleasure at all.

Her name was Mio and she was fourteen and she was, in the wholly
unremarkable way of daughters, the single justification he had ever found
for any of it. He had two hundred and forty thousand ryō put by for her. He would
have three hundred by the spring after next. He had costed it the way he
costed everything, and he was not going to be out on this tally either.

He was fifty-one and his hip hurt and he had begun, that year, to be
frightened of getting old in a town where nobody would come.

---

The lantern he took to the boy on the salt side because Tsune at the
counter had mentioned him twice.

It was his good lantern — brass, ten years old, a gift from his
principals in a year when they had been pleased with him — and the
hinge-plate had sheared and the pillar was bent and the smith had said
new one, six thousand, which was robbery, and Nakabori had said several
things about that in the smith's own doorway.

The boy's card said MENDING and the boy was about fifteen, sat on a
stool in a lodging window with his left leg out straight in front of him
like a plank, and he took the lantern in both hands and turned it over
twice and did not say anything for long enough that Nakabori began
composing a remark.

"Someone's had this apart before," said the boy.

"Twice."

"Whoever did it the second time put the pillar back a quarter-turn out.
That's why the plate's sheared — it's been working against itself for
years." He held it up so the light came through. "See where the wear's
all on one side of the collar? That's not use. That's an argument."

Nakabori looked, and saw it, and experienced the small pure pleasure of
being shown a true thing about an object he had owned for a decade.

"Can you do it?"

"Yes. Not today. The plate wants making and I've a queue." The boy set it
down on the bench with a care that was slightly excessive for a lantern.
"Three days. Twelve hundred."

"Twelve hundred."

"Twelve hundred."

"The smith wanted six thousand for a new one."

"The smith wanted six thousand because he'd rather sell you a lantern
than mend one, and I'd rather mend one," said the boy, "because I can't
make lanterns."

Nakabori laughed — a short bark of a thing, which surprised him, because
he did not do it often — and paid the twelve hundred there and then, which he
never did, and then, out of an embarrassment he did not examine, put
another four hundred on the bench and said it was for the plate, and left
before the boy could argue about it.

He was two streets away before he identified the feeling, and the
feeling was that he had been treated fairly by somebody who had had no
reason at all to bother.

---

He collected it three days later and stood in the lodging window with it
in his hands for some time.

The plate was new and was not new-looking; the boy had aged the brass to
match, badly on purpose, so it read as a repair from about six years ago
rather than a repair from Tuesday. The pillar was true. The collar had
been packed with something and turned back to where it had been in the
year it was made.

"It'll not shear again," said the boy. "It was never the plate's fault."

"What do I owe you?"

"You paid four hundred over."

"For the plate."

"The plate cost eighty," said the boy, and put three hundred and twenty ryō on the bench
and pushed them across, and looked at him with an expression of complete
and unembarrassed patience while Nakabori worked out that he was not
going to be allowed to leave them there.

He took the three hundred and twenty.

He was a man who had spent twenty-six years being exactly right about
money and it had never once, in all that time, been done *to* him.

"You're not from here," he said, to say something.

"No."

"Well." Nakabori wrapped the lantern in the cloth he had brought for it,
because it was his good lantern. "You'll do well enough. The river's up
this year — everyone's flush. It's the war, of course, over the water. A
war's a wonderful thing for a river." He said it the way he said all his
counter opinions, comfortably, as a man laying down a coin he knows is
good. "Half the lofts in this town are full of somebody's cousins from
the Mist paying three times the rent, and there's four new hulls on the
salt run that wouldn't exist in a quiet year. Terrible business.
Terrible." He tucked the cloth over. "Mind you don't take work in
a ryō's worth of work from those families, though. They'll promise and then
they're gone."

The boy said nothing at all. He had a very still way about him.

"Anyway," said Nakabori. "Good work. I'll say so."

And he did say so — twice that afternoon at the counter and once at the
guild the following day, because a man who does honest work at an honest
price is a thing worth mentioning, and because Nakabori Sōhachi, who had
put three men in a house of correction over a dinner-table remark, had
never in his life been able to hold his tongue about a job well done.

---

On the last afternoon he closed out the salt tally for the eighth month,
and it balanced, as it had balanced for twenty-six years.

He had a letter to write to Mio's aunt about the spring, and a
disagreement to have with the harbourmaster about lamp oil, and a barge
coming up on the morning tide that he intended to count personally
because the bargemaster was new and would need to learn.

He locked the counting-house and went down toward the water at the sixth
hour with his stick and his bad hip and his good lantern, unlit, because
he knew the way.

The evening was cold and clear and there was a lighter going down with
one lamp at its stern, and he stopped at the bend where the row jogged
out, the way he did, to watch it go and to think about three hundred thousand
by the spring after next.

He was thinking that he must ask the boy on the salt side whether he
could do brass in quantity, because the counting-house had eleven
tally-frames and every one of them was a disgrace.
