# Chapter 20, Scene 2 — An Hour

*Act 3, part B. The north shore, in fog. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~3,350 words.*

---

"He doesn't want you to," said Sazare.

Haku turned his head slightly.

"That's the first one, is it?"

"It's the first one." Sazare stood on the wet shingle with the sea
breathing in and out of the fog behind him and made himself say it in
order, the way he had built it, without hurrying. "I've been in that house
twice and I've watched him for a total of about five hours and I'm going
to tell you what I saw, and you can tell me where I'm wrong.

"He asked what I was *for*. Not who I was. He wanted to know what I was
for, because that's how he thinks about everybody who comes near, and it's
how he thinks about himself — two doors, he said, and both of them are
about what a man is *for*.

"He didn't ask what you were for. Not once. He talked about the sword and
the village and the money and the trade, and about me, and about people in
general, and he never once put you and that question in the same sentence,
and he's a man who is not able to stop assessing things.

"He eats whatever you put in the bowl and doesn't mention it, and he knows
perfectly well that you put it there on purpose, and the not-mentioning is
the loudest thing in that house.

"And when you said the thing to him — the tool thing — he made a noise and
changed the subject."

There was a long pause with the sea in it.

"How do you know that," said Haku, "about the tool thing."

"Because you told me you'd said it once and that he'd changed the subject,
and you told me on the hill in a completely level voice as an example of
something else." Sazare kept his hands still. "You didn't notice you'd
said it. It's the only thing you've told me that you didn't decide to."

Haku laughed — a short one, genuinely delighted, with his hand going up.

"You're very good," he said.

"So he doesn't want it. He wants you *alive*, and he can't say so, because
he's a man who has spent thirty years arranging himself so that he never
has to say so, and if you die on that bridge this morning to make his
schedule work you will have given him the one thing he would refuse if
anybody ever put it to him plainly—"

"Every word of that is true," said Haku.

Sazare stopped.

"Every word," said Haku. "Better than true; you've got it in the right
order, which nobody has. It took me four years to see the thing about the
bowl and I have never once heard anybody else say it, and I doubt he could
say it about himself." He shifted his hands in his sleeves. "And you have
drawn precisely the wrong conclusion from it, and I would like to explain
why, because you have earned it."

---

"He does care," said Haku. "I've never had a moment's doubt about it and I
don't need the evidence, though it's nice to have it laid out; thank you.

"But you're talking about it as though the caring is one thing and the use
is another thing, and one of them is real and one of them is the price.
That isn't how it is. It isn't how it's ever been.

"He is a man who cannot say a warm thing. Not *will not* — cannot; it isn't
in him, the way tune isn't in some people. So the caring has to come out
somewhere, and it comes out as *use*. He gives me things to do. He tells
me what he needs. He puts me where the work is and he expects me to be
equal to it, and when I am, he doesn't say anything at all, and the not
saying anything is—"

He stopped for a moment. The sea went out through the stones.

"You've had that," he said. "Haven't you. Somebody who never said a warm
thing to you in two years and you'd have walked into the sea for him."

Sazare thought about a flat dosage voice cracking one degree, the width of
a hair, over a lamp in a treatment room. *You'll go taught.*

"Yes," he said.

"Then you know it isn't a substitute for the other thing. It *is* the
other thing. It's the whole of it and there isn't a second, warmer version
underneath waiting to be got at." Haku's voice was perfectly even. "So
when you say *he wants you alive*, you're right. And when you imagine a
version of this morning where I am alive and he has what he wanted — you
are imagining a house with two people in it who have nothing whatever to
do with each other. He would be kind to me. He is capable of that; he'd
manage it for years. And I would be a person in a room, being kind to, and
there would be no reason on earth for me to be in the room.

"That isn't me choosing dying over living, and I'd rather you didn't hear
it that way. It's me telling you that the thing you're offering me isn't
the thing you think it is."

---

"You're a person," said Sazare. "Not a tool. That's the second one and I'm
saying it anyway."

"Yes," said Haku. "I know."

"That's it? *I know?*"

"I've always known. I don't understand why everybody assumes that's the
confusion." He sounded, for the first time, faintly impatient — the only
time in the whole hour. "I know I'm a person. I get cold. I like the
herbalist. I was pleased about the veranda for nine days and I'm still
pleased about it. There's nothing wrong with my grasp of what I am.

"A tool isn't what I am. It's what I'm *for*. They're not the same word
and I have never once mixed them up, and every single person who has ever
said that sentence to me has assumed I had."

He turned and looked out into the white where the sea was.

"Here is the thing nobody follows me on," he said. "Being a person is what
nobody wanted. That's not self-pity, it's chronology. I was a person for
seven years in a house in the north, and then my father found out what my
mother was, and killed her, and came for me — and I was a person the whole
time, the entire way through, and being one did not help at all, and then
I was a person for about eight months in the snow under a bridge, eating
out of bins, being a person very hard, and nobody came.

"And then a man stopped in front of me and looked at me for a while — I
remember this better than I remember my mother's face, which I would like
you to sit with for a moment — and what he said was: *you have got
something. You'd be useful.*

"That is the first time in my life anybody wanted anything of me. That's
the whole ledger. On one side, seven years and eight months of being a
person, which nobody wanted; on the other, one sentence about being
useful, from somebody who meant it."

He looked back at Sazare.

"So when you tell me I'm a person, not a tool, you are asking me to prefer
a version of myself that nobody has ever asked for over the version
somebody came and got. And I understand that you mean it kindly, and I
would like you to hear what it sounds like from where I'm standing."

---

The fog moved. Somewhere out on the flats a curlew went off — that long
falling whistle they do — and the sound came from nowhere and went
nowhere.

Sazare stood on the shingle and knew he had one left, and that it was the
one he had thrown away twice on the walk over and picked up again both
times, and that using it meant saying out loud, to another human being, a
thing he had not said to anybody in four years and had gone to some
trouble not to say to himself.

He did it.

"I was used," he said, "by somebody who loved me. So I know what it is.
That's the third one. I'd rather not have said it and I'm saying it."

Haku did not say anything. He simply turned to face him properly, and put
his hands down out of his sleeves, and gave him the whole of his
attention, and it was — Sazare thought afterwards, on a road, in another
country — the single most courteous thing anybody ever did for him.

So he told it. Flatly and badly, because that is how you tell that.

His sister, fifteen years old, coming in at the third hour with her hands
cold. The chip game on the step in the fog — *ours, safe, knock* — taught
to an eight-year-old as a puzzle, two hours a week for four years, because
a boy who thinks he is playing a game learns faster than a boy who thinks
he is learning. The errands. *Take this to the net store, second peg, put
it in the joint, don't knock, come home the long way.* Forty of them. Sixty.
A hundred; he had never counted and would have been able to.

Four years of carrying live traffic for a resistance network through a
district under a purge, at eight, at nine, at ten, at eleven, and being
told he was helping with the deliveries.

"She never told me," he said. "Not once. Not when I was eleven and could
have understood it, not when I was twelve, not at the end. And she was
extremely careful with me — I've worked it out since; she never gave me
anything that would have got me killed if I'd been stopped, she used me
for the boring parts, she thought about it — and she used me for four
years and let me think I was running errands, and then they came for the
house, and she was not in it, and the rest of them were."

The tide hissed out through the stones.

"So," said Sazare. "I'm four years past it. I've had a long time to work
on it and I've worked on it properly. And I can tell you it does not go
away, and it does not resolve, and there is not one single day when I do
not have it, and that is what being used by somebody who loved you *does*,
and I am telling you because you're about to do it to yourself on purpose
and I would like you to have the information."

---

Haku listened to the whole of it without moving.

Then he said, gently:

"Can I ask you something? It's not a trick. I'd genuinely like the answer
and I think you're the only person who has it."

"Ask."

"If she had sat you down," said Haku. "When you were eight. On that step,
in the fog, with the chips in her hand. And she had said: *I need you to
carry things for me. They're not what I'm telling you they are. It's
dangerous, and I can't explain it, and if you're caught it will be bad,
and I am asking you.*"

He paused.

"Would you have said yes?"

"Yes," said Sazare.

It came out of him before the question had finished landing. It came out
flat and instant and without one atom of hesitation anywhere in it, and he
heard it come out, and heard how fast it had been, and stood on a beach in
the fog with the whole of him going very cold indeed.

"Every time?" said Haku.

"Every time."

"At eight."

"At eight. At nine. At any point up to the last morning, and if she'd
asked me on the last morning I'd have said yes to that as well."

"Yes," said Haku. "I thought so."

He let it sit for a moment. He did not look pleased. He looked, if
anything, sorry — and he went on anyway, in the same even voice, because
he had been asked for the truth and was the sort of person who finishes.

"Then you're not angry that she used you," he said. "You've never once
been angry about that. If you were angry about being used, that answer
would have taken you longer than nothing at all.

"You're angry that she didn't ask."

---

Sazare stood on the shingle.

There was a very long silence and the sea filled it, in and out, in and
out, and the fog went on being white in every direction, and somewhere off
to the right the shingle ticked and settled the way it does on a falling
tide.

He went at it from four directions. He did it properly; he gave it
everything he had; he was, in the strictest sense, doing the only thing he
had ever been able to do, and he did it at full stretch on a beach at six
in the morning with a boy of fifteen waiting patiently for him to finish.

It held from all four.

"Yes," he said.

"I know," said Haku. "That's why I asked."

He came a step closer over the wet stones, and when he spoke again it was
without any triumph at all, which made it very much worse.

"Nobody asked me either," he said. "I want to be clear about that; I'm not
claiming something I haven't got. He didn't ask. He looked at a starving
child in the snow and said *you'd be useful* and I got up and followed him
and neither of us has ever pretended it was a negotiation.

"But he *told* me. From the second week. He has never once let me be
confused about what I am for, or let me think I was running errands, or
let me find out afterwards. Every single thing that has been done with me
I have known about while it was happening, and I have said yes to all of
it, out loud, every time, and I am going to say yes to this one in about
forty minutes.

"That is not the same as being asked. It's much less. And it is
immeasurably more than you got, and it is the whole of the difference
between us, and it is why I am going to walk up that causeway and you are
standing on a beach at six in the morning trying to stop me."

He put his hands back in his sleeves.

"You're not asking me to save my life," said Haku. "You're asking me to
give up the only thing you ever wanted."

---

Sazare could not speak for some time.

When he could, what came out was not an argument, because he no longer had
one, and he knew it, and there is a species of honesty that only turns up
after everything else has been taken away.

"I don't want you to die," he said.

"I know," said Haku. "Thank you. That's the best thing anybody's said to
me in about four years, and I've thought about it a great deal since
Thursday and I'd like you to know that it made a difference to the week."

"That isn't—"

"It isn't enough. No." He said it kindly. "Can I ask you the other one?"

"Go on."

"Was she wrong?"

And there it was.

Sazare stood in the fog on a foreign beach and looked at a boy in a grey
cloak with his hair down loose, and understood — about a second and a half
too late, the way he understood everything — that the entire hour had been
walking toward this one question, and that Haku had known it would from
the moment he came out onto the veranda, and had let him do all three of
his appeals in order first, out of courtesy, so that he would not be able
to say afterwards that he had not been allowed to finish.

"I've been trying to answer that for four years," he said.

"I know. And?"

The sea went out through the stones.

"No," said Sazare. "She wasn't wrong."

"No," Haku agreed.

And that was the end of it, and neither of them said anything else about
it, because there was nothing else and both of them could see the whole
shape of it lying there on the shingle between them: that Kohaku Ruri had
chosen a thing over her own family and it had killed them, and her brother
had spent four years at it and could not call her wrong; and that the boy
standing eight feet away was about to choose the same kind of thing in the
same kind of weather; and that Sazare had just, with his own mouth, in
front of a witness, licensed it.

---

"You should go back over the ridge," said Haku, after a while.

"I'm not going to."

"No, I didn't think you were." He glanced up, at the fog, at the flat
white nothing where the sky was going to be. "Then listen, because this
part is practical and I'd rather say it than have you find it out.

"If you are on that bridge, and I see you, I will take your left knee. I
have thought about how and it takes two seconds and you would not be able
to stand up again inside them, and I would do it without the slightest
difficulty, and I would be sorry about it afterwards and it would make no
difference at all to my hand at the time. That is not a threat and I am
not trying to frighten you. It is simply what will happen, and I would
rather you had it in advance than found out.

"So be somewhere I can't see you."

"Right," said Sazare.

"Under it, I should think," said Haku. "Low water's at a quarter past
seven."

Sazare looked at him.

"You're the fourth person I've ever met who thinks about tides," said
Haku, and smiled, and then the smile went — not replaced by anything;
simply gone, so that for about two seconds Sazare was looking at his
actual face, which was fifteen years old and extremely tired.

"One more thing and then I'll go," he said. "It's a favour and you can say
no."

"Say it."

"There's a pan in that house. It's a good one; I've had it three years and
it's been scoured properly its whole life, and there's a lid on it that
somebody mended eleven days ago so it shuts. There's about six days of
food, and a jar of the yarrow, and a quilt, and two knives, and a
lamp with oil in it."

The fog moved between them.

"O-Kiyo could use the pan," said Haku. "She's got that iron thing with the
crack in it and she boils everything twice because of it. Don't give it to
her from me — she'd not take it, and it would be a thing she had to think
about. Just leave it on the step."

Sazare stood absolutely still.

"Haku."

"And there's four foot of larch under the veranda," said Haku, "which is
yours anyway, and you should have it, because you'll want a plane body and
that hornbeam you got is going to split on you; it's got a shake in the
end grain about two inches in. Take it before you go."

"*Haku.*"

"I know," said Haku.

He said it without any drama whatever, the way he had said *I always use a
cloth*, and he was already turning away up the shingle toward the house
where a man was waiting who could not say a warm thing.

"I've known since Tuesday," he said, over his shoulder, into the fog. "It
isn't a sad thing. It's the best thing I've got, and it's *mine*, and I'd
have had to give it back one day anyway. Don't be on the bridge."
