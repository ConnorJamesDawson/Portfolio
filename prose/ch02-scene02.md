# Chapter 2, Scene 2 — The Unit

*Act 1, beat 7. Sazare Ruri, age nine. POV: Sazare. ~2,000 words.*

---

The thing about Mizuho was that she did not read.

She talked. She drew on the wall, badly. She sat on the front desk with one boot
up and did the whole history of the Second War as an argument between four men in
a room, doing all four of them, and when she got to the part where the treaty
fell through she stopped for so long that a girl at the back started crying and
had to be taken out. She had books. The books lived on the shelf behind her and
served, as far as anybody in that room could tell, no function at all.

So when she came in on a wet morning in the fifth month with a book already open
in her hand, thirty-eight nine-year-olds noticed.

"Right," she said. "New unit."

She sat down. Not on the desk — behind it.

"This is going in from this year. It's four sessions. There'll be questions on it
at the end of the year, so you'll want to write it down, and I'd write down the
words rather than what you think the words mean, because the words are what
they'll ask you for."

Somebody said, "Miss, what's it about?"

"It's about clans," said Mizuho, and began to read.

---

It went on for about forty minutes.

Sazare wrote most of it down. He was a reasonable writer for nine and a very good
copier, and he had worked out early that if you wrote fast and did not think you
could have the whole thing on the page and think about it afterwards, which was
generally more efficient.

*Certain lineages within the shinobi population transmit inheritable techniques
by descent. These are termed kekkei genkai. The distribution of such lineages is
uneven both within and between the great villages.*

*Where a lineage is concentrated in a single family, and where that family
maintains internal cohesion, the resulting body may accrue capabilities and
loyalties which are not fully accounted for within the village structure.*

*This is termed a clan-based risk to village stability.*

*Historical instances are set out below. Candidates should be able to identify
the four principal categories of risk and give one example of each.*

Then there were the four categories, and the examples, and the dates, and Mizuho
read all of it in a voice Sazare had never heard her use — not cold, exactly.
Flat. The voice of somebody reading out a list of names at a gate.

At the end she closed the book.

"Right," she said. "That's the first session. Three more."

A hand went up near the front.

"Miss — what's a kekkei genkai?"

"It's in the second line. It's a technique you get from your family. Your family
can do it and other families can't, and there's nothing you can do to learn it if
you weren't born to it."

"Like what?"

"Like a lot of things."

"But like *what*?"

Mizuho looked at the ceiling for a second.

"Like ice," she said. "There's a family — there was a family — that could make ice
out of nothing. Not out of water. Out of the air, out of a dry room, in the
middle of summer. That's a kekkei genkai."

Thirty-eight children made a noise.

"*Miss.*"

"Yes, it's very impressive. Write down the four categories."

"Can they still—"

"Write down," said Mizuho, "the four categories."

They wrote down the four categories.

---

Sazare thought about it that evening while he was doing the small lattices, and
concluded that he did not know anybody with a kekkei genkai, and that it was a
shame.

He was aware that this was slightly unfair of him. Kohaku, when she was home, could
do a thing with a coin where it went from one hand to the other without appearing
to cross the gap, and would not explain it. His mother could sit motionless for
four hours and produce, out of a tray of wet grey sand, an object that had not
previously existed. Iwao had once mended a lattice by holding it and looking at it
crossly.

But that was not a kekkei genkai. That was the work.

The work was the work. It was frames and sand and copper wire and the price of
copper wire and Genzō losing his spectacles and the boxes going up the hill on the
first of the month. It was a trade. Other families cured fish and made rope and
built boats. His family grew crystal, badly on his part, and sold it, and that was
what they did, and it had a name and the name was *the work*, and nobody in that
house had ever in his hearing used any other word for it at all.

So he did the corners properly and did not think about it again.

---

Two things did happen, though, and he noticed both of them and connected neither.

The first was that when he told his father about the ice family — over dinner,
with relish, because it was the best thing he had heard all month — his father
said "hm" and asked him to pass the pickles, and Iwao said nothing whatsoever,
and the conversation went to the wire.

The second was smaller.

The unit had four sessions, and by the third one the novelty had gone entirely and
the room had settled into the flat hostility of children being taught something
that will be on a test. Mizuho read. They wrote. Nobody asked anything.

Sazare, who wrote faster than he needed to, ran out of things to copy about
two-thirds of the way through, and looked up.

Mizuho was reading with the book held rather high, so that the top of it came up
almost to her chin, and her eyes were going along the lines, and she was speaking,
and there was about a page and a half left to go.

She was not turning the pages with any particular attention.

He watched her for a while — he was nine, and it was raining, and there was
nothing else to look at — and after a bit he realised what was odd about it, which
was that when Mizuho talked about anything she looked at the room, always, every
time, all the way round, and that in three sessions of this she had not looked up
once.

At the end she closed the book and put it back on the shelf with the others.

"Bowls out," she said. "Six minutes. Anyone who rounds up does it twice."

And she came down off the platform and went round the desks the way she always
did, and stopped at his and looked at the water for a good while, and said
"better," which was the first time she had said anything about it since the first
morning, and Sazare went home so pleased with himself that he forgot the whole
lesson entirely.
