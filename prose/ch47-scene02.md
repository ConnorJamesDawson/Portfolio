# Chapter 47, Scene 2 — What He Has Never Done

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, training ground eleven. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,859 words.*

---

He slept eleven hours, which he had not done since the invasion, and
went out to the ground on the second evening of the eighth month with
the whole ask constructed and rehearsed and sitting in his mouth.

He did not get to use any of it.

They did the falling — an hour of it, on ground that had gone hard
again after the wet, committed falls now, one point, chosen, and he was
still bad at it and was bad at it in a way that had a floor under it,
which had not been true in the fourth month. And at the end of it,
sitting in the dust with his back against the post, he opened his mouth
and Kakashi said:

"How's the count."

"...Two."

"Since when."

"Since the twenty-fourth of the fifth month. Eleven weeks." Sazare
looked at his hands. "Two threes in that time, both unrepeatable. I
don't know whether they were the same thing happening twice or two
different things that sounded alike."

"Ninety attempts a week?"

"About that."

"So call it nine hundred and eighty attempts for a count of two."
Kakashi had the book out and did not open it. "Sit still a minute.
I've got something and I've been sitting on it since about the sixth
week, and I'd worked out I was going to give it to you when your
arithmetic about yourself went wrong, and it went wrong about nine days
ago."

"Which arithmetic."

"You told Handa you were behind."

Sazare stopped.

"Handa tells me things," said Kakashi. "He's seventy-one and he thinks
I'm a nice young man and he has no idea I'm anything else, and I'd
appreciate it if that continued. What you actually said, apparently, was
that you were eleven years behind and that everybody in this village
had the first term at eleven and you hadn't."

"That's true."

"It's factually true and it is a piece of arithmetic with one enormous
term missing," said Kakashi, "and you have been doing it every night
for eleven weeks and getting a wrong answer, and I've let you, because
a man who's told he's better than he thinks stops working. You've now
gone past the useful part of it, so."

---

He put the book away entirely, which he did about four times a year.

"How many hand seals have you made in your life," he said.

Sazare opened his mouth and closed it.

"Take your time."

"None."

"None."

"I don't — " He was working it, backwards, eleven years at a time. "I
don't know any. I've watched about nine hundred of them. I could draw
you the twelve from memory because they're in a schoolbook I've had out
for six weeks and I read the diagrams. I've never used one. There has
never been an occasion where using one would have done anything,
because I've never known a technique that required a sequence."

"Right," said Kakashi. "Now listen, because I'm going to say this once
in the plain version and then I'm going to say the difficult half, and
the difficult half is the one you'll want."

"All right."

"Seals are notation."

Sazare went very still.

"That's the whole of it," said Kakashi. "Moulding chakra is almost
impossible to describe. You can't hand somebody an intention. So the
discipline worked out, over a few hundred years, that certain physical
positions reliably produce the same mould in nearly anybody, and it
wrote them down, and now you teach the *position* instead of the
thing." He turned his hand over, made the tiger seal, and held it.
"Twelve signs. Sequences. Every technique in the world is transmitted
as a string of them, in a scroll, in an academy, from a teacher to a
student, and it works, and it is the single most successful piece of
engineering in the history of this profession."

"It's the ladder."

"It's the ladder," Kakashi agreed. "Somebody built one, four hundred
years ago, and every ninja alive climbs it and almost nobody notices
they're on it."

He let the seal go.

"You never learned the notation," he said. "You learned the sound."

---

Sazare sat in the dust with his hands on his knees.

"Say the difficult half," he said.

"The difficult half is what it cost you and it's worse than you've
worked out, and I'd rather you had it from me." Kakashi leaned back
against the other post. "You have no jutsu. Not one. No Clone, no
Replacement, nothing off any shelf in this village, and you've been
walking around for two years assuming that's because nobody got round
to teaching you."

"Yes."

"It isn't. It's because the entire transmission medium is closed to
you." He said it flatly, in the camber register. "Every technique in
the world travels as a seal sequence. That's the road. You can't read
the road. I could sit here for a year and hand you sequences and you'd
be doing what a man does when he copies letters in a language he
doesn't speak — you'd get the shapes and nothing would happen, because
the shape is a lever for a thing you already do a completely different
way."

"Then I can never be taught anything."

"You can never be taught anything the *normal* way," said Kakashi. "By
anyone. Ever. Not by me, not by the Hokage, not by a scroll. Everything
you ever get, for the rest of your life, you will get the way you got
the crystal — on your own, at a bench, wrong, nine hundred times." He
let that sit. "That is a life sentence and I'm not going to dress it
up. It's also the reason you're going to spend six months learning to
fall over, because falling over is one of about four things I can teach
you by making you do it instead of by telling you how."

---

The crickets started at the far end.

"Now the other half," said Kakashi. "And you're going to want to be
careful with it, which is why it's second."

"Go on."

"Every shinobi alive watches hands. It is the first thing you're taught
and the last thing you stop doing. You read the sequence, you know
what's coming, you know how long you've got, and you spend your entire
career doing arithmetic off other people's fingers."

Sazare felt the whole of it arriving before Kakashi said it.

"There is nothing to read on you," said Kakashi.

"...No."

"No wind-up. No sequence. No tell. Nobody in the world can look at you
and know what's about to happen or how long they have, because there is
no interval in which to look." He put his hands back in his pockets.
"You did it in the street. You put a slab on your own forearm in about
a second and a half in the middle of an engagement with both hands
occupied, and you wrote it up as a technical failure because the plate
came apart, and not one person who read that report — including me,
for about four months — registered the actual event, which is that a
man produced armour with no hands and no seals in the time it takes to
take a breath."

"Nobody said anything."

"Nobody said anything because everybody in four countries writes
*bloodline* in the box where the mechanism goes," said Kakashi, "and I
am as guilty of it as the clerks are, and I have had a Sharingan in my
head for fifteen years and have copied more chakra moulding than
almost anybody alive, and I still filed it under *Shōton* and moved
on."

---

"You said to be careful."

"I did." Kakashi did not move. "Because there's a third thing and it's
the one that matters and it's the reason I've been making you fall over
for four months instead of teaching you anything."

"Which is."

"Seals are also a delay."

Sazare looked up.

"Three seconds of a sequence," said Kakashi, "is three seconds in which
somebody can shout *stop*. It's a gap between deciding and doing. Every
shinobi in the world has one and nobody thinks of it as a feature, and
it is the only reason anybody has ever been talked out of a technique
halfway through — including me, twice, and I would be a considerably
worse person without both of them."

He looked down at Sazare in the dust.

"You have no gap," he said. "Intention and effect are the same instant.
Which means nobody can read you, nobody can warn you, and nobody can
get between you and the thing you have decided to do — not a
teammate, not a Hyūga in a corridor shouting at you to stop, not
anybody."

"That's the corridor."

"That's the corridor, and it took me until about the sixth week of this
year to understand why you couldn't turn it off, and it isn't a moral
failing and it isn't a design failure in the crystal." Kakashi's voice
did not change at all. "It's that the brake every other person in this
profession has is a physical one, and it's in their hands, and you
haven't got hands to put it in."

Sazare sat there for a while.

"The governor."

"The governor," said Kakashi, "is not a refinement. It is the only
brake that exists on you, and it exists because you built it, out of
stone, four months ago, before you had the faintest idea what it was
for. And you've applied it to crystal and you've applied it to your
mouth, and I'd like you to notice that both of those were things you
did *after* somebody got hurt."

---

He walked him back the long way, as always, and said nothing for most
of it.

At the bottom of the hill Sazare said, "You could have told me this in
the fourth month."

"I could have."

"Why didn't you."

"Because in the fourth month you'd have heard the middle part and not
the last part," said Kakashi. "Everybody does. You tell an eighteen-
year-old that nobody in the world can see him coming and he stops
listening at *nobody in the world can see him coming*."

"And now?"

"Now you've spent eleven weeks failing at a thing eleven-year-olds do,
and you've written the number down every night, and you didn't stop."
Kakashi got the book back out. "A man who has done nine hundred and
eighty repetitions for a count of two will hear the last part. That's
the whole of my reasoning and I'd have waited another six months if
I'd needed to."

Sazare stood in the road.

"I came out here to ask you something," he said.

"I know. You've had it in your mouth since we started and you've been
sitting on it for an hour and a half, which for you is a remarkable
piece of self-control and I'd like it noted." Kakashi turned a page.
"Ask me tomorrow. Not because I'm putting you off. Because whatever it
is, you're going to ask it better having had a night with what I've
just said, and I'd like the better version."
