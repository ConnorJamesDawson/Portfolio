# Chapter 4 — Schedule B

*Act 1, beat 11. Kirigakure district office, ninth month. POV: Hidaka. ~3,900
words.*

---

The trouble with the new forms was that they were the same size as the old forms.

Hidaka had said this in the meeting. He had said it politely and at what he
considered to be the correct moment, which was after the man from the ministry had
finished and before anybody had had time to agree with him, and he had said: if
you are adding a schedule to a form, the form has to get bigger, or the boxes have
to get smaller, and if the boxes get smaller people write outside them, and if
people write outside them we cannot read them, and if we cannot read them the
whole exercise is a waste of everybody's morning.

The man from the ministry had said that was noted.

So now there was Schedule B, and Schedule B had nine columns on a sheet designed
for six, and Hidaka had a satchel with two hundred of them in it, and a quota of
eight households a day, and a chair at the district office that had ruined his
back over eleven years and which he had been promised would be replaced in the
spring of a year he could no longer remember.

He was forty-four. It was the ninth month. It was raining.

---

He did the Ozaki first, because Ozaki was on the cutting and it was on the way and
because you wanted an easy one to start.

Fishmonger. Wife, two sons, one daughter, one grandmother. Trade registered
1041. No inherited technique, which Ozaki confirmed with a shrug and a *chance
would be a fine thing*, and Hidaka wrote **N** in the fourth line and did not
require Schedule B, because Schedule B was for households returning **Y**, which
he explained, and Ozaki said "right you are," and that was eleven minutes.

"How many you got today?"

"Eight."

"Eight." Ozaki whistled. "You want a cup of something."

"I'd love a cup of something. I've got eight."

"Suit yourself." Ozaki wrapped him half a dozen small fish anyway, unasked, and
Hidaka took them because refusing would have taken longer than accepting, and put
them in the satchel on top of two hundred copies of Schedule B, where they would
remain for the rest of the day, gradually becoming a factor.

---

The second was a rope-maker called Ide who could not read.

This was the part of the job Hidaka was actually good at, and he knew it, and he
allowed himself a small and entirely private satisfaction about it.

You did not say *can you read*. You said, "I'll take you through it, it's quicker,"
and you turned the form round so it faced you and not them, and you asked the
questions out loud in an order that made sense to a human being rather than in the
order they appeared on the sheet, and you wrote the answers down yourself, and at
the end you said, "That's you, that's your mark there," and you pointed, and they
made their mark, and nobody in the room had to say anything embarrassing to
anybody.

Ide's household returned **N**.

"You sure?" said Hidaka. "Nothing on your wife's side? Nothing anybody's ever said
about your father?"

"Nothing anybody's ever said about my father that I'd repeat."

"Right." Hidaka wrote **N**. "Good enough for me."

He was thorough about that question. He had been thorough about it since the
previous year, when a man three streets over had returned **N** and it had come
out afterwards — through a completely unrelated matter, an assault, something in a
bar — that there was an aunt somewhere with something, and the household had been
brought in and there had been a great deal of unpleasantness about *false return*,
which was a serious matter, and none of which would have happened if the man had
simply written **Y** and specified.

That was the thing people did not understand about forms. The form was not the
danger. The form was the *protection*. An accurate return sat in a drawer for a
year and nobody looked at it twice. It was the discrepancies that got people
pulled in — because a discrepancy meant somebody had lied, and once somebody had
lied everybody wanted to know what about.

He told people this, when they hesitated. He told them it in his own words and he
believed every syllable of it, because it was true, and because he had watched it
be true.

---

The third and fourth were nothing. The fifth was a woman who cried, which
happened, and which was not about the form; it was about her son, who had gone to
Nagihama and not written, and Hidaka sat and listened for a quarter of an hour he
did not have, and then filled in her form, and she returned **N**, and he left her
better than he found her, which was worth a quarter of an hour of anybody's day.

The sixth was the Ruri.

---

It was a proper house. Not grand — it was the lower district, nothing there was
grand — but it was old, and it had a wall and a yard and a gate that shut, and a
north room with nine trestles in it and a smell he could not place until he had
been in there for a minute, which was wet sand.

The man of the house met him at the gate and was pleasant. Ren Ruri, forty,
married in from a net-making family, which Hidaka knew before he arrived because
it was on last year's return. He had that quality some men had of asking you three
questions and not waiting for the answers, which Hidaka had learned to identify
early in his career as either extreme warmth or extreme nerves, and which was
usually both.

There was an uncle. There was a woman, the wife, who was the Ruri by blood and who
came out of the workshop with her sleeves pinned and shook his hand and offered him
nothing at all, and who watched him for the entire hour with an attention he found
faintly wearing and did not resent. In his experience the ones who watched you like
that had usually had something happen to them at some point involving a man with a
satchel.

And there was a boy of about ten who came and stood at the corner of the table
where he could see upside down and stayed there for the whole of it.

"Right," said Hidaka, and got the forms out.

---

Name. Trade — *crystal-growing, registered 0987, guild-linked (inscription
external)*, which was already on last year's and which he copied across. Persons
resident: eight, of whom two currently absent — one daughter on active service, one
cousin at Nagihama.

"Fourth line," he said. "Inherited technique in the household."

"Yes," said Ren.

"Yes." Hidaka wrote **Y**. "Specify?"

"Shōton," said the wife, from the end of the table. "Crystal Release."

"Spell it for me."

She spelled it for him. He wrote it in the box, which was too small, and finished
it in the margin.

"And names of persons in whom manifest."

"Four," said the uncle, leaning in. He was eager. Hidaka had met a great many eager
uncles. "Hisui, that's my niece, that's her. Kohaku, that's the daughter, she's the
one that's away. Myself. And the boy."

Hidaka wrote the four names.

"That's the same as last year," he said.

"Same as last year," Iwao agreed. "Same as the year before, and the year before
that."

"Good. That's what we like." He blotted it. "Right. Now there's a new bit."

---

He got Schedule B out and turned it round so it faced him, and put it next to the
main sheet, and squared them up.

"It's an annex to the trade return," he said. "It's only for registered trades
where the technique's part of the work. So — you, the smiths who do the folding,
one or two of the shipwrights. Twenty-odd households in the district. It's not
a new question, it's the same information you'd have given the guild anyway, it's
just that now it comes to us instead of us asking the guild for it, which if
anything is fewer people knowing your business, not more."

That was true. He had checked, because he had been asked it twice already that
week and had wanted to be able to answer it honestly.

"What's on it?" said the wife.

"Output. Grades and volumes." He turned it so she could see, which he was not
required to do and always did. "Annual production by grade. Working practice —
that's how many frames, how long a cycle, that sort of thing. Capability by
individual, which is where the four names come back in. And then a couple of
lines at the end for anything that doesn't fit."

"Capability."

"What each of them can actually do," said Hidaka. "Because it's not the same for
everybody, is it, and last year we had a household return four names and it turned
out three of them couldn't do anything much at all, and they got assessed as
though they could, and it caused them all sorts of grief. This is meant to stop
that." He tapped the sheet. "It's more accurate. More accurate is better. It's
always better."

The wife looked at the sheet for a while.

"All right," she said.

---

It took forty minutes, which was thirty more than he had budgeted, and it was, he
thought afterwards, the most interesting forty minutes he had spent that month.

He had never had it explained properly before. He had been in and out of that
district for eleven years and he had walked past crystal in eleven hundred shop
windows and he had never once known how the stuff was made, and it turned out to
be nothing like he had assumed. He had assumed it was cut, like a gemstone, out of
something you dug up.

It was grown. You laid a bed of grey sand in a wooden frame with a copper lattice
over it, and you put your hands either side, and you sat there, and you did not
move, and after four hours there was a thing in the tray that had not been there
before.

"Four hours," he said.

"Four for a good piece," said the wife.

"And you can't — stop? Halfway?"

"You can stop. Then you've got half a piece and you've wasted a night."

"Right," said Hidaka, writing. "Right."

The columns went in one at a time. Frames: nine. Cycles per month: twenty-two,
allowing for the sand. Annual production by grade — she gave him the numbers off
the top of her head and then, when he asked, went and got a book and checked them,
and two of them were wrong, and she corrected them downward.

He liked her considerably better after that.

Capability by individual. Hisui Ruri: nine frames, four-hour cycle, all grades.
Kohaku Ruri: assessed at, and here the wife said "put four frames," and the uncle
said "she can do six," and the wife said "she's on active service, she does four
frames when she's home, put four," and Hidaka put four, because the wife was
right and because she was clearly the one who had thought about it.

Iwao Ruri: three frames, lower grades. Given freely, without embarrassment, by the
man himself.

And then the boy.

---

"He's ten," said the wife.

"That's fine. There's a column for it. Under-fifteens get assessed at whatever
they're at, it's not held against anyone."

"He makes small pieces."

"Then that's what I'll put." Hidaka looked up. The boy was still at the corner of
the table with his chin nearly on it. "What sort of small? Small how?"

"Earrings," said the boy.

"Earrings." Hidaka wrote *earring-grade, single small piece, extended cycle*, and
then, because there was a column for it and he had been told to complete all
columns, "Hollow or solid?"

There was a pause of about a second and a half.

"Hollow," said the wife.

"Not any more," said the boy.

The wife said, "Sazare."

"It's *not*," said the boy, and went out of the room, and came back about eight
seconds later with something in his fist, and put it down on the table next to
Schedule B.

---

Hidaka picked it up.

He had, by that point in his career, held a very large number of objects that
people had put down on tables in front of him to prove things, and almost all of
them had been disappointing.

It was about the length of his thumbnail, and it leaned. That was the first thing.
It went up out of the base at an angle and it had a definite crook in it about two
thirds of the way along, and the surface was not polished, and it looked — he was
not an expert and would not have said this out loud — it looked like something a
child had made.

He held it up to the window.

The bottom third of it had a grey emptiness in the middle, like a bubble in glass.

The rest of it, all the way to the tip, was clear.

"That's nine tenths," he said.

"About that."

"He's ten."

"He's ten," said the wife.

Hidaka turned it over. The uncle was watching him. The father had gone very
slightly still. The boy was standing at the corner of the table with his ears
scarlet and an expression Hidaka recognised immediately, having been ten himself
once, which was the expression of a child who has done something magnificent and
has just realised he does not know whether he was allowed to.

"How long did you feed it?" said Hidaka.

"Fourteen hours."

"*Fourteen.*"

"I did it over two nights. You're not supposed to. It's supposed to be one go." The
boy shifted from foot to foot. "I did seven and then I stopped it and I did seven
more the next night and it *worked*, and nobody does that, mum says nobody does
that, and I don't know why nobody does that because it worked—"

"Sazare," said the wife.

"—and it's got a line in it," the boy said, in a rush, "there's a line where I
stopped it, you can see it if you hold it sideways, and it's not weak there, I
tested it, I put it under the—"

"*Sazare.*"

The boy stopped.

Hidaka sat with a crooked earring between his finger and thumb and one hand on
Schedule B, and became aware that he was thirty minutes over on his sixth
household of eight, with the rain getting worse, and half a dozen fish going off
in a satchel by his foot.

"Well," he said. "That's very good."

---

The column was two fingers wide and had a heading that read **Grade (see key)**,
and the key had five grades in it, and the lowest one was *ornamental, small*.

He wrote *ornamental, small*.

Then he looked at it.

Then he put the nib down and sat back, and rubbed the bridge of his nose, and
thought about the man from the ministry saying *that's noted*, and about a form
designed by somebody who had plainly never sat at a kitchen table in his life.

Because *ornamental, small* was correct. It was exactly correct. Anybody reading
that column would see *ornamental, small* and move on to the next household, and
the boy would go into the file as what he currently was, which was a child who
made earrings.

And that was not, in Hidaka's opinion, the whole of it.

He had watched a woman with eleven years of it behind her say *four hours for a
good piece*, in the tone of somebody stating a law of nature. And then a
ten-year-old had told him, with his ears going red, that he had stopped one
halfway through and started it again the next night and it had held.

Hidaka did not know whether that was remarkable. He was a clerk. He had learned
about crystal forty minutes ago.

But he had spent eleven years learning to tell the difference between a household
that was giving him the answers and a household that was telling him something,
and something had happened in that room in the last five minutes, and everybody in
it except the boy had known it.

There were two lines at the bottom of Schedule B under the heading **Notes /
anything not covered above**, which in two hundred forms he had filled in perhaps
nine times.

He picked the nib back up.

*Youngest returns ornamental grade but note: subject reports achieving a solid
piece across two interrupted cycles (7hrs + 7hrs) contrary to standard practice.
Piece inspected. Solid to approx. 9/10 with no visible fault at the join. Subject
is 10 yrs. May be worth the guild's attention.*

He blotted it, and read it back, and was pleased with it.

It was accurate. It was the kind of thing the form ought to have a column for and
did not. And if anybody ever went through this household's file — for a permit, for
an apprenticeship, for anything at all — they would find, at the bottom of an
annex, a note from a district clerk saying that the youngest one was better than
the grade suggested.

Which was, he thought, the least he could do for a boy who had gone out of a room
and come back with something in his fist.

---

"That's you," he said, and turned the sheets round, and pointed. "Head of
household, there. And there."

Ren signed.

"Anything you want to add?"

"No," said the wife.

"Right." Hidaka squared the sheets, slid them into the satchel between two
untouched copies of Schedule B, and stood up, and his back informed him about the
eleven years. "Sorry to have been so long. It's the annex. It's the annex
everywhere, I'm running an hour behind since the first house."

"That's all right," said Ren, already moving to see him out.

At the door Hidaka stopped, because he had remembered, and because he had a
daughter of thirteen and knew exactly how little it cost.

"That's a good piece," he said to the boy. "That's a genuinely good piece. You
keep at that."

The boy went red all over again and said nothing at all.

The mother said, "Thank you," and it was the only thing she had said all afternoon
that had anything in it, and Hidaka went out into the rain feeling rather pleased
with the whole business.

---

He did two more and got home at the eighth hour with his back gone and six fish
that had been in a bag with two hundred forms since ten in the morning.

"You can't eat those," said his wife.

"Ozaki gave me them."

"You can't *eat* those, Hidaka."

"No," he agreed, and sat down, slowly, in the chair, and let his daughter take his
boots off because she liked doing it and he had stopped pretending he did not
need her to.

"Long one?"

"Eight households. Got through eight." He shut his eyes. "There's a new annex.
It's on a sheet the same size as the old one, which I did mention, and nobody's
going to be able to read a word of the ninth column by the time it gets back to
the office."

"Mm."

"Nice family in the lower district. Crystal people." He found that he wanted to
tell her about it and could not think of a way to make it sound like anything. "The
little lad grows it. Grew a thing over two nights, which apparently you can't do."

"Can't you?"

"Apparently not." He shifted, and his back caught him, and he made the noise he
made. "I put a note on. He'll get looked at properly one of these days. Good for
him."

"That's nice," said his wife, who was reading.

The forms went in on the Friday, all forty-one of them, and were checked and
counter-signed and bundled by district, and the trade returns with a Schedule B
attached were separated out from the rest, because Schedule B was collated
centrally rather than held at district — which Hidaka knew, because it said so in
the circular, and which he had not thought about at all, because collation was
not his department and never had been.
