# Chapter 71, Scene 3 — The Emissary

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the tower meeting room. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1020 words.*

---

Hyūga Hinata arrived for the Monday appointment four minutes
early, bowed at precisely the depth the meeting warranted, sat
where the protocol placed her, and produced, from a case, a
clan proposal so thoroughly prepared that his first professional
response was the one he usually reserved for good masonry.

His second response, running underneath on the instrument that
never shut, was the read, and the read was the most interesting
he had taken since a tea house in the summer.

*Heir of the village's oldest dōjutsu clan. Nineteen. The file
says: gentle fist, close range, acrobatic doctrine — the exact
load case Akimoto's spec was written for. The person says: a
gaze that has been trained since infancy to see everything and
permission-trained just as long to admit none of it. Hands
folded to a discipline. A voice pitched a fraction under its
own strength — not weakness; MUTING. Somebody who learned
early that her full volume cost somebody something.*

*And under all of it, the thing I know from the inside because
I wore it for three years in this village: a person executing
a prepared script because the script is the bridge over the
part of them that would otherwise not manage the crossing.*

"The clan's position," said Hinata, to her notes, carefully,
"is that the Hime line has — that the demonstrated benefit to
active kunoichi is of interest to the Hyūga, whose training
doctrine places particular demands on — " a breath, the script
holding — "on the practitioners. We would propose a standing
order: initial cohort of eleven, fittings conducted within
the compound by the board's own protocol officers, clan
seamstresses to be trained in maintenance under licence, and—"
she turned the page, and here the script steadied into
something with real iron in it, because it had stopped being
about her— "and a hardship provision. Four of the eleven are
branch-house kunoichi whose stipends would not cover bespoke
work. The main house will carry their cost, and the invoices
are to show it as standard issue. They are not to know. That
term is not negotiable, and it is mine, not the elders'."

He looked at her for a moment.

"Accepted before the rest," said Sazare, and wrote it first,
and watched the heir of the Hyūga look briefly, privately
pleased in the way of people who have spent a preparation's
worth of courage on one clause and seen it land.

The rest was craft, and inside the craft she changed. He had
watched it happen in Tachibana and in the matron and in every
specification holder the line had touched: the moment the
subject became load paths and doctrine rather than herself,
the muting lifted. She talked rotation — the full-body spin
of her clan's signature defence, which no garment in the
five nations had ever been engineered to survive — with her
hands describing arcs, fluent, exact. She flagged, unasked,
the one constraint nobody outside her clan would have known
to state: nothing in the articles that pooled chakra in
patterns, "because we — a Byakugan reads chakra the way you
read print, Sazare-san, and a garment that reads as a seal
array under a teammate's dōjutsu would be — it would raise
questions in the compound that the wearer should not have
to answer."

*Noted,* he wrote, *the articles run inert-passive, no
pooling, confirm with spec —* and underneath, in the file
that never closed: *a Byakugan reads chakra the way I read
print. Hold that sentence. It is load-bearing somewhere I
cannot see yet.*

---

Tsunade came through at the half hour, because a first
clan-to-line agreement touched the office and the office was
thorough.

She came in as the Hokage, entirely: reviewed the terms,
approved the hardship clause with one down-stroke and a
glance at Hinata that carried a whole paragraph of respect,
asked two questions about licensing that saved the village
money, and was — for eleven minutes — the fifth Hokage of
the Land of Fire conducting clan business with the heir of
the Hyūga, formal as architecture.

And then, leaving, at the door, with the meeting concluded
and the room already relaxing its grammar, she paused behind
his chair, and put her hand on his shoulder, and said,
"Good terms, fabricator."

Three words. The hand a half-second longer than furniture.
The register — he heard it the moment it left her, the way
you hear your own casting ring wrong — the register was the
second-floor one, the one with the lamplight in it, its
weight unmistakable to anybody with an instrument for
weight, deployed at one-quarter volume in a meeting room at
noon because the war had made them rich and the rich get
careless.

The door closed behind her.

The meeting resumed. The licensing schedule was agreed, the
cohort dates set, the case repacked with the same quiet
precision it had arrived with, and Hyūga Hinata bowed her
leaving bow at precisely the depth the meeting warranted,
and said her courtesies, and was gone — and nothing in any
of it, not one grain, disturbed the surface of the
afternoon.

*Meeting concluded,* he entered. *Terms excellent. The
emissary is the best-prepared negotiator the line has
faced, the hardship clause is the finest term anybody has
brought to it, and the rotation problem is the most
interesting engineering commission since the ovals.*

That was the whole of the entry. The finest observational
instrument in the Land of Fire, restored to full function,
running at peace — and pointed, that noon, entirely at
specifications.

He had missed it.

For the first time since a wall in Kiri taught him what
watching was for, somebody had sat across a table and read
him — read *them*, whole, in one pass, at survey grade,
through a half-second of hand and three words in a wrong
register — and he had not felt the survey land.

The Hyūga see a great deal. He would have the sentence by
Wednesday, from the source, with a kindness attached that he
had not earned.

He did not have it yet. The afternoon proceeded, sunlit,
excellent, and undisturbed, the way afternoons do when the
reader is very good.
