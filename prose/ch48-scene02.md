# Chapter 48, Scene 2 — Four Minutes and Some Floor

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the Hokage's tower. Tsunade, age fifty-three.
POV: Tsunade. ~1,947 words.*

---

She stood in the corridor outside the room at the end and made herself
do the thing she made her students do, which was to say the situation
out loud in four sentences before acting on it.

*Eighteen-year-old male. Petrification, left hand, distal to
mid-metacarpal, staggered, thumb spared. Decelerating, rate unknown,
endpoint unknown. Contact with a saturated Mokuton artefact
approximately fifty minutes ago.*

She got that far and stopped, because the fifth sentence was *and I do
not know what to do*, and she had not put that sentence in her own
mouth in a very long time.

She stood in a corridor in her shirt with her hair down and counted how
long.

It came out at eleven years, which was a lie, and she made herself do
it again properly, and the honest answer was that she had not been in
front of a thing she could not name since she was thirty-four and had
had a man's chest open in a field station and had known perfectly well
what was wrong with him and had simply been unable to stop it, which
was a completely different animal and did not count.

*Not since I was young.*

*Not since before I stopped.*

She put both hands over her face for a second and then took them off,
because the boy in that room could hear a fault in a piece of stone
through nine inches of air and she was not going to give him a sigh
through a door.

---

There was the other thing and she was not going to be able to leave it
in the corridor either.

*I came down the stair at a run.*

She had been at the window on the second floor with a border commission
report she had read four times and not taken in, and a guard had said a
name up the stair, and she had been on the outside steps in her shirt
before any part of her had produced a sentence.

Forty years of triage. She had walked to arterial bleeds. She had
finished a cup of tea while a chūnin held his own forearm together in
the doorway, because walking is faster than running when the running
makes your hands shake, and she had taught that to nine hundred
students in four countries and had believed it since before most of
them were born.

*I ran.*

*I ran, in front of a guard who will tell four people, for a boy with
no pain and a pulse of eighty-eight, and there was no clinical
justification for any part of it and I am not going to be able to
construct one.*

She stood in the corridor for another four seconds with that.

Then she put it where she put things, which was in the same place as
the rest of it, and went back in.

---

"Before I do anything," she said, "I need one answer and I need it
without any construction. Who told you about natural energy?"

The boy looked up.

He was sitting exactly where she had left him with the stone hand flat
on the table and his good hand in his lap, and he had not moved, and he
had not taken a reading, and she registered both of those and put them
away for later.

"Nobody," he said.

"Ruri—"

"Nobody told me. Nobody has ever said the words to me. I got the term
out of an Academy examination paper at seventeen, one paragraph, and it
said natural energy exists and is not usable and I copied it out for a
mark and forgot it for a year." He said it evenly. "Everything else I
built. Reject rates by medium in Kanzaki's ledger. A splint paper from
thirty-one years ago. Weighing a rod before and after. Weighing a
Wood Release user on a sack scale. A rose that feels like my own work
and shouldn't."

"You derived it."

"I derived that there's something, and that it's in the ground, and
that it's thicker under old cedars than under a burnt street." He
looked at his hand. "I did not derive that it would do this. I didn't
know there was anything to derive. My protocol has a risk line and it
says *none identified* and I underlined it."

"Say what you actually did. Not what you were touching — what you
*did*."

"I copied him."

Tsunade shut her eyes.

"I held a finished article and I matched the operation, because that is
the only way I have ever learned anything in my life and I have not got
another one." He said it without any defence in it. "It took four
minutes and it was easy. That's the part I'd want on the record if
somebody asked me tomorrow. Not that it worked — that it was *easy*.
It's the same operation. It's the identical operation with a different
thing on the end of it."

Tsunade stood in the middle of the room.

*Right.*

*That changes it and it changes it entirely and I want four minutes to
think about it and I have not got four minutes.*

Because if somebody had told him, then this was a security problem
wearing a medical coat, and there was a person somewhere who had put a
teenager in front of a thing that petrifies people, and she would find
them and there would be a conversation.

And if he had derived it — from a ledger and a rejected splint study
and a flower — then nobody had put him in front of anything.

*He walked here himself, and he will walk here again.*

*And there is an inch of green wood in a slip of paper in his coat with
the time written on the outside, and it is the first piece of physical
evidence in the history of this profession that a kekkei genkai is a
technique, and it was produced by an eighteen-year-old fabricator in a
rented room in about four minutes because he had a specimen to copy.*

*I take the hand off tonight and it is clean and it is safe and he is
back in that yard inside a fortnight with one hand and the same
question, because the question is not in his hand.*

*Cutting is the correct answer to the injury and it is not an answer to
the man, and I have been in this job for two years and the entire
difference between the job and medicine is that the job requires me to
treat the second one.*

---

"Right," she said. "Two. I'm doing two. Sit still and don't watch my
hands, it's rude and it puts me off."

She went to the middle of the floor and pushed the stool out of the
way with her foot.

And then, with the seals half formed, she stopped.

---

She had summoned Katsuyu perhaps four hundred times.

Field stations. Two wars, one of them somebody else's. A hospital roof
in the rain. Nine occasions she did not think about and one she thought
about most weeks. She had put that slug across a battlefield in a
hundred pieces and had had her hold four surgeries together at once and
had never, in any of it, said thank you afterwards, because you do not
thank your own hand.

Forty years.

And in forty years she had never once asked her a question that was not
an instruction.

*Not once.*

She stood in the middle of a scrubbed floor with her hands half up and
did the arithmetic in about three seconds and did not enjoy any of it.

*Because a question would have been a conversation, and a conversation
with a creature out of the Shikkotsu Forest goes one place, and it goes
there in about four exchanges, and I have known that since I was
nineteen years old.*

*It goes to sages. And sages go to that forest. And that forest goes to
my grandfather, who walked into it and came out able to do a thing
nobody has been able to do since, and who is the reason there is a
village here, and who is the reason there is a name on me, and whose
face is on a rock I look at from my own window every single morning of
my life.*

*Forty years of not asking a slug a question so that I did not have to
have four exchanges about a man who has been dead for fifty years.*

She could hear the boy behind her not moving.

*And it has cost nothing at all, until eleven o'clock tonight, when it
turns out to be the entire content of the only thing that might work.*

She finished the seals.

---

Katsuyu arrived on the table at about the size of a large cat, which
was the size she came at when nobody was dying, and settled, and said,
in the voice that had never once in four hundred summonings been
anything other than mild:

"Tsunade-sama."

"Katsuyu."

"You are not injured." A pause. "You are also not in a hurry, which is
unusual, and you have not told me what you need, which has never
happened."

Tsunade found that she had folded her arms.

"I want to ask you something," she said.

"Yes?"

"Not an instruction. A question."

There was a silence of about two seconds, and Tsunade — who had spent
forty years around a creature she had never once wondered about — found
herself entirely unable to read it.

"Ah," said Katsuyu.

That was all. Just the syllable, quite softly, in a room with a lamp
and a scrubbed table and an eighteen-year-old sitting on a stool with a
stone hand.

Tsunade felt something go through her that she had no intention of
examining at eleven twenty at night.

"There is a boy behind me," she said, "with natural energy set in his
left hand, and my discipline does not apply to it, and I need to know
where it goes when a body cannot take it."

"You have never asked me about natural energy."

"No."

"In forty-one years."

"No," said Tsunade. "I haven't."

"You never asked, Tsunade-sama," said Katsuyu, without any weight on it
whatsoever, in the exact tone she used for *the fourth-floor ward is
holding* — and it went into Tsunade somewhere below the ribs and lodged
there and stayed lodged for a considerable time afterwards.

"I know."

"I am not reproaching you. I would not know how." A slow settling of
the mantle. "I am saying it because it is the answer to a question you
have not asked either, which is why you do not already know the thing
you are about to ask me. There was nothing preventing it. There has
never been anything preventing it."

---

Behind her, very quietly, Tsunade heard a notebook open.

She turned round.

The boy had it on his knee and a pencil in his good hand and he was
already writing, left-handed, badly, at speed, with an object on the
end of his other arm that was not tissue and was moving four
millimetres an hour towards his wrist.

He caught her looking.

"Sorry," he said. "Do you want me to stop?"

And Tsunade — who had come down a stair at a run, who had eleven years
of not knowing behind her and forty-one of not asking, who had been
standing in a corridor eight minutes earlier with both hands over her
face — heard herself make a sound that was not entirely a laugh and
was not entirely anything else.

"No," she said. "No. Write it down."
