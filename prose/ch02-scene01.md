# Chapter 2, Scene 1 — The Stall

*Act 1, beat 6. Sazare Ruri, age eight. POV: Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

The coin was Jirō's and had been obtained by a process Jirō would not describe,
which meant it had come out of his mother's jar, which meant they had about an
hour before this became a different sort of afternoon.

They spent it on the cutting, at the stall by the third bridge, because the woman
there did the fried things with the batter that came off in sheets.

"Two."

"Two each?"

"There isn't two each," said the woman, without looking at the coin. "There's
two."

"Two, then," said Jirō, with dignity, and they took them away to the wall and sat
on it with their heels knocking, and ate them too fast, and burned their mouths,
which was the entire point.

It was the second month and the fog had come inland and settled on the cutting so
that the far bank was a suggestion. The boats went past below them as noise. It
was the kind of afternoon that Sazare would later be unable to distinguish from
about four hundred others.

Then the two men on the bench along from them stopped talking.

They did not stop the way people stop when they've finished. They stopped in the
middle — one of them had been saying something about his brother-in-law — and then
they both looked at the same thing at the same time and then both looked away
from it at the same time, and picked the conversation back up about four seconds
later, at a volume noticeably lower than before.

Sazare turned round to see what it was.

It was a young man walking up the cutting.

He was perhaps seventeen, and he was very tall, and he was bare to the waist in
the second month with the fog on, which struck Sazare as more remarkable than
anything else about him. He had bandages wound round the lower half of his face,
the way men did when they had something wrong with the jaw, except that nothing
about the way he moved suggested anything was wrong with him anywhere. There was
something enormous slung across his back under a wrapping of oilcloth.

He came up the cutting at an unhurried pace, and the fog went round him, and about
a third of the people on the walkway found a reason to be on the other side of it.

He stopped at the stall.

The woman had his food ready before he had finished stopping. Sazare watched her
do it: she had it in her hand, she had turned, she had it wrapped, and she put it
down on the board and stepped back from it, all in about the time it took the
young man to lift his chin at her once.

He put money on the board. She did not touch the money.

He picked up the food and pulled the bandages down under his chin to eat, and
turned round, and leaned on the rail with his back to the stall about six feet
from where they were sitting.

Jirō had gone completely still.

Sazare, who was still turned round on the wall with his mouth full, found that
he was looking at the side of the man's face from a distance of two arm-spans,
and that there was nothing whatever remarkable about it. He looked like anyone
from the harbour. He looked tired. He had a small pale scar across the bridge of
his nose that had healed badly and a way of eating that was entirely
businesslike, and he was watching the boats the way anybody watches the boats.

Then he looked round.

He looked at Sazare, and at Jirō, and at the wall they were sitting on, for about
as long as it takes to check whether a thing needs any attention.

Then he looked back at the boats.

That was all of it. It lasted a second and a half. Nothing happened. Sazare
turned back round to face the water with his ears ringing, and neither of them
said anything at all until the man had finished, and pulled the bandages up, and
gone on up the cutting, and been swallowed by the fog somewhere past the fourth
bridge.

The two men on the bench got louder again.

---

"That's Zabuza," said Jirō.

"Who's Zabuza?"

"*Zabuza*." Jirō looked at him with real pity. "Momochi. You don't know Zabuza?"

"Obviously I don't know Zabuza."

"He killed a hundred people."

Sazare stopped with the last of the fried thing halfway to his mouth.

"He never."

"He did. In one day." Jirō had gone up onto his knees on the wall, which he did
when a thing was important. "Right — right, so. There used to be a *test*, yeah?
For the Academy. At the end. To get out."

"There's a test now."

"Not that test. The *old* test." Jirō dropped his voice, and it was the drop that
did it, more than the words. "The old test was they put you all in a room. All of
you. Your whole year. And you had to fight. And you only got out if — you know."

"If what?"

"If you were the one left."

Sazare put the fried thing down on the wall.

"That's not true."

"It's true. Ask anyone. Ask your dad." Jirō shrugged, up on his knees, delighted
and appalled in the way of an eight-year-old with a genuinely good horror. "And
Zabuza wasn't even in it. He wasn't even *at* the Academy. He just came in off the
street on the day and killed everyone in the room and walked out. A hundred of
them. And they couldn't do anything about it because he hadn't broken any rules,
because there weren't any rules, that was the whole *point* of it—"

"How old was he?"

"Dunno. Little." Jirō thought about it. "Our age? Bit older?"

They both looked up the cutting to where he had gone.

"Why isn't he in prison?" said Sazare.

Jirō opened his mouth and then closed it again, because he did not have an answer
and had not, until that second, noticed that he did not have one.

"Because he's a shinobi," he said eventually.

"So's my sister."

"Yeah, but he's a—" Jirō made a gesture that meant *a bigger sort*. "He's one of
the Seven. Or he's going to be. My brother says he's going to be."

"But he *killed a hundred people*."

"He killed a hundred *students*," said Jirō, and heard himself say it, and looked
faintly ill, and got down off his knees.

They sat there for a bit.

"Anyway they stopped the test after that," Jirō said, to the water.

"Because of what he did?"

"Yeah."

"So he—" Sazare worked at it. It would not come out right in his head no matter
which way he turned it. "So the test was bad. And everyone knew the test was bad.
And nothing happened until he did that, and then it stopped."

"S'pose."

"That's stupid."

"Yeah," said Jirō, with feeling. "It is stupid." Then, after a moment, with the
resilience of his entire species: "D'you want to go and see if the eel's still
under the third bridge?"

They went and looked at the eel.

---

Sazare asked Mizuho about it the following week, in the small gap between the
end of the morning and the start of the afternoon, when she was cleaning the
slate wall and there was nobody else in.

He had thought about how to do it for four days and had arrived at the direct
approach, which was the only approach he had.

"Is it true about the old test?"

Mizuho did not stop cleaning the wall.

"Which old test?"

"The Academy one. The one where—"

"Yes," she said. "It's true."

"And a boy called Zabuza—"

"Sazare."

He stopped.

Mizuho finished the corner she was on, and turned round, and folded the cloth in
half and then in half again, and looked at him with an expression he could not
read at all, which was unusual, because he had spent a year finding her extremely
easy to read.

"It's true," she said. "All of it's true. It was before you were born and it isn't
how it is now and there is not one single thing about it that will be of any use
to you." She put the cloth down. "How's the water coming?"

"I did four minutes."

"Four?"

"Three and a bit," Sazare admitted. "But it was nearly four."

"Then it was three and a bit," said Mizuho. "Don't round up. Nobody who rounds up
ever gets past six." She turned back to the wall. "Go and eat something."

He went and ate something.

He noticed — he could not help noticing, being the sort of child he was — that she
had answered the question about the test, straight away and without any fuss at
all, and that she had never once said the name.
