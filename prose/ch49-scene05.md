# Chapter 49, Scene 5 — Two Things, Not One

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard, after hours. Sazare
Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,067 words.*

---

They started on the Saturday and the first four attempts produced
nothing at all, which he had expected and which was still worse than he
had expected.

The problem was stated on the first sheet and did not change for four
days.

*I cannot stop a reflex I cannot detect.*

*I have no report of the close. None. It happens at the end of every
course and it has happened nine hundred thousand times and I have never
in my life felt it, and asking me to not do it is like asking me not to
blink at a thing coming at my eye — except that I know when I have
blinked, and I do not know when I have done this.*

*So the first problem is not stopping it. The first problem is finding
out that it has happened.*

So they did the only thing available, which was that he laid a course
and Katsuyu said *now*.

He laid nine hundred courses over two days and she said *now* nine
hundred times, at the same point every time, in the same mild voice,
and by the end of the second day he had begun — faintly, unreliably,
about one time in four — to feel something a fraction before she
spoke.

*This is how a person learns a thing they have no sense for. Somebody
outside tells them when, over and over, until the inside catches up.*

*It is exactly what Yagi did to me with pulses when I was fifteen. She
put my fingers on a wrist and said "there" for eleven days and on the
twelfth I had it and have never lost it.*

*Nobody has ever been able to do this for me with crystal because
nobody has ever been able to see it.*

---

Kanzaki found them in the yard at eleven on the Sunday night and did
not ask what they were doing.

She brought out a stool for the felt, which was a thing she had never
done in three years, and put it at the end of the bench, and put a
lamp on it, and said, "That's better for her, she's not got eyes and I
don't know how she's managing but the shadow can't be helping."

Then she went in.

"That was extremely kind," said Katsuyu.

"She's like that," said Sazare. "She'll be unpleasant about it for a
week now to make up for it."

---

She gave him the thing on the Sunday, at about the four hundredth
course, and she gave it in the middle of a sentence about something
else, which was how she gave everything.

"It is two things," she said.

"What is?"

"The close. You have been calling it the close and I have been saying
*now* and I have been getting slightly wrong for two days, and I have
only just worked out why, and I am sorry, because I think it is the
whole of what you are asking me." She settled on the felt. "It is two
things. They happen about a tenth of a second apart, or less — I am
poor with intervals — and the first one is always first, and I have
never seen them separated."

Sazare put his hand flat on the bench.

"Say what the two things are."

"The first is that you *let go of it*." Katsuyu's mantle shifted, which
he had learned meant she was hunting. "The connection between you and
the object ends. That is one act and it is quite abrupt.

"And the second is that the object *shuts*. It stops being open to
anything. That is a different act and it happens to the object rather
than to you, and it happens fractionally afterwards, and it is —
forgive me — considerably louder, from where I am, than the first one."

The yard was very quiet.

"They're not the same act," said Sazare.

"They are not the same act."

"I have thought they were one act for eleven years because they have
never once happened separately, and there has never been anybody in the
room to tell me there were two." He was writing now and his hand had
gone bad with it. "That's — Katsuyu, that's the whole problem. That's
the entire problem. I've been trying to work out how to not close a
thing while letting go of it and I have been assuming that is a single
operation with a switch on it, and it *isn't*, it's two operations that
have been welded together by habit since I was seven, and all I have to
do is—"

"All you have to do," said Katsuyu, "is a thing you have never done in
your life, in the dark, with no report, at speed."

"Yes."

"I only mention it because you said *all*."

---

The Monday was bad and the Tuesday was worse.

He got the first one on Monday afternoon — he could let go, deliberately,
as a separate act, and feel himself do it, and it was an extraordinary
sensation and it lasted about four minutes before he lost it again.

But the second one came anyway, every time. He would let go, aware of
letting go, pleased with himself for being aware of letting go, and a
fraction later Katsuyu would say "and it has shut," and it had.

Attempt sixty-one on Tuesday he got it in the wrong order and produced
an article that was sealed and still connected to him, which was a
sensation he did not care for and which he wrote up as *not dangerous
and profoundly unpleasant, like a room with the door locked from the
inside by somebody else.*

Attempt sixty-eight the article shut before he had finished laying it
and cracked from the inside out.

Attempt seventy-one he was tired and did four things wrong at once and
Katsuyu said, in the ordinary mild voice with nothing at all changed in
it:

"Stop. Now. Both hands off."

He stopped and took both hands off.

There was a pause of about six seconds during which nothing whatsoever
happened.

"...What was that."

"It was open and you were still on it," said Katsuyu, "and it had begun
to draw, and it was drawing through you, and you had not noticed
because you were concentrating on the wrong end. It was very small. It
was perhaps four seconds from being the fifteenth."

Sazare looked at his left hand, which had a patch on the heel of it the
size of a large coin and would for the rest of his life.

"How long had it been going."

"About two seconds."

"And how long had you had it?"

"About one and a half," said Katsuyu, "and I said stop as soon as I was
sure, and I have been sitting here since wondering whether I should
have said it at one instead of one and a half, and I think the answer
is that I should, and I will next time."

He sat down on the yard stone.

"Tsunade-sama set a term," he said. "On the sixteenth, at about half
past seven in the morning, on no sleep. She said: you don't do it
alone."

"I know. She told me."

"She told you?"

"On Tuesday, before she put her coat on," said Katsuyu. "She said: he
will do it anyway, so please be there, and please stop him early rather
than late, and please do not wait to be certain. And then she went and
argued about a border."

---

He got it on the Tuesday night at about half past one, on the
hundred-and-eleventh attempt of the four days, and it was quiet and
undramatic and over in about six seconds.

He laid the rose.

It took nine minutes, which was slow, because it was a rose and he was
holding the whole geometry of a specific object with a crooked third
thorn while doing something with his hands that he had learned to feel
about a hundred hours ago, and by the end of it his forearm had gone.

Then he let go — the first act, deliberately, cleanly, aware of it.

And did not do the second.

That was all. There was no effort in the not-doing. It was, he wrote
afterwards, *like deliberately not saying a word you were about to say:
the difficulty is entirely in the fraction of a second before, and once
it has gone past there is nothing there at all.*

He took his hands away.

"Well?" he said.

"It is drinking," said Katsuyu.

---

He put his head down on the bench for a moment, which he had never done
in a working yard in his life.

Then he looked at it.

Nine inches of crystal on a felt. Two leaf pairs, four thorns, the
third one crooked, the brown mark at the margin of the second leaf
rendered as a faint clouding in the lattice because he had copied it
without deciding to and his hands did not know how to leave a fault
out. A bud about a third open.

Colourless. Translucent all the way through, with the lamp on the far
side of it going into the body of the thing and coming out somewhere it
had no business coming out of.

And it was singing.

Very quietly — at the edge, the way the sword had been on the
twenty-eighth night, and he had to get his head down to about nine
inches to have it at all. One voice. Steady. No decay.

Nothing was touching it.

"It doesn't stop," he said.

"No."

"It's been four minutes and there's nothing on it and it hasn't
stopped." He was writing left-handed and badly and could not read his
own hand afterwards for two of the lines. "Every article I have made
since I was seven goes quiet the instant I take my hand off. That's
what a mouthful is. This has nothing in it but what it is taking, right
now, out of a yard, and it will go on taking it."

"Yes."

"How much?"

"Very little," said Katsuyu. "About what a small plant would take. Less
than the persimmon outside your window, considerably less than the
cedars at the wall. I would not be able to find it in a week if I did
not know where to look."

"And it doesn't run out."

"I do not see how it could," said Katsuyu, "any more than a root runs
out. It is standing in the thing it is made of."

---

He sat in a yard at two in the morning with a flower that hummed.

*Article: open. Test piece, no function, 9 in., crystal, drawing.*

*One hundred and eleven attempts. Four days. One near-miss at
seventy-one stopped at approximately one and a half seconds by an
observer who has since concluded she should have stopped it at one.*

*It does not degrade. It cannot degrade. It has no store to run down —
it is not a jar with a lid, it is a root standing in a field, and the
field is everywhere and it is what everything is made of and I have
been standing in it since I was seven years old with the lid on.*

He stopped.

*Six days is over.*

*Not solved. Over. That number was never a fact about crystal. It was a
fact about a habit I acquired before I could read and have not once
examined, and it has been on every label I have ever written and in
every entry in Kanzaki's ledger and in a degradation study that a man
four hundred miles away read and wrote to me about, and it was never
true of anything except the way I happened to do it.*

He looked at the rose.

*And I cannot put this near a patient, or a ward, or another person,
because it is open in both directions and she said so before I could
get excited about it and made me say it back.*

*So it has no use whatsoever.*

*It is nine inches of the first permanent object anybody in my family
has made in three hundred years and its entire function is that it
exists.*

He sat there a while longer.

Then, at about ten past two, in a yard, to a slug:

"How do I give this to her?"
