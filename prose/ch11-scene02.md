# Chapter 11, Scene 2 — Lamps on the Water

*Act 2, part C. Sazare Ruri, age fourteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,200 words.*

---

Tose died in the tenth month, in her sleep, in the room she had been born in
above the old bathhouse, and the first Sazare knew of it was Tōan taking his
coat off the peg at first light and saying, "Bring the case. Not the trauma
roll. The other one."

The other one was a flat lacquered box that Sazare had cleaned monthly and
never seen opened. It held combs, and cloths of a fineness the house
otherwise had no use for, and a stoppered flask, and cords of plain white
braid, and he understood what trade they were doing this morning about
halfway down the hill, and his hands went cold on the handle, and he kept
walking.

"Her heart," said Tōan, on the road. "It's been finishing for a year. She
knew. We had an arrangement — no needles, no fuss, and I was to see to her
myself after, because the new bath keeper's a good girl and squeamish, and
Tose said she hadn't spent fifty years running the cleanest house in the
valley to be sent off by somebody who'd make a face."

He said all of it in the dosage voice, and then he said, in a different one:

"You can wait on the stair. It's work you should see done before you're
ever asked to do it, and it's work nobody should be made to see. Both are
true. You choose on the landing."

Sazare chose the room.

And afterwards he could never make anyone understand — there was never
anyone to try to make understand — that it was the gentlest thing he had
ever watched done. The washing, the dressing, the small offices, Tōan's
enormous forearms moving with the exact unhurried economy they brought to
everything, and the whole of it conducted as though Tose were not gone but
merely, for once in her life, not talking; he handled her the way Sazare's
mother had handled finished work: as a thing whose worth was settled, that
would not be improved by anything now except care.

At the end Tōan stood a moment with his hand flat on the folded cloths,
which was, in the whole time anyone in that valley had known him, the entire
recorded extent of the man's ceremony, and then he opened the shutters
himself, both sides, wide, and the steam of the town came in on the morning
air, and that was that.

"Why the shutters?" Sazare asked, on the stairs.

"Custom, here. The house breathes out." Tōan shouldered the case. "In our
country they'd have sealed the room."

He said *our country* — the first time in two years he had put himself and
Sazare inside one boundary — and said nothing else the whole way home.

---

The wake was three days later, and it was the town entire, and it was
nothing whatever like grief as Sazare understood the technology of it.

Grief where he came from was a shut stall. It was washing left on a line,
and a chain on a door, and a name you did not say in a queue, and a black
wall of chalk where hope was rationed by the season. He had been raised —
he understood this now, standing in a bathhouse yard packed with lamplight
and food and noise — inside a country where the dead were a category of
information, and information was a category of risk.

Here they said her name so often it stopped sounding like a name.

They told stories *against* her, which scandalised him for a quarter of an
hour until he caught the mechanism: the meaner the story, the more love it
carried, and the best of them — Tose repricing a daimyō's retainer's bath
because "armour rusts the pipes"; Tose barring a famous actor for singing
flat *in her water*; Tose, at seventy, wading in fully dressed to separate
two fighting miners with a boot-brush — were delivered by old women exactly
as merciless as she had been, to shouts of joy, six feet from where she
lay.

And at dusk they floated the lamps.

The whole town went down to the river with a paper lamp apiece, and set
them on the warm water among the skeins of steam, and the current took
them down the gorge in a long turning procession of lights — hundreds of
them, going away, unhurried, through the standing white — and nobody wept
much, and nobody stopped talking, and a very small child fell in at the
shallow step and was retrieved, steaming, to general satisfaction.

Sazare stood on the bank with a lamp in his hands.

It was a crowd, and it was all facing one way, and his body, tested,
reported — nothing. He stood inside four hundred people all turned toward
the same lights and was only a person at a river. He examined that from a
small distance, the way he examined everything, and could find only one
difference that mattered: nothing on that water was going to be done *to*
anybody. Four hundred people facing the same direction to give something
away.

The new bath keeper's mother, beside him, said: "Did you know her, love?"

He had been asked that question, in one form or another, at intervals, all
his life, about everyone, and had answered it his whole life the same way:
*I make earrings.* Some version. Some hollow thing that fit the hole and
held nothing.

"She held the fourth pool forty minutes past closing every eighth day,"
he said, "so the night miners could soak the dust off. She docked herself
the firewood for it in her own books, fifty years, so nobody could ever
say she'd given anybody anything." He set his lamp on the water and let
the current find it. "She told me the ground here is the kindest thing in
the country. And she thought my hands were wasted."

"On the doctor?"

"On everything," said Sazare. "She never said on what they wouldn't be."

The woman laughed, and cried a little, in the easy way the whole town
seemed able to do both at once, and patted his arm, and moved on along
the bank; and Sazare stood in the steam and the lamplight and understood
that he had just spoken of a dead person, out loud, in the past tense,
plainly, and that the sky had not come down, and that somewhere very far
underneath him, in the sealed rooms, eight lamps he had never been allowed
to light were still standing in the dark, waiting.

Not yet.

But he stayed on the bank until Tose's fleet of lights had gone all the way
down the gorge and out of sight, every one of them, and he was among the
last to leave, and he walked home along the warm river through the steam
with his hands quiet in his sleeves, thinking, with great care, about
nothing at all.

---

That night, for the first time, the dream changed.

The platform. The flat hand. The rearranging — his mother moved along by a
pace, third, always third, and the order never right and no voice in him
to call it — all of that came as it always came.

But this time, in the dream, the square had a river in it.

It ran where the barrier had been, warm, steaming, between him and the
platform, and on it there were lamps — hundreds, turning, going down
through the crowd like the crowd was a gorge — and the executioner
paused, in the dream, with his flat hand out, because he could not
arrange anything while the lamps were passing. Nobody could. That was
suddenly, in the way of dreams, the oldest law there was: everything
waits while the lamps go by.

They went by for a long time.

Sazare woke at the third hour as always — but slowly, this once, like a
lamp coming to the end of its wick rather than a rope jerked — and lay in
the warm listening to the river run under the house, and did not need the
sound of the scales, and slept again before he had finished being awake.

It was not a mending. He knew his trade now well enough to know what it
was: the body doing it right, for one night, slowly, because for once
nothing had panicked and something had been fed.

The hand came back the next week, and the week after. It always came
back.

But the river had been in the square once now, and what has been in a
room once can be let in again — that was Tōan's whole method, said over
a miner's crushed fingers a year ago, *the body knows how, it has only
to be reminded* — and Sazare turned fifteen in the eleventh month,
quietly, unremarked, in a country that was teaching him, fortnight by
fortnight, lamp by lamp, in a language nobody official had ever spoken
to him, all the things a person's dead might be besides a list.
