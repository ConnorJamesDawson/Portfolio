# Chapter 6, Scene 3 — Ninety Seconds

*Act 1, beat 16. Sazare Ruri, age twelve. POV: Sazare. ~2,600 words.*

---

The door came open about a hand's width and a woman looked out at him.

She was perhaps thirty and had a lamp behind her so he could not see her face
properly, and she looked at him for slightly less than a second and then past him,
up the jetty, along the water, and at the roofline of the sheds opposite, and then
back down at him.

"No," she said.

"I've got—"

"No." She started to close the door.

Sazare put the blue lozenge into the gap.

The door stopped.

There was a pause of about four seconds, and then the woman opened it again and
took the crystal off him and turned it over twice, and something in the way she was
standing changed entirely.

"Where did you get this."

"Off the post."

"Which post?"

"That one." He pointed. "At the end. It was just sat on it."

"On top of it."

"On top of it."

"*Hell*," said the woman, with feeling, and put her head out and looked at the
post, and then at him again, and then she said, "How long have you been walking?"

"Since the eighth hour."

"Which eighth hour?"

"Last night's."

She looked at him for a while.

"Come in out of it," she said. "Don't touch anything."

---

There were nine people in the boat shed and every one of them stopped what they
were doing when he came in.

Sazare had spent his entire life in rooms where he was the least important person
present and had never minded it, and this was not that. This was nine adults
looking at a twelve-year-old and doing arithmetic.

"He came up the jetty," said the woman.

"We saw."

"He had the marker off the post."

"We *saw*."

A man near the stove — big, forty-odd, with a plank across two crates for a table
and charts on it — put down what he was holding.

"Whose is he?"

"Ruri," said Sazare.

The room's temperature changed. He could not have described how. Two people looked
at each other. Somebody at the back said something short that he did not catch.

"Kohaku's brother," said the man at the stove. It was not a question.

"Yes."

"Right." The man came round the plank table. He was not unkind; he had the manner
of somebody who had been up for two days and was allocating what was left of
himself carefully. "Sit down. Have that." He put a heel of bread in Sazare's
hands. "Eat it. Then you're going home."

"Where's my sister?"

"Eat the bread."

"*Where's my sister?*"

"Eat the bread," said the man, "and then we'll talk about it, and I'll tell you the
truth, which you won't like. Sit down."

Sazare sat down and ate the bread. It was the best thing he had ever eaten in his
life and he was ashamed of that for years.

---

"We don't know," said the man.

His name was Ryōsuke, or that was the name he gave. He had sat down on a crate
opposite with his elbows on his knees.

"That's the truth and it's all of it. She went out on the eleventh and she was due
back on the nineteenth and she didn't come, and that's twenty-nine days. We've had
nothing. Not a mark, not a word, not a body." He said the last one the way you say
a thing you have made yourself get used to saying. "We've asked. There's four
people asking and one of them is very good. If she's held anywhere we'd expect to
know inside a fortnight and it's been four weeks."

"So she's—"

"So we don't know," said Ryōsuke.

Sazare looked at the floor of the boat shed. It was very clean. Somebody had swept
it, at some point, in the last day, which struck him as an insane thing for
anybody to have bothered doing.

"I want to help," he said.

"I know you do."

"No — I'm not — I don't mean I want to *look for her*, I know I can't do that." He
had his fists on his knees. "I mean I want to *do* it. The thing you do. Whatever
she was doing. I've been doing bits of it for four years and nobody told me, and I
worked out the marks by myself, tonight, off the pegs at the chandler's, and I came
across the whole village and nobody stopped me *once*—"

"How old are you?"

"Twelve."

"You've graduated?"

"I graduate in the spring."

"Then you haven't graduated," said Ryōsuke.

"That's four months—"

"Then come back in four months."

---

Sazare had rehearsed a certain amount of this on the walk without knowing he was
rehearsing it, and none of it had gone this way, and what came out of him was not
any of the things he had assembled.

"Test me," he said.

Somebody behind him made a noise.

"Test me. You must — you must have a way of testing people, when they come, you
must—" He stood up. "Test me and if I'm no good I'll go home and I won't come back
and I won't say a word to anybody for the rest of my life. But you can't just say
*four months* like it's a — like there's nothing to it—"

"Sazare," said Ryōsuke.

"*Test me.*"

Ryōsuke looked at him for a long moment, and then past him, at the woman who had
opened the door.

"Ninety seconds," he said.

"Ryō—"

"Ninety seconds. He walked eleven miles." He stood up. "Clear the floor."

---

She was called Tomi, and she was about thirty, and she was very tired, and she was
better than him by a margin he could not have measured with any instrument he
possessed.

It took about forty seconds.

He got one thing right, at the start, which was that he did not run at her, because
you did not run at people; he went sideways and low the way you were taught in the
second year. She let him do it. She let him do it entirely, and then she was not
where she had been, and his arm was up behind his shoulder blade and his face was
against the boards of the floor that somebody had swept, and it did not hurt in
the slightest, which was the worst part of the whole thing.

"All right," said Tomi.

"Get off—"

"I'm not on you." She wasn't. She had let go the instant he started talking, and
was standing about a pace back with her hands loose. "Get up. Try again if you
want."

He got up and tried again.

The second time took nine seconds.

The third time he did not get up straight away, and lay on the very clean floor of
the boat shed with his cheek on the boards and his eyes stinging, and there was
absolute silence in the room and nobody said anything at all until he got himself
up onto one knee.

"Right," said Ryōsuke. "Sit down."

---

"That's not—" Sazare's voice had gone. He got it back. "That's not fair. That's
not what I'm *for*. She's a — she's a *jōnin*, or whatever she is, I'm eleven —
I'm twelve — I can do nine minutes of still water, I can do *fourteen*, nobody in
my year can do six—"

"I believe you."

"—and I found the pegs, and I did the whole village in a *night*, and none of that
counts because I can't fight a grown woman on a floor? What's the *point* of a
test if the test isn't—"

"The test wasn't for you," said Ryōsuke.

Sazare stopped.

"It was for me," said Ryōsuke. "I needed to see it before I said this, because I
didn't want to say it on a guess."

He sat back down on the crate.

"You're right," he said. "It doesn't count. You're better at the marks than half
this room and you found a live drop chain cold, at night, at twelve, which I have
never in my life heard of anybody doing, and if you were nineteen I'd have you
sworn in before breakfast."

"Then—"

"And I'm still sending you home, and it's got nothing to do with the floor." He
leaned forward. "It's this. If they take you — not if they beat you, if they
*take* you, if a man puts you in a room and shuts the door — how long do you last?"

Sazare opened his mouth.

"Don't," said Ryōsuke. "Don't answer fast. Think about it properly and answer me
honestly, because I'm going to know if you don't."

Sazare thought about it properly.

He thought about the cart with the box on it, and a boy with two red marks on his
forehead looking out at the walkway the way you look out of a window on a journey.
He thought about his mother saying *if anybody ever asks you what you can do, you
tell them you make earrings*, with her arm round him, and how he had said *all
right* and had not for one second imagined anybody actually asking.

"Not long," he said.

"No," said Ryōsuke. "Not long. Nor did I, at twelve, and nor did anybody else in
this shed, and the difference between us and you is nine years, and there is no
way to have those nine years faster." He put a hand briefly on Sazare's shoulder,
once, and took it away. "And she knew that. That's why you were carrying rubbish.
Every single thing that boy ever took anywhere was worthless, do you understand me?
Every one. She checked. She checked with me, twice, and the second time she was
extremely rude about it."

---

They put him out at the top of the jetty at about the fifth hour of the morning
with the sky going grey behind the saltworks.

"Four months," said Ryōsuke.

"You won't be here in four months."

Ryōsuke did not insult him by answering that.

"If we're not," he said, "then it'll be because it went badly, and you'll be very
glad you were in the Academy doing seven minutes of still water in front of an
examiner." He put his hands in his pockets. "Go home the way you came. Don't stop.
If anybody asks you—"

"I'm going to my aunt's at Nagihama," said Sazare. "I've gone the coast way because
the low road's under water."

Ryōsuke looked at him.

"Yeah," he said, in a slightly odd voice. "All right. Go on."

Sazare went home.

He got over the back wall at about the sixth hour and his mother was in the
workshop with her hands on frame two, and she did not turn round, and she did not
ask him one single question about where he had been all night, then or ever, and it
took him another eleven years to work out what that meant.
