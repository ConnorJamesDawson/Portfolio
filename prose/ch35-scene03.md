# Chapter 35, Scene 3 — Nine Seconds

*Act 4, part C. Konoha General, second floor. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,187 words.*

---

He went to find the correct person for a process problem, and the
correct person for a process problem in that building was Nurse Emiko,
because she had been running admissions for nineteen years and there
was no form in the Land of Fire she had not seen go wrong.

He found her at the desk at the end of the second-floor corridor at the
eleventh hour, doing the thing she did at the eleventh hour, which was
the bed state.

"Emiko."

"Mm." She did not look up. "Bed nine's for discharge and I want the
chair back off the fever ward before Thursday, they've had it a
fortnight."

"It's not about the ward."

Emiko put the pen down and looked up.

She was fifty-one now and had gone properly grey at the front in the
two years since the invasion, and she wore it the same way she had
always worn it, off her face and pinned, because it was practical.
She had a cardigan over the uniform from the first cold week of the
year to the last and it had two pens and a pair of bandage scissors in
the left pocket, always, and the pocket had been re-stitched twice
because of the scissors.

*Rung two: she carries her own scissors because the ward's walk off.*

*Rung three: she has never once complained about it and simply
replaced the pocket.*

"Go on," she said.

"There's a form. It's a reparations claim against a Kirigakure state
schedule and there's a claimant declaration on it that has to be in my
handwriting, and I've attempted it eleven times and I've not been able
to complete it, and I think the problem is that I don't know how the
relationship clause is meant to be stated, because it's foreign probate
and I've never—"

"Sit down," said Emiko.

"I'm—"

"Sit down, Ruri."

---

He sat down in the chair beside the desk, which was where relatives
sat.

Emiko did not say anything for a moment. She squared the bed state,
which did not need squaring, and put it under the corner of the blotter,
and turned her chair to face him.

"Three questions," she said. "Straight answers. You can refuse any of
them and I'll stop."

"All right."

"When did you last sleep a full night?"

He opened his mouth to say *I sleep adequately*, which was what he had
written in a notebook four nights earlier, and found that Emiko was
looking at him with the particular flat patience she used on people who
were about to tell her something that was true in a way designed to be
useless.

"The night before the delegation," he said.

"That's five weeks."

"I've slept. I've slept most nights. Not all the way through."

"Second question. How many times this fortnight have you come onto this
floor when you weren't rostered?"

Sazare did the arithmetic and did not want to.

"Nine."

"Eleven," said Emiko. "You've forgotten the two on the Sunday, and I'd
have taken nine as an answer if you'd been guessing, but you weren't
guessing, you were counting, and you were four out."

He said nothing.

"Third question." She did not change her tone at all. "What did you
drop on Tuesday?"

"...A kidney dish."

"And on Thursday?"

"That's a fourth question."

"It is. Answer it anyway."

"The obs board. On the stairs."

"You have worked on this floor for two and a half years," said Emiko,
"and until three weeks ago I had never once seen you drop anything. Not
one thing. There's an auxiliary three weeks in who's dropped four
items and I haven't said a word to her about it because she's three
weeks in and her hands are still learning the trays." She folded her
own hands. "Yours aren't."

---

"That's not — " Sazare stopped. "I came to ask about a form."

"I know what you came to ask about."

"The form's a real problem. The relationship clause is a genuine
technical question and I can't proceed without—"

"Ruri." Emiko leaned forward about four inches. "I have been a charge
nurse for nineteen years and I have had roughly nine hundred people sit
in that chair, and about a third of them opened with a problem that was
not the problem. That's not a criticism, it's just what people do. They
come in about the discharge paperwork and it's about the stairs at
home, or they come in about the visiting hours and it's about the fact
that nobody's visiting."

"I'm not a patient."

"No. You're the ward lead, and you have come to the admissions desk on
your own floor at the eleventh hour to ask me a question about
Kirigakure probate, which is not a thing I know anything whatsoever
about and which you know I know nothing about, because you have worked
next to me for two and a half years."

He had not thought about that.

He had genuinely not thought about it, and he sat in the relatives'
chair and turned it over and found that it was completely true — that
there were four people in that building who would have known more about
a foreign claim form than Emiko did, and that he could have named all
four of them, and that he had walked past two of them to get to her
desk.

"Nine seconds," he said.

"What?"

"You took about nine seconds. From when I started the sentence about
the form."

"Six or seven, probably." Emiko sat back. "That's not clever, love,
that's twenty years. Knowing which work needs a medic and which doesn't
is the entire job, and it's the same muscle for this, and you've been
watching me use it since your first week without noticing what it was."

---

She let him sit there for a while, which he later understood to have
been deliberate.

Then she stood up.

"Right," she said. "Here's what happens now, and I'm telling you rather
than asking you, because I've watched you make about four hundred
decisions on this floor and every single one of them was good and I'm
not going to hand you this one."

"Emiko—"

"You're not in trouble. Nobody's in trouble. Nothing's going in
writing." She took the two pens out of her cardigan pocket and put them
on the desk, which he had never seen her do. "This is above my line.
It's not a nursing matter and it's not a ward matter and it is very
definitely not a form, and the rule on this floor — your rule, in the
standing instructions, which you wrote — is that anything above your
line gets escalated the same day."

"That rule's about clinical—"

"It says *anything*." She was already moving. "Stay there. Don't do
observations, don't tidy anything, don't mend that trolley, and do not
go and hide in the sluice, which is what you did in the spring when
bed fourteen went and I have never mentioned it until this moment."

"Where are you going?"

"To get Yagi," said Emiko, from four feet down the corridor, without
turning round.
