# Chapter 13, Scene 3 — Weights and Measures

*Act 2, part D. Sazare Ruri, age fifteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,000 words.*

---

He paid the count on his last night in the valley, and he paid it in the
only coin that had ever meant anything in that house.

He had thought about it for the whole of the summer — what you leave a
man who keeps no ledger with names in it, who had fed him before touching
him, who had moved a bed nearer a stove in the second week and never once
in two years referred to it. Money was absurd; Tōan repriced other
people's money on principle. Words were worse. The trade of that house
ran on a different standard entirely, and it had taken Sazare most of a
season to see that the standard had been in front of him the whole time,
chiming, at three in the morning, four nights out of seven.

The good scales.

They were the finest thing Tōan owned and the truest thing in the house,
and their weights were brass, and brass wears. Fifty years of fingers had
rounded them; Tōan compensated by habit, a pinch here, a grain there, the
way a master compensates for a tool he loves too much to retire — and a
man who measured medicine for a living deserved, once in his life, to
measure against something that could not wear, could not tarnish, could
not lie.

So on the last night, after the rice, Sazare carried the tray in from
the porch rail and set it on the treatment bench in the good light, and
levelled the sand, and pinned back his sleeves.

"Four hours," he said. "If you want them."

Tōan looked at the tray, and at the boy, and understood — Sazare watched
him understand — exactly what was being offered: the thing behind the
door with no date on it; the family art, whole, unbutchered, in the slow
true form no living stranger had ever been given leave to watch. The
witnessing. The only heirloom the last Ruri had it in his gift to give.

He took the kettle off without a word, and pulled the stool to the end
of the bench, out of the light, where observers sat, and sat.

Sazare put his hands out, heels turned in, and found where it was
moving, and began.

He grew five weights, in graded sizes, through the middle of the night —
clear all through, tuned deliberately apart so no two could ever ring
each other, their masses called against the old brass standards Tōan
laid out one by one in the lamplight like a man dealing a hand he had
waited years to be shown. The frames' hum filled the treatment room,
one small voice of it, high and dry and steady; and steam went past the
shutters; and the river ran under the floor; and neither of them said
anything at all for four hours, because the house's first language had
always been work, and this was the longest sentence Sazare knew how to
say in it.

Near dawn he released the last weight from the sand, and passed it,
still blood-warm, down the bench.

Tōan sat turning it in the lamplight for a long time. Then he got up,
and set the five in their new row beside the scales, smallest to
largest, squared to the bench edge with one thick forefinger; and took
the worn brass weights, fifty years of his trade rounded soft, and
wrapped them in the polishing cloth, and put them not in the drawer but
on the shelf — the patients' shelf, the shelf of things with no names on
them, the shelf of what the house had mended and meant to keep.

"They'll outlast me," he said at last. "And the next man, and his next.
That's a strange gift to give a doctor, boy. We're taught not to want
things that outlast us."

"You weren't taught it well," said Sazare. "There's a woman up the second
valley whose whole family exists because of your hands. That outlasts
you. I've watched you build things that outlast you for two years.
The weights are just — " he looked for it, and found it, tradesman to
the end — "the weights are just the receipt."

Tōan stood with his back to the lamp, and his shoulders moved once, and
Sazare looked with great care at the scales, and the house's third law
held, gently, for the last time, over what neither of them was doing.

"The rice goes on at dawn," Tōan said finally, in the dosage voice,
somewhat thickened. "You'll eat before you go. A month at least."

It was the oldest joke the house owned — it was, in fact, the first
sentence the house had ever said to him — and Sazare laughed, at last,
properly, fifteen years old in a lamplit treatment room at four in the
morning, and the sound of it surprised them both, and neither of them
was sorry.

---

Hachirō took him down for the last time on the first of the month, with
the tenth and the tenth riding ahead of him in the lamp-oil manifest,
and the whole cart smelling of the fish-cloth Gonta had loaded past
arguing.

At the shrine they stopped, and Hachirō washed his face, and stood with
his cap off; and Sazare went up to the offering shelf through the steam.

The earring was still there.

Two years of weather had taken the shine off the clear end and pitted
nothing — grown work pitted at nobody's pleasure but its own — and
somebody, some hand in two years of passing hands, had moved it to sit
upright against the shrine's back wall, in the place of small honour,
between a child's clay horse and a coin worn blank; and around it the
season's offerings had accumulated the way they did, oranges and
wish-papers and a thimble-cup of spring water, the ordinary traffic of
other people's dead.

He did not touch it. It wasn't his. It had stopped being his the moment
it went on the shelf, which was the entire technology of the thing —
Tose's country's one great secret, that you could give a grief away
sideways and the ground would hold it for you, warm, indefinitely, no
questions taken.

"Kept," he said quietly, to the shelf at large — to the earring, and
the clay horse, and the blank coin, and a woman forty years dead who
liked the water warm, and a woman two years dead who thought his hands
were wasted, and eight lamps unlit under a fog five hundred miles east
— "this year also."

And got back on the cart.

Hachirō clicked at the fat horse, and the road turned south, and the
steam of the springs stood over the shoulder of the mountain behind
them for six miles of switchbacks, white and lit and climbing, patient
as held breath — and then the road went round a bluff of black pine,
and the whole warm breathing country of it slid out of the mirror-flat
morning air, and was gone.

Sazare faced front.

South, the coast. On the coast, a peg-board, second peg from the left.
In his pack, a medical roll the size of his forearm, a bokken cut to
the wood's own say-so, a cloth roll of thirty-four worthless offcuts,
twelve chips against the day he needed a door, and a pendant's weight
of a question he had refused to answer beautifully.

Sixteen years old. Taught, not run.

"Now this stretch," said Hachirō, comfortably, as the cart came down
onto the coast road's long grey levels, "this stretch is *honest*—"

and Sazare, who had four seconds of ground anywhere he stood, and a
count still open in a ledger nobody could read, settled the pack
between his feet, and listened to the whole of it, every mile, all the
way down to the sea.
