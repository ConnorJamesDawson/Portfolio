# Chapter 44, Scene 6 — Two Columns and a Blank Head

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,690 words.*

---

He did it on the Thursday, at a bench, nine feet from her.

It was not a decision. That was the part he had to be accurate about
afterwards and the part that took him four attempts to write down,
because every version he produced made it sound like a thing he had sat
down and chosen, and it had not been. It had been a Tuesday finding and
a Thursday afternoon and a pencil already in his hand.

The finding was three days old. *Not a property of the object, not a
property of the receiver, in the taking-up.* He had it from a
fourteen-year-old on a staircase and it was the cleanest thing anybody
had handed him in a year, and it had the shape of every good finding he
had ever had, which was that it immediately produced the next
experiment.

If it is in the taking-up, then it is a *rate*.

If it is a rate, it is measurable.

If it is measurable, you measure it against something.

He got out a fresh sheet, and ruled it, and put two columns on it, and
wrote *K.S.* at the head of the left one.

He did not write anything at the head of the right one.

---

He filled it in over about fifty minutes.

The yard was hot and the tar had gone soft and Kanzaki had shut herself
in the office with the shutters half over, and Kurihara was at the far
bench under the sky with the taper going, working her own commission —
a set of eleven for the sealing office, due the following month, which
she was doing in the wrong order deliberately and had explained twice.

She corrected him once, at about the twentieth minute, without turning
round. He said *thank you* and wrote the correction in the log, and
then went back to the sheet.

*K.S. — corrects without softening. Has done it forty-one times. Not
one of them unkind.*

*— better than me at a thing I am good at, four feet away, in a way I
can follow every step of and could not have got to.*

*— does not stop working when I arrive and does not stop working when I
leave.*

*— eleven visits with no work order.*

He looked at the right-hand column.

*— reads a document I have spent nine days on and finds the thing I put
in the third paragraph, and says so, out loud, before I have finished
the sentence.*

*— has never once told me a thing I could have got off a shelf.*

*— makes the conversation about something I did not bring.*

*— I cannot predict the subject of any exchange I have ever had.*

He wrote nine of those and four in the other column and stopped,
because the pencil had got to the tenth and the tenth was going to be
about a hand on a shoulder that had taken four seconds, and the whole
apparatus of the thing arrived in him at once, the way a fault does.

He put the pencil down.

Then he read the sheet from the top, properly, the way he read
everything, as though somebody else had written it.

---

Item three in the left column was *told me why she left. Two people in
the room. A thing she has not repeated to anybody in two years.*

He had written that down.

He had written it in a comparison table, in a yard, at a bench, in the
sun, forty-eight hours after a woman had sat in an empty noodle house
and given it to him at a cost he had watched her pay across a table,
and he had entered it as **evidence**.

Sazare sat very still.

*This is the paper trap.*

*This is the identical object. I have built a nine-month covert
measurement of a person who has not been told, using material she gave
me in confidence as the input, and I did not decide to do it — the
scheme assembled itself out of an honest finding and my own hands
between two and four in the afternoon, and I would not have noticed at
all except that the tenth entry was going to be about somebody else.*

*Kanzaki asked me to read the doctrine back and tell her where it said
patients.*

*It does not say patients. It does not say this hospital. It does not
say paper. It says nobody.*

He looked at the sheet for some time.

His hand went to the top corner, which was the ordinary movement, the
one he had made perhaps nine hundred times with a bad draft.

He stopped it.

*No.*

*I do not destroy records. I have not destroyed a record since I was
seven and the entire reason anybody in this village lets me build
anything is that I keep the half that makes me look bad.*

*If I tear this out, then in four years I will remember it as a thing I
thought about for a moment and did not do, because that is what an
absence in a book becomes.*

So he did not tear it out.

He ruled a line under the last entry, and turned the sheet over, and
wrote on the back:

*The above is a comparison table constructed on the 22nd of the fifth
month in the quartermaster's yard, over approximately fifty minutes, in
which I entered a woman who was standing nine feet away against a
person whose name I did not write at the head of the column.*

*It contains, at item three, something told to me in confidence four
days ago.*

*I am not removing it. I am recording what it is, because a page torn
out is a page I will remember incorrectly.*

*Two further observations and both go in.*

*One. I did not decide to do this. It assembled out of a correct finding
and it took under a minute to start and it felt, throughout, exactly
like work.*

*Two. There are nine entries in the right column and four in the left,
and I could not write the name at the top of the right one, and I have
noticed that I have now failed to write that name twice in nine days in
a private book that nobody will ever read.*

*The failure to write it is the measurement. Not the table. The table
is just the ugliest thing I have ever caught myself doing.*

---

She noticed at about ten past five.

He never worked out what the tell had been, and he thought about it a
great deal afterwards and could not construct it — he had not moved
oddly, he had not gone quiet in a way that was different from the way
he went quiet at a bench four times a week, and the sheet had been face
down for an hour by then.

But she came over, which she did not do, and stood at the end of his
bench, which she did not do either.

"Something's happened," she said.

"No."

"You've been on that log for two hours and you've made one entry."

"It's a slow month."

Kurihara looked at him.

She had the lens still pushed up on her head and ink to the second
knuckle and the permanent small frown from close work, and she was
twenty years old and had buried nobody and had lost a teacher across
town without being told, and she had come nine feet across a yard in
the heat to ask a question she plainly did not want to ask.

"Right," she said. "I'll do it properly then. Has something happened?"

And Sazare, who had spent eleven months learning what it does to a
person when somebody decides which parts of the truth they can handle,
and who had said so out loud in a noodle house four days ago with a
straight face, said:

"No."

---

She went back to the far bench.

She worked for another forty minutes and she was perfectly pleasant
about all of it — she corrected him twice more, and the second one she
softened, which she had never done, and he heard her do it and knew
exactly what it meant and had no move available that was not worse.

She left at ten to six instead of seven.

At the gate she said, "I've got the eleven due, so it'll be next week
some time," and did not name a day, and it was the first visit in five
weeks that had ended without one.

Sazare stood in the yard with a log in front of him for a while after
that.

Then he wrote the last entry of the day, which was not about aggregate.

*Asked directly, twice, in a yard, at cost to her, having crossed nine
feet of ground to do it.*

*Answered "no."*

*I have four separate entries in this book about what that does to a
person and I have been extremely eloquent in all four of them.*

*The true answer was available. It is: I did something with what you
gave me that you would not have consented to, I stopped, I have
recorded it, and I am ashamed. Eleven seconds. I had it constructed
before she finished the question.*

*I did not say it because saying it costs me her, and I worked that out
inside about two seconds, exactly the way I worked out inside four
seconds in the third month that filing a slip on the letter in the
drawer would cost me the thing I had got for filing a slip on the
letter in the envelope.*

*Same shape. Same speed. Same man.*

*The difference is that the letter had Ibiki at the end of it and this
has nobody at the end of it, and there is no procedure that is going to
come and make me say it.*

He closed the log.

At the far bench the taper had gone out on its own about half an hour
earlier and nobody had been there to notice it.
