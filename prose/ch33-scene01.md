# Chapter 33, Scene 1 — The Drawer

*Act 4, part C. Konoha, the hospital gate. Sazare Ruri, age seventeen.
POV: Sazare. ~1,414 words.*

---

He rehearsed it for nine days and it came out wrong anyway.

That was not a failure of preparation. He had established, over six
attempts and a ruled page with no unit at the head of the second
column, that rehearsing a sentence made it worse rather than better —
that a line assembled in advance arrived in the room sounding like a
line assembled in advance, and that the two things he had ever
successfully said to another human being had both been unplanned and
had both cost him more than the rehearsed ones did.

Knowing that did not stop him rehearsing.

He caught Ashida at the gate at the fourth hour on a Thursday in the
third month, which was where they still met most weeks despite there
having been no movement form to complete for over a year, because
neither of them had ever suggested stopping and it had become, without
either of them agreeing to it, a thing that happened.

Ashida was a father twice over by then. The second had arrived in the
winter and had taken about four stone off his sleep and put most of it
back on his face, and he had the specific grey around the eyes that
Sazare had spent eighteen months learning to recognise on a ward. His
flak jacket was done up wrong — one buckle across from the correct
loop — and had been since at least the previous week.

*Rung two: nobody's checked it and he hasn't looked in a mirror.*

*Rung three: he's dressing in the dark so he doesn't wake anybody.*

"You look terrible," said Sazare.

"Thank you. That's the nicest thing anybody's said to me since
Tuesday." Ashida leaned against the gate pier. "Go on then."

"Go on what?"

"You've had a face on for two weeks. My wife has a face like that when
she's about to tell me we're out of money, and I've learned to just
stand still and let it happen."

Sazare stood at the gate of a hospital in a foreign country and
discovered that all nine days of rehearsal had gone.

"I want to know what happened in Kiri," he said.

---

Ashida did not say anything at all.

That was the first thing that was wrong, because Ashida always said
something — he was a man who filled silences the way water fills a
hull, reflexively and without any particular intent, and Sazare had
never in twenty months seen him take longer than about a second to
produce a sentence.

He took eleven.

"Right," he said.

"If it's not something you can—"

"It's not that." Ashida straightened up off the pier. "It's not that at
all. Give me a minute, I'm — give me a minute."

He looked out at the road for a while.

"Twenty months," he said. "You've never asked. Not once. Not about the
war, not about the purges, not about who's running it, not one word,
and I have sat at this gate with you about four hundred times."

"I know."

"And I have had, in the drawer of my desk in the liaison office, since
about the fourth month, a folder."

Sazare went entirely still.

"Not intelligence," Ashida said quickly. "I want that clear before you
say anything, because I've thought about how this sounds for about a
year and it does not sound good. Nothing classified, nothing off a
desk I shouldn't have been at. Newspapers. The Fire Daily and the
trade sheets and twice one of the coastal ones a merchant left in the
gate house. Public notices. Anything with the word Water in it that
was going to be thrown away."

"Why?"

"Because I clipped the first one out on a Tuesday without thinking
about it, and then there was a second one, and by the time there were
four it had become a thing I was doing." He rubbed the back of his
neck. "And then I never gave it to you."

"Why?"

"Because you never asked."

---

They stood at the gate.

"That's not a reason," said Sazare.

"No. It's an excuse and I know the difference, I've had a year to work
out which one it was." Ashida looked at him properly. "The reason is
that handing a man a folder about his dead country is a thing you do
*to* him. If you'd wanted it you'd have said. And every single day you
didn't say, that got a bit more true, and after about eight months
handing it over would have meant saying *I have been keeping this and
watching you not ask for eight months*, and there is no hour of the day
you can say that to somebody and have it land well."

"So you kept clipping."

"So I kept clipping." He laughed, once, without any humour in it at
all. "There's about ninety of them in there. It's got a rubber band
round it. My wife knows about it. She asked me in the summer why I
didn't just give it to you and I gave her the eight-months answer and
she looked at me exactly the way you're looking at me now, which was
extremely annoying, because she was right and it took me until about
February to admit it."

Sazare found that his throat had done something inconvenient.

"I thought—" He stopped and started again, because the first version
was not the true one. "Everybody in this village decided I didn't want
to know."

"Yes."

"Because I never said."

"Because you never said, and because you took the headband, and because
you tie it to a bag." Ashida shrugged. "You have been, and I mean this
kindly, the easiest man in the Land of Fire to not tell things to. You
don't ask. You've never asked anybody for anything in the whole time
I've known you. You asked *me* for a form once and you looked like you
were confessing to something."

"I remember the form."

"So do I. It was a stationery requisition." Ashida pushed off the pier
properly. "Right. Come on."

"Where?"

"The office. I'll get you the folder, because it's yours and it's been
yours since the fourth month and I've been sitting on it like a
coward." He was already walking. "And then I'm going to put in a proper
request for the actual thing, because ninety newspaper clippings will
tell you what happened and it will not tell you what happened, and you
know that and I know that and we may as well both stop pretending
otherwise."

---

The folder was buff, tied with string as well as the rubber band, and
had *WATER* on the front in Ashida's own hand — the confident,
badly-spelled hand that his wife corrected when it mattered and had
evidently not been asked to correct here.

It weighed almost nothing.

Sazare put both hands on it and did not open it.

"You can take it home," said Ashida.

"Yes."

"You don't have to read it tonight."

"No."

"Ruri." Ashida stopped tidying the desk, which he had been doing to
have something to do with his hands. "I'm sorry. That's — I've been
trying to work out how to say that for about a year, and there isn't a
good way, so: I'm sorry. I had it and you didn't and I told myself
eleven different reasons why that was your fault."

Sazare stood in a small office on the ground floor of the
administration building holding ninety pieces of newspaper about a
country that had killed everybody he was related to, and understood
that he was being apologised to, properly, by somebody who had thought
about it for a year.

He had no procedure for this.

He fell back, as he always did, on the true thing.

"You kept them for twenty months," he said. "Nobody asked you to. You
did it on a Tuesday without thinking about it and then you kept doing
it for twenty months, and you did it for somebody who never once gave
you a single sign that he wanted it."

"That's — I mean, it's a rubber band and some scissors—"

"It's the kindest thing anybody has done for me since I came to this
village," said Sazare, "and I'd have gone the whole of my life without
finding out about it, because you'd decided it was embarrassing."

Ashida Kōhei looked at the desk.

"Get out of my office," he said thickly, "I've got forms."
