# Chapter 19, Scene 3 — What People Need

*Act 3, part A. The north shore. POV: Haku, age fifteen. ~2,550 words.*

---

Haku woke at the fourth hour the way he always did, without an alarm and
without effort, in the dark, on a folded quilt on an earth floor eight
feet from the hearth, and lay still for a moment listening to Zabuza-san
breathe.

That was the first thing every morning and had been for eleven days.

Better. Definitely better. The catch at the top of the breath had gone
entirely on the seventh day and what was left now was only the shallowness
— a man sleeping in the top third of his lungs because the bottom two
thirds still hurt to fill — and that would go too, and would go faster
now he was up and moving.

Haku got up without a sound, banked the fire, put water on, and went
outside to be cold for a minute.

The beach was silver. The tide was two hours off the bottom and the whole
flat of the shingle lay pale under a sky going from black to the deep
grey-blue that comes before any colour at all, with the islands out in the
channel just beginning to separate themselves from the water. It was cold
enough to see his breath. There was frost on the top of the woodpile and
on the new boards of the veranda, which had gone from raw yellow to a
dulled honey in nine days and would be silver in two summers.

He stood on them in bare feet — they did not flex; the whole front of the
veranda had stopped flexing, which he noticed every single morning and had
not stopped being pleased about — and looked at the water and thought
about breakfast.

Rice, obviously. The last of the mackerel, which wanted eating today. Egg,
if the woman at the second house on the ridge road had any, and she
usually did and always said she did not. And something green, because
Zabuza-san had eaten nothing green in a fortnight and was beginning to get
the specific dull look about the eyes that people get, and would not
notice it himself, and would not thank anybody for mentioning it, and
would eat whatever was in front of him without comment if it simply
appeared in the bowl.

That was the whole method. You do not ask people what they need. Asking
makes it a favour, and a favour has to be accepted, and there are people
who cannot accept anything.

You *look*, and then you put it in the bowl.

---

The thing Haku had learned before he learned anything else — before the
ice, before the needles, before he could read — was that people are
extremely easy to see.

Not their thoughts. He had never had the least interest in what people
were thinking and would not have known what to do with it. What he saw was
simpler and, as far as he had ever been able to tell, invisible to almost
everybody else: the *lack*. The thing a person was short of. It sat on
them like a colour.

The woman at the well who touched his arm was short of somebody to say
something ordinary to before noon. The herbalist was short of anybody
alive who thought his trade was interesting. Old Watari on the plank walk
was short of being asked a question he knew the answer to; you gave him
one and he talked for a quarter of an hour and went home a different
shape.

None of it was a trick and Haku had never once thought of it as one, which
was why it worked. You saw what somebody wanted, and if it cost you
nothing you gave it to them, and the town then behaved toward you in a
manner that a man like Rei of the paper school would have spent four
months and a great deal of money to arrange.

It was also the reason he was good at the other thing.

Because what a person lacks is where they are open, and the two facts sit
in the same place, and Haku had never been able to see one without the
other and had stopped expecting to.

---

He went up over the ridge at first light with the basket and the small
knife and came back down the town side into the woods above Nami, where
the ground was soft with needles and the light came through in long low
bars full of standing damp, and he worked the good bank for an hour and
did well: yarrow, some late plantain, and a great deal of the
grey-leaved thing his mother had called by a name he had never heard
anybody else use.

There had been a boy asleep on the ground in the clearing at the top.

Twelve or so, in orange, filthy, flat on his back in the wet with his arms
out and his mouth open, having plainly trained himself unconscious some
time in the small hours; and he had a Konoha protector on his forehead and
scrapes down both forearms and the beginnings of a chest cold.

Haku had thought about it for the length of two breaths.

It would have taken about a second and a half. He had the needles in his
sleeve and the boy was on his back with his throat up and there had been
nobody within a mile.

He had decided against, and the reason he gave himself was operational and
was perfectly sound: a body in that clearing brings the jōnin out of the
house at a run, and moves the whole thing forward by a week, and
Zabuza-san was at eleven days out of the fifteen he needed. Killing the
boy would have cost them four days. That was the sum and it came out
clean.

There was a second reason underneath it and Haku did not look at it, in
the way that he did not look at a number of things, on the perfectly
reasonable grounds that looking at them had never once changed what he
did.

He had woken him up instead, and told him he would catch cold, and they
had talked for a while. He was an extremely funny boy. He had no idea in
the world who he was talking to and had said so much in four minutes that
Haku could have written a page on the household — the old man, the man
with the crutch, the girl, the dark one he clearly hated and clearly
did not — and had gone off up the path afterwards shouting something over
his shoulder.

Haku had watched him go and had felt, for about a hundred yards' worth of
walking, an emotion he did not have a name for and put down as tiredness.

---

He got back at the ninth hour and made the egg and the greens happen, and
Zabuza-san ate all of it without one word about any of it, which was
correct and was how it was supposed to go.

Then he sat up against the post with his cup and said:

"The mender's still in the town."

"Yes."

"You've been up there."

"Twice." Haku scoured the pan out with sand and salt water, which is the
only thing that gets a pan properly clean and which nobody in the Land of
Waves seemed to know. "He mended the lid."

"Course he did." A grunt. "What's he for?"

And that was the actual question and they both knew it, and Haku sat back
on his heels by the fire with the pan on his knee and gave it the four or
five minutes it deserved.

He had watched the boy for nine days, on and off, in the way you watch
something in a town: from the ridge, from the top lane, from a tree above
the plank walk at supper time. It had cost him nothing. He had gone up
anyway, most days, because he had to be in the town for the herbs and
because there was very little else to do while a man's chest knits.

Here is what he had.

*He is in the trade.* Not a guess. He had seen it in the doorway on the
first afternoon before either of them had said a word: the way the boy
came up the beach with the cart at an even pace in plain view from four
hundred yards, whistling, which is not a thing a person does by accident;
the way he put his hands where they could be seen without appearing to;
the way he had chosen, out of every possible reason for walking up to that
house, the only one that could not be disproved.

*He is not very good at it.* Also not a guess. The knee is finished. The
ribs went four or five weeks ago and were not rested. He stands with about
four-fifths of himself on the right foot and does it without knowing, and
in a scuffle he will go over the first time anybody puts a shoulder into
his left side. He carries nothing. He has no reach and no weight and no
speed and, unless he is concealing it better than anybody Haku had ever
met, no jutsu at all.

*He is dangerous in exactly one way and it is not a way that helps him.*
He looks at things until he understands them. That is the whole of it.
Haku had watched him look at the underside of a pot lid and get nine years
of somebody else's bad decisions off it in four seconds, and it was not
cleverness, it was not deduction, it was more like a sense — the boy put
his eyes on an object and the object told him what had been done to it.

Point that at a house and it will tell you what is in the house. Point it
at a town and it will tell you who is paying whom. Point it at a bridge —

"He knows," said Haku. "About Friday. He worked it out days ago; he had
it before I did, probably, and he has not said a word to a single person
in that town."

Zabuza did not move.

"Then you'll do him on the bridge," he said, "if he's on the bridge."

"Yes."

"How."

"Left side," said Haku, scouring the pan. "He'll turn to look at whatever
I want him to look at, because he always does, and then it's the left
knee, and then it's two seconds, and he won't be able to stand up again
inside them. He'd have to be a great deal closer to the water than he'll
be. I'd rather it was quick and it can be."

"Good."

And that was that, and Haku set the pan on the ledge to dry and put the
rice on, and the fire ticked, and out on the beach the oystercatchers went
along the tideline in their line, piping.

---

He thought about it that afternoon while he was cutting kindling, in the
way he thought about everything, which was slowly and without any sense of
being troubled.

He liked the boy. That was simply a fact and did not appear to be
connected to anything. He had liked him within about ninety seconds, at
the bottom of the steps, over the business of the planks, and had liked
him more on the hill over the two numbers, and would go on liking him,
and would put a needle through the side of his knee on Friday morning
without the liking making the slightest difference to his hand.

People found that hard. He knew they found it hard because he had watched
them find it hard: Zabuza-san had once explained to him, at some length
and with visible discomfort, that a man in this work has to be able to
put things away, and Haku had listened politely and had understood, at the
end of it, that Zabuza-san had a room in him with things in it and a door
on the room, and that keeping the door shut was work, and that it cost
him.

Haku did not have a room and had never had one, and for years had assumed
this was because he was young and would grow one later, and had eventually
worked out — at about twelve, gutting fish — that he was not going to.

He was not divided. There was nothing in him at odds with anything else in
him. He liked a boy on Thursday and would kill him on Friday and both of
those were simply what he was doing, in the way that gathering yarrow was
what he was doing, and there was no seam anywhere in it for a thing to
catch on.

He had told Zabuza-san once that he was his tool. Zabuza-san had made a
noise and changed the subject, and Haku had let him, because he had seen —
this was the lack; it sat on the man like a colour — that Zabuza-san
needed it not to be true, and needing a thing not to be true is a form of
being short of it, and you do not argue with somebody about their own
lack.

It was true, though. It was the most restful sentence Haku knew. A tool is
not asked to decide anything. A tool has no second reasons underneath its
first ones. A tool is picked up by somebody who wants it, which is the
only thing Haku had ever wanted in his life and the only thing he had ever
been given, and the whole of the rest of it — the herbs, the pan, the
egg, the town, the lid, the two wooden pins in his hair — was simply what
a well-kept tool does with its mornings.

---

He thought about the other boy too, once, at the end of the afternoon,
with the light going amber down the beach and the kindling stacked.

*Thirteen*, and everything on that hill in the way he had said it.

Somebody had sat Sazare Ruri down at thirteen years old and explained it
to him — the reason, out loud, in front of people, with a piece of paper —
and he had been carrying the explanation ever since like a man carrying a
door, and it had made him into something Haku could recognise perfectly
and could not envy at all: a person with a decision in him that would not
finish.

You could see where it had worn him. It was in the not-asking. It was in
the way he had said *yes* three times in a row on that hill without adding
a single word to any of them.

Haku picked up the kindling and went in and set it by the hearth.

He was, on the whole, extremely glad nobody had ever told him anything.
He had a man who wanted him, and a fire, and a pan that was clean, and a
lid that shut properly for the first time since it was made; and on Friday
morning there would be fog on the bridge, because there always was, and he
would do what he was for.

"There's rice," he said.
