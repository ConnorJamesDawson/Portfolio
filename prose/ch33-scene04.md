# Chapter 33, Scene 4 — An Occupying Will

*Act 4, part C. Konoha, the east side. Sazare Ruri, age seventeen.
POV: Sazare. ~1,456 words.*

---

He walked home the long way, which added a mile and cost his knee about
twenty minutes, and did not notice he had done it until he was on the
wrong side of the market row.

It was the fifth hour and the light was going and the stalls were
coming down. O-Shige saw him from forty feet away and did not call out,
which she had never once failed to do in two years, and he understood
about a street later that she had looked at his face and made a
decision.

The block was quiet. Nemoto was not on his stool; it was too cold. Two
floors down somebody's radio was playing the second half of something.
Ōtaki Sue's window was lit and he did not stop, because there was
nothing he could have written on that slate.

He got up to his own room and sat down on the mat without taking his
coat off and stayed there while the light went out of the window
entirely.

---

The thing he could not get to stand still was that he had already put
it down.

That was the whole of it. He had made himself go through it four times
on the walk and it came out the same way each time.

He had given up the revenge on a shingle spit in the Land of Waves at
sixteen years old, in the fog, with the sea going out through the
stones, in an argument he had lost to a boy who was dead four hours
later. He had put it down deliberately and completely and had not
picked it up again, not once, not in fourteen months of a village that
would have given him a hundred quiet opportunities to.

He had stopped wanting it because he had worked out that wanting it was
making him into something.

Not because it was aimed at the wrong man.

*I did the right thing for reasons that turn out to be beside the
point,* he thought, sitting in the dark in his coat, *and now the
reasons have arrived, fifteen months late, and there is nothing for
them to do.*

*If I had found this out at fourteen it would have broken me open.*

*If I had found it out at fifteen, in the rope-walk, I would have gone
back. I would have gone back and I would have died somewhere between
Kasado and the coast, and it would have been for a man who was not
holding the pen.*

*I found it out at seventeen, in a room with a radiator that doesn't
work, from a woman with pen calluses, and I have nowhere to put it.*

---

The second thing took him longer and was worse.

*For a substantial portion of his tenure.*

He turned that over for about an hour and could not make it produce a
date, and eventually understood that the absence of a date was not a
gap in the intelligence. It was the actual shape of the thing.

Somewhere there was a first morning. There was a day on which the
Fourth Mizukage of Kirigakure had woken up as a man with his own
opinions and had gone to sleep as an instrument, and nobody alive knew
which day that was, and nobody ever would, because the only person who
could have said was dead and had not known either.

Which meant the purge had two possible histories and no way to choose
between them.

In one, a young man decided that a category of people was a danger and
acted on it, and then, at some later point, was taken over by something
that continued a policy it found already running.

In the other, he never decided anything at all, and everything from the
first list onward was done through him.

*And my mother is equally dead in both of them.*

He sat with that.

*And in one of them there is a man to be angry at who has been dead
for two years and was probably a prisoner for most of the time I spent
hating him, and in the other there is a thing with no name and no face
that nobody has found, and I am not going to find it either, because I
am a seventeen-year-old crystal fabricator with a bad knee and a
support classification and four hundred hours of anatomy to do.*

He got up at some point and lit the lamp because sitting in the dark
had begun to feel like a decision rather than an oversight.

---

*And the purges have stopped.*

He made himself look at that one properly, because it was the part he
had been walking around for three hours, and it was the part that would
not fit anywhere in him.

The bloodline restrictions were repealed. Standing purge orders
rescinded. A general amnesty, subject to individual review. Somewhere
in the Land of Water tonight there were children with kekkei genkai
whose parents were not going to be visited, and there would be more of
them next year, and in fifteen years there would be a generation of
them who had never once been afraid of a knock, and every single one of
those people was alive because a war was won.

He had wanted that. He had wanted it more than he had wanted the
revenge, and he had gone on wanting it after he put the revenge down,
and it was the one thing in his life he had ever paid into steadily and
on purpose.

It had happened.

And he sat in a rented room in a foreign country and could not feel one
single thing about it, and that frightened him more than the rest of
the evening put together.

He wrote it down, eventually, because writing it down was the only
instrument he had:

*The war is over and the purges have stopped and I feel nothing.*

*I have checked this four ways. It is not numbness — I felt the ward
eleven entry, and I felt it in my hands. It is not disbelief. It is
that the part of me that wanted this was dismantled deliberately on a
beach fifteen months ago and I did the dismantling myself, and I did
it well, and there is nothing left in there to receive the news.*

*I am glad. I know that I am glad the way I know the persimmon is
outside the window. I cannot get to it.*

He looked at that for a while.

Then he added, underneath, in the same hand:

*Handa would say that is the joint and not the pain. Ask somebody.*

---

At the eleventh hour he took the buff folder with the rubber band off
the shelf and opened it for the first time.

Ninety-one clippings, in date order, which meant Ashida had gone back
and sorted them at some point rather than simply adding to the top.

The first was four lines from the Fire Daily's foreign column,
ten months old, about a fishing dispute.

The eleventh had been cut out badly, with the scissors going into the
text of the column beside it, and there was a thumbprint on the corner
in what appeared to be soy sauce.

The twenty-second had a note attached in Ashida's spelling: *this one
says the same as no. 19 but the numbers are different, might be
wrong.*

The fortieth was a public notice, reprinted in a trade sheet, of the
repeal of the bloodline restrictions. It was eleven lines long. It had
run below an advertisement for a shipping agent.

There was a second note on that one, in a different and much better
hand, which he looked at for a long time before he understood that
Ashida's wife had written it.

*Kōhei — I think this is the big one. — M.*

Sazare Ruri sat on the floor of his room at the eleventh hour with
ninety-one pieces of newspaper spread around him in a fan, and put his
head in his hands, and stayed like that for some considerable time.

It was not the repeal that did it, in the end, and it was not the
Mizukage or the ward number or the man with no face.

It was that a woman he had met twice, in a village he had lived in for
fourteen months, had read a shipping page over her husband's shoulder
at some point in the previous year and had recognised, correctly, that
eleven lines under an advertisement were the most important thing that
had happened to a boy neither of them was related to.

And had picked up a pen.

And had not told him either.
