# Chapter 13, Scene 4 — The Second Peg

*Act 2, part D. Shiranui. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,300 words.*

---

Shiranui had grown the way a wound swells.

He saw it from the last hill, from the seat of the cart, and spent the whole
descent reading it: a third again more masts in the harbour than three years
ago, and rafts of them the wrong kind — deep-keeled coasters riding empty,
waiting on cargoes that were worth waiting on. New roofs up the eastern
slope, raw wood, thrown up fast. A queue at the water-bailiff's shed that
had not existed. And down at the west end, where the exchange stood, the
chalk board that had once said *quarry, 6; nets, 2* now ran to two boards,
and a boy was wiping and rewriting the far end of it while Sazare watched,
because the far end was the day-work end, and day-work in a swelling port
changes by the hour.

"Busy," said Hachirō, in the voice he used for bad cambers.

"Is it the war?"

"It's always the war. A war's a fire in the next street, boy — everybody
who isn't burning does a roaring trade in buckets." He clicked at the
horse. "Three years back this coast said *it's a shame*. Now it says
nothing and rents out its lofts. You can watch a country learn to live
off a thing it's ashamed of, if you stand still long enough. It's not
improving, as a sight."

They said their goodbye at the top of the exchange lane, because Hachirō
had freight to see to and forty years of practice at not making a
ceremony of anything. He looked at Sazare a moment, from the cart, with
the reins loose in his brown old hands.

"You'll want to know what I'll say when they come up my road asking," he
said. "Because they will. You got famous, up there. Famous for mending,
which is a foolish lovely way to get famous, and it travels." He gathered
the reins. "I'll tell them the truth. Quiet lad. Paid his way. Got off at
Shiranui in the spring, and I don't know where he went, because I won't."
He clicked at the horse. "Keep off the platforms. Mind the camber. Go
on."

And that was the whole of it, and it was somehow more use to walk away
from than any embrace would have been, which Hachirō had almost certainly
calculated, because he had been managing other people's partings from
that cart seat for thirty-one years.

---

The exchange smelled the same — tea, wet wool, ledger-ink — and had
doubled its benches, and half the faces behind the counter were new, and
Anzu was not one of the new ones.

She was greyer, and thinner about the face, and she was three people's
worth of official now — he watched her run the room for a quarter-hour
from the bench, sorting a boat's worth of new arrivals with the pen
stopping and not-stopping in the old exact rhythm — and when the room
finally emptied she looked over at him without one particle of surprise.

"Well," she said. "The arm works, then."

"The arm works."

"Come here where I don't have to shout."

He sat down on the stool. The ledger between them was a fatter cousin of
the one four years dead; she flipped it backward through seasons of
other people's names, found her page, and laid her finger on the old
entry without hunting for it, which told him she had looked at it more
than once in the interval.

*Ruri Sazare, 13. Crystal (artisan, part-trained). Other: none. Not fit
for work. Medical — refer T.*

"Tōan's bird said you'd learned some doctoring," she said. "And the
coast says the spring towns have a mender who charges fair, which was
you being careless, and we'll come to that." She took up the pen,
crossed *not fit for work* with one clean line, and wrote, in the quick
small upside-down hand he could read as easily as ever: *general labour;
carries.* Then she looked up at him over it. "That's the paper version.
Say the true one."

"Courier," said Sazare. "South. And the tenth still goes in — I'll
carry my own remittance to your counter from now on, when there's a
counter. And I can mend, and I can do field-doctoring of the
keep-them-alive-till-morning kind, and I'd rather be paid in routes
than in coin where the choice comes up."

"Routes." Anzu sat back. "You've been talking to somebody who knows
what's worth what."

"I had good teachers."

"You had the last three of a breed," said Anzu, flatly. "I know
exactly what you had, I sent you to it." She squared the ledger.
"Right. Two things, and then the work, and then you're gone by the
morning boat-watch, because this town's grown eyes.

"First thing. You've been asked after. Twice that reached me, which
means more that didn't. One lot polite — trade-flavoured, patient,
paying for their drinks with too-clean money and asking about *old
island crafts*, which is a phrase I want you to hear the way I heard
it." She watched him hear it. "Good. You know that lot already, then.
The other lot was this spring, three men through the coast towns
calling themselves merchants' guards, and the ones my people marked
were marked because they never once asked a question. You people—"
she said it the way Sakai said it, with the same worn tenderness —
"you're the only trade on earth that thinks not asking questions is
invisible. In a port, a man who doesn't ask questions is the loudest
thing in the room.

"Second thing." She reached under the counter and put a twist of
waxed paper on the boards between them, and he knew the weight of it
before he touched it. "Ryōsuke's alive. Working, south, which is all
the where you'll get. He left these against the day you came through —
that's his hand on the wrapping, look at it properly, because from
here south you check every peg against *that* hand and no other. The
old twelve are burned; there was a bad spring two years back and we
lost a chandler, and everything he'd touched got retired. New marks."
She pushed it across. "He said to tell you the postbox is the same,
because he's an old man and he only ever had the one idea."

Sazare unwrapped a corner. A dozen chips, cloudy, worthless; the hand
on the marks was the hand off the boat-shed charts, older now,
tighter, the curve of it a shade more careful — a hand he'd have
picked out of a hundred at eight years old, sitting on a step in the
dark with his sister saying *no* and *no* and *yeah*.

"He knew I'd come through."

"He knew if you were alive you'd come through carrying money," said
Anzu. "You've been sending a tenth of everything down a mountain for
three years, love. Ryōsuke can read a manifest. Everyone who matters
has known you were up that valley since the second winter; the whole
art was making sure nobody who mattered *otherwise* did." She let
that sit exactly one beat. "It held, near enough. Now it's done
holding, so: south, small, and boring. Sell the mending less. Carry
more. Famous is finished."

---

He went to the wall before he left, because everybody went to the
wall.

It had grown a second board, and the second board was fuller than the
first had been in his year, and the chalk hung on two strings now,
and the grammar of the thing was unchanged: names, places, seasons;
lines through, second hands, *found. found. Kirigakure, held. found.*
And the ghosts — more ghosts than before, whole constellations of
them, names gone grey-on-black where season after season nobody had
come to freshen the asking.

He stood in front of it the way the quarrymen did, cap off, and read
it top to bottom, every name, in case. Nothing he knew. Then — down
the first board, half-height, in fresh chalk over old — he found
*Emiko, w. of Tatsue the caulker, & inf. dtr.* struck through, with
the second hand and the one word, and had to stand with that a
minute: the loft above the net store, nine days, a baby who had
cried at sea and been dosed quiet, a woman whose child's name Anzu
had asked with the pen not moving. *Found.* Somewhere on this coast
that family had knitted itself back together, and the wall had done
its work, and nobody would ever know the size of it who hadn't slept
nine nights in that loft.

And at the bottom corner of the old board, where he had stood at
thirteen with the chalk in reach and written nothing, there was a
name so faded it was more shadow than mark — a woman's name, he
thought; the *ko* survived and little else — with no line through it
and no second hand and nobody left, apparently, in the whole traffic
of that coast, who came of an evening to keep it asking.

He had not written four years ago because a name on that wall was a
question, and his questions had answers, and the wall was for people
who were allowed to hope.

But a question someone else had paid to keep open — that was
different property altogether. That was maintenance. He was a mender;
maintenance was the whole of his religion.

Sazare took the chalk on its string and went over the ghost of the
name, letter by letter where letters could be made out and stroke by
faithful stroke where they couldn't, freshening it back to black,
asking it again on behalf of whoever had once stood here every
evening asking — and put the chalk back, and put his cap on, and
walked down to the net store at the west quay in the last of the
light.

Second peg from the left.

There was a chip wedged flush in the joint, cloudy, worthless, and
the hand on it was older and tighter than a boat-shed chart and
would have been his out of a hundred.

*Ours. Safe. Knock.*

Sazare knocked, and a door on a foreign coast opened to him, four
years and a war away from a boy on a jetty with everything he owned
in one hand; and by the morning boat-watch he was gone south along
the grey levels with a parcel in his pack that did not rattle, a
route in his head instead of coin, and the tenth and the tenth
entered, in his own presence, at the counter, in a ledger, under a
name that wasn't true, by a woman who had never once written a true
thing about him in her life, and had thereby kept him alive.
