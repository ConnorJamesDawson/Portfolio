# Chapter 15, Scene 2 — Collecting

*Act 2, part D. The strait coast. Jirō, age fifteen. POV: Jirō. ~2,550 words.*

---

They did not ask a single question in Tomari.

That was Rei, and it was the best piece of work Jirō watched him do in
eleven months, and it was done two towns north and four days before they
ever saw the strait.

At a ferry-stage called Nakabe, Rei spent three days losing money to a
ferry clerk with a bad chest — not a great deal of money; enough to be
liked — and in three days he did not once mention a mender, a scar, a
boy, a barge, or anything that could be repeated afterwards by a man
trying to be helpful. What he talked about was *carrying*. He talked
about it the way a factor's man talks about a trade he is thinking of
buying into: sourly, with figures, complaining about the risk.

And the clerk, who had a bad chest and no money and a professional's
contempt for outsiders' figures, corrected him for three days.

Jirō sat at the end of the bench with a cup and learned the whole
machine.

Couriers on the thin coast were not paid for a journey. They were paid
in halves — half at the peg going out, half at the peg coming back, and
the coming-back half sat with a holder, a chandler's wife, an innkeeper,
some fixed unglamorous person in a fixed unglamorous town, until the
courier came and stood in front of them and was seen to be alive. It
was not a courtesy. It was an anti-theft device four hundred years old.
A man carrying his whole earnings on the road is a man worth robbing; a
man with nothing on him but a parcel and a route is worth nothing to
anybody, and both halves of the coast knew it, and the system had
therefore outlived every government that had tried to tax it.

Rei asked, sour, doing sums on his thumb, whether that didn't mean a lad
got stuck in one town like a nail in a plank.

"Course it does," said the clerk, and coughed, and took another of Rei's
coins. "That's the *point*. Money's an anchor. Runner works three, four
runs out of a stage before he'll shift, because shifting means walking
away from his coming-backs, and no man walks away from his coming-backs.
I've seen 'em stay through a fever. I've seen one stay through his
mother dying inland. You don't leave your halves."

That night in the tent Rei laid the coast charts out and did not look
pleased, because he never did, and said the sentence that arranged
everything after it.

"We've been chasing him along a line. That's the amateur's shape and
I've let us hold it for a month." He put his finger flat on the strait.
"You never catch a man moving. You catch him collecting. We stop
following the mender and we start reading the ferry-stages: which of
them has enough traffic to hold four runs' worth of pay, and which of
them has a holder, and which of them has had a young hand living in it
long enough to have got comfortable. That's not a search. That's a
list, and the list is short."

The list was four towns. Tomari was second on it, and they took the
towns in order, and the first one took a day and a half to clear, and
then they walked down the ferry road in the last of an afternoon and
Rei stopped on the rise above Tomari and looked at it for a long time
without saying anything at all.

Two hundred people. Four inns. A chandler. And on the jetty, at that
distance, small as a mark on a page, a figure kneeling to a boat's
thwart with a smaller figure beside it holding something in both hands.

Jirō stood on the rise and did not put his eyes there again, and could
not afterwards have told anybody the colour of the sky.

---

They took rooms at the far inn as men whose horse had gone lame, which
is a thing nobody in the world has ever found interesting, and Kōga
went and walked the town.

That was Kōga's whole contribution and it took him one evening and he
came back and sat down at the end of the bed and said more consecutive
words than Jirō had heard from him since Ōura.

"He's here." Kōga pulled a boot off. "Loft over the chandler's. Two
ways out of it and he's put a plank across the gable window that isn't
nailed, which means it's a lid, not a repair. Sleeps at the back. Piss
pot's by the *far* wall from the stair." The other boot. "Been here
weeks and hasn't slept a night of it. That's not a hiding man, that's a
man keeping house against something. Somebody taught him."

"Taught him what?" said Rei.

"Not to be found in a room," said Kōga. "Which he can't be. So we don't
do the room."

Rei nodded slowly, and pulled the chandler's out of the day's collation
— a chandler with a wife who held money for half the strait, which the
town had told him in about nine minutes, because a holder is not a
secret, a holder is a public utility — and said: "Then it's the
collection. What's his rhythm?"

"Runs out at first light. Back on the fourth or fifth day. He's out
now." Kōga lay down with his hands behind his head. "He'll come in off
the north path and go to the chandler's and stand in that back room
while she counts it, and he'll come out into the alley with more money
on him than he's had in his life, and I want him in the alley."

"Why the alley."

"Because it's the only ninety seconds this month when he'll be thinking
about something other than us."

Rei made a note. Jirō sat with his back against the wall and his knees
up and the taste of the inn's tea going metallic in his mouth, and put
it in order — he could still do that, it still worked, it worked
horribly well — and what came out in order was: *four days. Maybe
three.*

---

The flag was the argument, and the argument happened the next night, and
it was the first time Jirō ever heard the trade discuss him as a
schedule.

"Alive, undamaged, premium," said Rei. "Which in the wording is hands
and forearms, and only hands and forearms, and I want that said out
loud because I'd rather say it than have one of us discover it."

"It's said," said Kōga.

"Say what you'd do."

"Knee." Kōga did not sit up. "Left, going away, before he's ten feet in
any direction. Everything past that is politeness. A man with a knee
gone can't run, can't set his feet, can't do a thing with his hands
worth a damn because he's holding himself up with them, and it heals,
and a healed knee's not a *damage* by that wording, and nobody in any
office is going to read the file and find a knee in it." He shifted.
"Ribs if I can't get the knee. Collarbone if it's chaos. Nothing above
the elbow-line and nothing to the arms at all, either arm, which loses
me two of the three ways I'd normally do this and I want *that* said out
loud as well."

"It's said," said Rei.

"Then it's a bad job at a good price," said Kōga, "same as all of them,"
and went to sleep in the way he had of simply ceasing, like a lamp.

Rei wrote for a while. Jirō sat.

There is a thing that happens to a young man's face when he is being
watched by a professional and does not know it, and Jirō, who was very
good, and who had learned everything he had at a table where the
watching went both ways, knew perfectly well that Rei had put the pen
down and was doing it now.

"Say it," said Rei.

"Nothing to say."

"You've had nothing to say for four days, which for you is a shout."
Rei capped the ink. "You're eleven months in and this is your first
retrieval-grade close, and every one of you gets one thing stuck in the
throat on the first, and if you swallow it now it'll come up at a bad
moment. So. Say it here, where it's free."

Jirō thought about the several answers that would have been good
tradecraft and would have satisfied Rei entirely and would have been the
correct professional discharge of the question, and heard himself say
instead, in a voice that was almost level:

"He's fifteen."

"He is."

"He never served. There's no oath in the file to break. He killed a man
who was killing his family in front of four hundred people and the file
*says so*, it's in the incident line, it's not even disputed—"

"And the office read all of that," said Rei, "and priced him anyway, and
put *alive* on it, and here's the part I'd like you to hear properly,
because you're clever enough that it'll be worth something to you and
young enough that you'll get it wrong on your own. *Alive* is not
mercy." He set the pen in the fold of the book. "*Alive* is worse.
Dead's a closed file and a fee. Alive's a use. Every hour you spend
being sorry for him you might spend on the actual question, which is
whether you can do this work at all — and I'd rather know in a border
inn than in an alley."

"Can I do it?"

"You tell me in four days," said Rei, and blew the lamp.

Jirō lay in the dark of a foreign inn in a town with two hundred people
in it and did the honest accounting, because he had never in his life
been able to stop, and it came out like this:

He wanted the flag to hold. That was the first thing, and it was clean,
and he could look at it. *Alive* meant a room and an office and a
schedule of unspeakable things and it also, unarguably, meant *not in
the ground at Ōura or on a beach or floating*, and Jirō had read four
years of retrieval files and he knew what the other kind of flag
produced, and he had decided a long time ago, on his back in a barracks
at thirteen, that he would rather have a friend alive in a bad room than
not have one.

That was the first thing and he could look at it.

The second thing he could not look at, and it was this: he wanted to be
the one who found him. Not so that somebody kinder would. Not for the
premium. He wanted it because if it was anybody else in the world then
the last conversation the two of them would ever have would be a
stranger's, and Jirō had already had the last conversation once, at the
end of a street, in his indoor things, with his face going, saying *keep
your head down* about six times because he could not think of anything
else that was true — and he had spent three years discovering that
there was no version of his life in which that got to be the end of it.

He turned that one over until it stopped being possible to hold, and put
it where everything went.

The place was full. It had been full for a year. Things had begun,
lately, to fall out of it.

---

On the fourth morning, in the grey, in a stand of pines above the ferry
road, Rei opened the kit-roll and handed them out, and Jirō put on the
mask.

It was the same object it had been in the tent — white, wave-marked, the
three-slit eye-line, a chin you could tie under; two hundred years of a
village's design work, hammered flat in the making until there was
nothing in it a man could like. It was cold. It sat on the cheekbones
and not on the nose. He had worn it a dozen times in a yard at Kiri with
an instructor shouting, and it had been a piece of kit, and he had
thought Rei's warning was the kind of thing older men say.

Rei was not wrong.

What happened was not courage and it was not cruelty and it did not feel
like anything arriving. It felt like something *leaving*. He tied it,
and the world came down to three slits and his own breath, and the
enormous unremitting labour of being a face — the labour every human
being does all day without noticing, of arranging the front of the head
so that other people can read it and be answered by it — simply stopped,
the way a noise you have lived beside for years stops, and the silence
where it had been was so vast and so restful that Jirō stood in a pine
stand at fifteen years old and felt his shoulders come down for the first
time in three years.

Nobody could see him. Not hidden — *not there*. There was no one for
anything to be difficult for.

He understood, standing there, exactly what Rei had told him he would
understand: that this was not him, that it was the mask, that it was
built to do this, that a village had spent two centuries refining the
precise weight of bone-white porcelain that lifts the obligation of
being a person off a boy's shoulders and hands him, in its place, a
clean afternoon's work.

He understood it perfectly. It made not the slightest difference.

*That's the trick, then,* he thought, with the pines dripping and the
tide going out under the road. *That's the whole trick. They don't make
you into something. They just take the you off, and whatever's underneath
does the job, and it turns out the thing underneath has been standing
there this entire—*

"Bell's gone," said Rei, from twenty feet, in the flat voice the masks
seemed to require. "He's on the north path. Kōga has the alley; I've the
shop front and the road.
You're the alley mouth and you are *the alley mouth*, do you hear me —
you don't improve on it, you don't close, you stand in a gap and you are
a wall. That's the whole of your part."

"Yes."

"Say it back."

"I'm a wall," said Jirō, in the flat voice.

They went down through the pines to the ferry road, and Jirō, in a white
face on a foreign coast, discovered somewhere on the descent that the
rested feeling had a floor under it and the floor was not far down, and
that what was standing on the floor, quite patiently, waiting for the
next four minutes to be over, was an eleven-year-old at a kitchen table
listening to his mother lie beautifully to men with papers, thinking:
*they can't have him. Whoever they are. They can't have him.*

He could no longer tell, and had not been able to for some weeks, which
side of the argument that thought was on.
