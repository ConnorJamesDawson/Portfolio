# Chapter 33, Scene 2 — Nobody Wanted to Sign It

*Act 4, part C. Konoha, the intelligence building. Sazare Ruri, age
seventeen. POV: Sazare. ~1,174 words.*

---

The request took nine days and came back approved with two conditions
and a note that Sazare read four times before he understood what it was
telling him.

*Approved. Subject to (1) supervised reading, no removal of material,
(2) presence of a T&I officer throughout. Note: material has been held
in prepared form since [month 4] and requires no further compilation.*

Held. In prepared form. Since the fourth month.

He took it to Ibiki because Ashida had put Ibiki's name on the form and
because there was no version of walking into that building and asking a
clerk.

Ibiki's office was on the second floor and had one window with the
blind down, and a desk with nothing on it, and four chairs that had all
been placed at slightly different distances from that desk, which
Sazare had noticed the first time and had spent a year deciding was
either extremely deliberate or extremely careless and had settled on
deliberate.

He was heavier than he had been in the autumn and it had gone on
through the shoulders. The bandana was the same. His coat was hung on
the back of the door rather than worn, which meant he had been in that
room since before the building was warm.

"Sit anywhere," Ibiki said.

Sazare sat in the middle one.

"Interesting."

"It's the one at a conversational distance."

"It is. Most people take the far one and about one in nine takes the
near one and both of those tell me something." Ibiki did not smile.
"Your file says jeweller. It never says *and he counts the furniture*."

"It's in there twice now. Two people have written it down."

"Three." He put a folder on the desk. "You've read the note."

"It says the material's been ready since the fourth month."

"It has."

"That's eighteen months."

"It is."

---

"Then why didn't anybody give it to me?"

He had not intended it to come out with anything in it. It came out
with something in it.

Ibiki did not react to that either, which Sazare understood, four
seconds later, to be a courtesy rather than indifference.

"Because of the sentence somebody would have had to write on a form,"
he said. "Do you want the honest version or the institutional one?
They're both true and the institutional one is shorter."

"Honest."

"Then I'll do the institutional one first, because it's the one that
actually stopped it." Ibiki turned the folder round so it faced
Sazare and did not open it. "You are a foreign-origin shinobi. Your
country of origin is a hostile power with an active file on you. In
this building, providing you with an intelligence digest on that
country is one of two things: it is a serving Konoha shinobi being
briefed on a region he has expertise in, or it is a foreign national
receiving intelligence on his home nation. Those are the same act.
They produce the same paper. And which one it is depends entirely on
an assessment of your loyalty that nobody wanted to make in writing."

"Because if I'd defected—"

"Because if you had *ever* defected, at any point in the following
thirty years, then the officer who signed that form would have spent
the rest of his career being the man who briefed you." Ibiki tapped the
desk once. "It is a small career risk with an enormous tail. And it is
the exact species of risk that institutions do not take on behalf of
people who have not asked."

"And if I'd asked?"

"Then the form says *at the subject's request*, which moves the
decision onto you, which is a sentence anybody in this building would
sign before breakfast." He sat back. "That's it. That's the whole
mechanism. Nineteen months of it and it is not more complicated than a
preposition."

Sazare sat with that.

"That's obscene," he said.

"It's clerical. It's frequently the same thing." Ibiki's chair creaked.
"Now the honest version, which is shorter and worse."

---

"Nobody thought you wanted it."

"That's the same as Ashida's."

"It's not, because Ashida is fond of you and I am not, and we arrived
at the same place from opposite ends, which should tell you something
about how strong the signal was." He held up one finger. "Twenty
months. Not one question. Not to your liaison, not to your assessor,
not to me, not to Yamanaka, who I remind you went inside your head at
your own invitation and came out with a note that says *there is nobody
in the middle of you*."

"I remember what he said."

"You are the only person I have ever assessed who has never once, in
any interview, asked me a question about anything that was not directly
in front of him." Ibiki leaned forward. "Do you understand how rare
that is? Every prisoner asks. Every recruit asks. Foreign nationals
ask constantly, it's the single most reliable thing about them. You
have sat in that chair four times and answered everything and asked
nothing, and the file therefore says — because a file can only say what
happened — that this man does not want to know things."

"I did want to know."

"Then that is a fault in the file," said Ibiki, "and the file is a
record of your behaviour, and the behaviour was real."

---

They sat there.

"Can I ask you something," said Sazare.

Ibiki's expression did not change and something behind it did.

"Yes."

"Is any of it good?"

"Some of it." He put a hand flat on the folder. "Not the part that will
matter to you, and I'm telling you that now rather than in an hour,
because you are about to sit in a room with a clerk who will read you
sixty pages in a flat voice and you would rather be braced."

"Why a clerk?"

"Because a clerk has no stake in it and no opinion about you, and I
have found over twenty-nine years that people take a thing like this
better off somebody who is plainly just reading. If I read it to you,
you will spend the whole of it watching my face for what I think you
should feel." Ibiki stood. "Also she is faster than I am and she does
not editorialise, which is the single rarest skill in this building."

At the door he stopped.

"Ruri."

"Yes?"

"When it's done, ask her a question. Any question, doesn't matter what.
You'll want to sit there and take it and leave, because that is what
you do, and I am telling you as a professional matter that you should
open your mouth in that room at least once before you walk out of it."

"Why?"

"Because in nineteen months this village has built an entire model of
you out of the things you didn't say," said Ibiki, "and I have just
spent a quarter of an hour explaining to you what that cost, and I
would rather not do it again in another nineteen."
