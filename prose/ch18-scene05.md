# Chapter 18, Scene 5 — Eleven Boards

*Act 3, part A. The north shore, and the ridge. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen.
POV: Sazare. ~2,200 words.*

---

He came back the next morning at a little after seven with the cart and
his roll and a bag of dogleg nails he had made himself out of the
straightened remains of somebody's old gate, and he worked on that veranda
until the light went, and it was the best day he had had in four years.

The weather did half of it. It came up one of those cold bright washed
mornings you get on that coast after rain, with the sky a hard pale blue
right down to the horizon and not a rag of cloud on it, and the sea flat
and glittering so violently that he had to work with his back to it for
the first hour. The pines behind the house dripped for a while and then
stopped and began instead to give off that hot resin smell as the sun got
round onto them. There were oystercatchers working the tideline forty
yards off, going along in a line, piping at each other in a high
ridiculous whistle every few minutes for no reason he could establish.

The joists, when he got the rotten boards up, were larch and were forty
years old and were entirely sound apart from one, and the one had gone
soft at the outboard end where a downpipe had been dripping on it since
before he was born.

He sistered it. He cut a length of new larch, drove it up alongside the
bad one, bolted the pair together through the good wood, and the whole
front of that veranda went from something that flexed when you stood on it
to something that did not, and the going-from-one-to-the-other took about
forty minutes and cost eleven coppers of timber, and he sat back on his
heels afterwards and looked at it with a satisfaction so pure and so
uncomplicated that it was almost embarrassing.

Then he laid the boards. Six new ones spaced in among the old, which meant
they were going to sit proud and yellow among all that weathered silver
for about two years and then stop showing at all; and he set them with a
finger's gap for the swell, and he counter-sunk every nail head, and where
the new met the old he ran a plane along the edge four or five strokes so
that a bare foot going across in the dark would not find a step.

Nobody had asked him for the plane. Nobody would ever notice it. He did it
anyway, and knew he was doing it, and had a fairly good idea by then what
it was he was actually doing on that beach, and went on doing it.

---

Haku brought him tea twice and food once and sat on the steps for a while
in the middle of the afternoon with his knees up, watching, saying almost
nothing, which turned out to be a thing he was extremely good at.

He had a way of being present without pressing on the air. Sazare noticed
it because it was a version of something he did himself — the difference
being that when Sazare did it he was hiding, and when Haku did it he
appeared simply to have arrived at the natural condition of a person, like
water finding its level.

"You've done a lot of this," Haku said, once.

"Some."

"No — this." He nodded at the plane, lying on the boards with its sole up
the way you leave a plane down if anyone has ever taught you to. "The way
you put it down. Nobody does that unless they've been shouted at about
it."

"My mother."

"Ah," said Haku.

And they left it there, because that was the sort of afternoon it was; and
Sazare sat on a mended veranda in the sun and worked and did not say the
other thing, and the other thing was this:

The boy's accent was from the Land of Water. It was not from Kiri. It was
country, and it was north — the flat *e*, the swallowed final syllable, the
way *water* came out with the weight in the wrong place — and Sazare had
heard it about four times in his life and every one of those times had
been on a jetty or a boat or a queue, from somebody who was leaving.

He had known it since the first sentence in the doorway.

He said nothing about it. And an hour later, when he asked for the small
gouge and used the Kiri lower-district word for it without thinking,
because it is the only word for it that exists in that district, he watched
Haku's hand stop for about a fifth of a second on the way to the roll and
then go on and pick it up.

Neither of them said anything about that either.

There are two ways to know a thing about somebody, and one of them is to
be told, and the other is much older and much quieter and does not require
either party to admit to a single word.

---

Zabuza came out at about the fourth hour and stood on it.

He came out slowly and with a hand on the frame and he was grey round the
eyes above the bandage, and he was up, which eleven days after two
punctures in the neck and whatever was under that chest strapping was — as
Sazare noted with an entirely professional detachment that surprised him —
not normal, and not explicable, and not any of his business.

He put his weight on the top step, which was one of the two Sazare had
replaced, and bounced on it once, testing, the way you do.

"Huh," he said.

That was all. He stood on the mended boards in his own doorway with the
low sun coming up the beach into his face and looked out at the water and
the islands for about four minutes without saying anything else at all,
and Sazare, kneeling twelve feet away, watched a man who had spent eleven
days on his back in a dark room look at the sea.

Then he said, "You'll want paying."

"No."

"Boy."

"I don't want paying," said Sazare. "I've been paid this month in eleven
eggs and a bucket of clams and I'm doing all right." He knocked the last
nail flush and ran his thumb over it. "You can give me the offcuts."

"There's four foot of larch there."

"Yes."

Zabuza looked down at him for a moment.

"Take the offcuts," he said, and went inside; and paused in the door, and
said, without turning round, in the flat voice of a man mentioning the
weather to a workman: "You'd want to be gone off this coast inside a
fortnight, by the way. I've a job to finish when I'm on my feet, and when
it's finished this'll be a bad country to be a stranger in for about a
month."

"Right," said Sazare.

He did not ask. He had spent a year training himself not to ask and it
came out of him automatically now, like a hand going up in front of a
blow, and afterwards — for years — he would go back to that exact moment
on that exact veranda with the plane in his hand and the sun on the back
of his neck, and would understand that he had not been protecting anybody.
He had simply been fast.

---

He went home over the ridge with four foot of larch offcut under his arm
and his roll on his back and his knee complaining about the climb, in the
last hour of a very good day.

The path came up through the pines in a series of turns and the light came
down through the branches in long slanting bars full of midges, and there
was a smell of leaf mould and warm sap and, from somewhere below and to
the right, woodsmoke. Twice he stopped, because the knee wanted it. He was
in an entirely uncomplicated good mood. He was thinking about the
sistered joist, and about whether he could get a second-hand plane iron
in Nami and what he would have to trade for it, and about the way the old
man on the bridge had said *I built that part right* with his chin up.

He came out on the top of the ridge where the trees opened, and stopped to
get his breath, and looked west.

The sun was going down behind the mainland hills and the whole channel had
gone the colour of a struck match. Nami sat below him on the right with
the smoke standing straight up out of it in the still air. And out across
the mouth of the bay, four hundred yards of grey concrete lay on the water
with the low light on it, throwing a shadow half a mile long — the piers
black, the deck a pale line, the unfinished end squared off in mid-air
with the reinforcement rods standing up out of it and one small crane
sitting on the last of it, hand-cranked, going nowhere.

Sazare stood on the ridge with his offcuts under his arm.

*I've a job to finish when I'm on my feet.*

The mender's habit did what it always did. It did not ask permission. It
did not wait to be told the name. It laid the parts out flat on the bench
in a row, in the right order, the way they went, and every single one of
them had been lying in his tray for six weeks:

A man with his name painted six feet high on five warehouses, in good
black over two coats of primer, on a coast where a child fishes into an
empty bucket.

A country with one export, one channel, and fourteen ways in, thirteen of
which put a keel through the top of a rock.

And a bridge. A road out over the sea to the mainland, wide enough for two
carts to pass, three-quarters unbuilt, that when it opens will end the
monopoly of every hull in that man's fleet inside a season.

And a man of sixty-five with concrete in the creases of his hands and one
spectacle arm whipped with black thread, who had built fourteen sets of
forms in the water on a falling tide and struck them and rebuilt them and
not got sloppy on the ninth, and who was the only person alive who could
finish it, and who had said *come at seven, there'll be food*, and had
fed him, and had told him not to tell anybody about the pine because
*they've enough on*.

And, in a fisherman's house four miles round the north shore, sitting
against a post with a cup in both hands, a professional taking a shipping
magnate's money to buy himself a country.

It took him one second. There was no assembling to be done. It was already
assembled and had been for some time and had been sitting in the tray with
the parts lying beside each other in plain view, and the only thing he had
achieved in six weeks was not turning round and looking at it.

Sazare stood on the ridge for a long while as the light went off the water
and the bridge went from pale to grey to a black line and then to nothing
at all.

He had spent the whole of that day on his knees in the sun making a
stranger's house safe to walk on, and he had done it beautifully, and he
had refused payment; and the stranger was going to get up off that mat in
a week or so and go out and kill the old man who had fed him.

Both of them had been kind to him. Neither of them had asked him for
anything. He had no standing with either of them, no claim on either of
them, nothing to offer either of them, and no version of himself — none,
he went through them all on that ridge in the dark, the mender, the
courier, the fortifier, the man with the ledger and the five lines in it —
that could do the smallest thing about any of it.

He went down off the ridge and along the plank walk to the net loft with
the tide out under the boards and the mud giving up its cold iodine smell
into the dark, and climbed the stairs, and sat down on the mat by the
window without lighting the lamp.

At about the third hour he got up and went round the room, quietly, on his
hands and knees, feeling along the skirting and the door rebate and the
sill; and he found the places where they would go, and he put his palm
flat on the plaster under the window and left it there for a while.

Then he took it away and lay down and did not grow anything at all.
