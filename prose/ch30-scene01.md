# Chapter 30, Scene 1 — Not Mine to Give

*Act 4, part C. Konoha General. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare.
~1,679 words.*

---

The daily heads meeting happened at the seventh hour in the room behind
admissions that had been a records store until the invasion filled it
with chairs, and Sazare had walked past its door four hundred times
without ever having a reason to open it.

He had the reason now and did not like it.

"Sit down," Kaburagi said, "before you make the argument you have been
constructing since I told you the time of this meeting, because I have
heard the first two sentences of it already and they were not good."

"They were accurate."

"Accuracy was never in dispute. Sit."

He sat. The room had one long table with a scorch mark at the far end
where somebody had put a steriliser tray down without a mat, and six
chairs, and a window looking into the light well, which meant the light
in there was grey at every hour of every day regardless of what the sky
was doing. It smelled of paper and old tea and, faintly, under both, of
the laundry two doors along.

Fujita was already there with a chart he had brought to read rather
than to discuss. Emiko came in with her sleeves still rolled. Yagi
arrived four minutes late from surgical with her hair flattened where a
cap had been and sat down without apologising to anybody, which nobody
appeared to expect her to. A thin man from pharmacy whose name Sazare
had known for three months and had never once heard anybody use.
Stores sent a woman with a clipboard and an expression of settled
grievance.

Kaburagi did not sit.

"Second floor," she said. "Ruri's been running it eleven weeks. In that
time we've had no medication error, no pressure injury, no fall, and
the fastest observation compliance in the building, which I mention
because pharmacy has been complaining about the second floor's
requisition timings for two months and I want it on the record that the
timings are the only thing wrong with that floor and they are wrong
because I set them."

The thin man from pharmacy said nothing at all.

"He has also, in the same eleven weeks, developed a distributed
chakra-support device under a properly consented single-patient trial,
which worked, and which is now sitting in a dish in a quartermaster's
shed weighing half a gram and doing nothing, because the arrangement
that produced it has no name."

"It has a name," Sazare said. "It's an acting appointment with a list
of things I'm not allowed to do."

"That list was written for a boy who lifted patients and mended
trolleys. It is now being applied to a person who has an experimental
medical programme, four departments' worth of collaborators and a
consented patient with a device keyed to his own chakra." Kaburagi put
two fingers on the table. "The list is not too narrow because you have
outgrown it. The list is too narrow because we kept using it after it
stopped describing anything. That is my failure, and I am correcting
it in front of the people who would otherwise have to correct it for
me later."

---

She read the new designation off a form she had already filled in.

"Acting Ward Lead, second floor. And Medical Crystal Technician, which
is a post that did not exist before this morning because I invented it
last night and Fujita has agreed to be embarrassed by the wording
rather than argue about it."

"It's a terrible title," Fujita said, without looking up from the
chart.

"It is a terrible title that is precisely correct. Technician. Not
physician, not medic-nin, not apprentice anything. It says: this person
has expertise nobody else in this building possesses and it does not
make him qualified to decide who receives the results of it." She set
the form down where Sazare could read it. "You'll attend this meeting.
You'll answer for the floor and for the crystal programme. Your
clinical restrictions do not change — not one line of them — and I want
that said out loud in this room so that nobody at this table has to
guess about it in six weeks' time."

"They don't change," Sazare repeated.

"Say the ones that matter."

He said them. No independent diagnosis. No prescribing. No surgical
approval, no altering a physician's plan, no overruling Yagi or Fujita
on a clinical matter. And — the one that had been sitting in his chest
since she had said the word *programme* — no enrolling a patient in
anything experimental, which meant that the thing now formally
belonging to him was a thing he could not put on a single arm without
somebody else's signature.

"Good," said Kaburagi. "Now the part you're going to like less."

"I hadn't finished liking the first part less."

Emiko made a small noise into her tea.

"This appointment is above my level," Kaburagi said. "Not the ward
lead — that's mine, I can move a nurse into a ward lead post on a
Tuesday and answer for it on a Wednesday and no-one above me cares.
The technician post is not mine. It creates a permanent role in this
hospital, held by a provisional genin on a residence order, running
experimental medical work on Konoha personnel. There is exactly one
desk in this village that can sign that, and until three weeks ago
that desk was empty."

The room's grey light did not change. Sazare found, all the same, that
he had stopped hearing the laundry.

"You've sent it up," he said.

"I sent it up on Monday, with the trial file, Kanzaki's audit, Yagi's
pattern notes, Urushibara's seal notation, Fujita's eligibility
determination and the consent record with Nohara's name on it." She
straightened. "All of it. Not a summary. I have watched this village
summarise a thing into something it wasn't twice this year and I was
not going to hand her the short version and let her find the long one
by accident."

"Her."

"Her," Kaburagi agreed. "Who did you imagine ran the medical corps?"

---

He did the arithmetic in the corridor afterward, standing at the top of
the stairs with the observation boards under one arm and no immediate
memory of having picked them up.

It was not the promotion. The promotion was a title and a small
adjustment to a pay grade and one more line on a form Ashida would file
in a tray, and he had been through enough of those in eleven months to
have stopped experiencing them as events.

It was the file.

Everything he had done since the gate was in it. Not the version he
would have assembled for a hearing, which he had constructed four
separate times in his head over four separate sleepless nights, and
which was accurate and complete and arranged in the order that made him
easiest to approve. The actual file. Eleven weeks of a floor. Five
failed devices, each one dated in his own hand, including the one that
discharged in a coat pocket and the one that did nothing at all to a
living animal because he had built a lock that only opened for the
locksmith. His own note underneath attempt three: *cost Yagi forty
minutes of correctly loaded pattern to learn something I already knew.*

He had written all of it that way on purpose, because Kanzaki had told
him in a yard full of scales that an audit does not improve events, and
because he had believed her, and because he had never once considered
what it would be like to have the whole of it read by somebody whose
opinion he had not yet decided he could survive.

"You've gone a colour," Handa observed, when he reached bed eleven.

"I'm fine."

"You're grey. You were grey before the invasion and you're greyer now,
and I've been watching you come through that door twice a day for
sixteen weeks, so don't." Handa shifted himself up the pillow by an
inch, which was four weeks' worth of progress and had been the entire
achievement of his Tuesday. "Kaburagi's given you the floor properly."

"How do you know that?"

"Because two orderlies discussed it at the foot of my bed at half past
six as though I were a hatstand. Congratulations."

"It's an acting post with the same restrictions I already had."

"Congratulations," Handa said again, with emphasis. "That's what you
say. You say *thank you* and then you say something dry about the pay,
and then we move on, and I don't have to watch you take a decent thing
and file it under *administrative* to stop yourself feeling anything
about it."

Sazare put the boards down on the end of the bed.

"Thank you," he said.

"And the pay?"

"Four hundred ryō a month more, which the village will deduct back at
the next rent review."

"There he is." Handa closed his eyes, satisfied. "What's actually
bothering you, then, if it isn't the title?"

"She sent my whole file to the Hokage."

"Ah."

"All of it. The failures. The dated ones."

Handa was quiet for a moment. Somewhere down the ward, a trolley wheel
clicked once per revolution, the same trolley that had needed a bearing
since the second month and would go on needing one for as long as
nobody with authority walked past it at the same moment as somebody
with a spanner.

"Boy," Handa said. "In twenty-six years of joinery I never once had a
customer ask to see the pieces I'd got wrong. Not once. And every
single one of them would have been better off looking at those than at
the finished chair, because the chair only tells you what I can do on a
good day, and the pile behind the shed tells you what I do when it
isn't going right."

"That isn't how institutions read files."

"No," Handa agreed. "But she isn't an institution, she's a woman with
a desk, and if she's any good at all she'll go straight past the chair
and start on the pile."
