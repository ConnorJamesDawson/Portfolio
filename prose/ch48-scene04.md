# Chapter 48, Scene 4 — The Fourth Hour

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the Hokage's tower. Tsunade, age fifty-three.
POV: Tsunade. ~2,919 words.*

---

It was nothing like medicine and it was nothing like anything.

She got both hands round the wrist above the line and reached in, and
there was no process to find and nothing to get behind, and after about
ninety seconds of hunting for the thing she had hunted for in eleven
thousand people she understood that she was going to have to do it the
stupid way.

The stupid way was: take hold of it and pull.

Not chakra. It was not chakra and she knew that inside the first
minute, because chakra had a *grain* to it — everybody's did, she could
identify four hundred people by it — and this had none. It was
completely uniform in every direction and it was heavy in a way that
had nothing to do with weight, and there was an enormous amount of it
in a volume the size of a hand.

She took some.

It came, slowly, at whatever speed it came at, exactly as the slug had
said, and it went into her, and there was no unpleasantness about it
at all and that was the frightening part, because a thing that hurts
tells you when to stop.

*Capacity, and the rate's fixed.*

*He worked that out in eleven seconds off a slug who couldn't explain
it, and he is sitting four feet away with a pencil and he has no idea
that he has just written the operating procedure for the next six
hours.*

---

She filled up at about the fortieth minute.

There was no warning and there was no sensation of nearing anything.
There was simply a point at which the next amount did not go, the way a
foot does not go into a boot that is already occupied, and she stopped
and looked at her own arms and found that she could not entirely feel
the ends of her fingers.

*Right.*

She got up and went to the window and opened it and put both hands flat
on the stone sill, and put it back.

That worked. She did not know why it worked and she had not expected it
to, and it went out of her into a tower wall the way water goes into
sand, unhurried, taking about four minutes, and when it was done she
could feel her fingers again and her hands were shaking slightly and
she was furious about it.

"You've gone to the window," said Sazare, behind her.

"I'm putting it somewhere."

"Where?"

"The wall."

There was a short silence.

"Then it's the ground," he said. "It came out of the ground and it's
going back into the ground and this whole building is sitting on the
oldest piece of rock inside the wall. That's why it takes it. Can I—"

"You can write it down and you can shut up for four minutes," said
Tsunade, "because I am putting my hands back on your arm shortly and I
would like to arrive there without an argument in my head."

"Yes."

"...And yes, it's the ground. Obviously it's the ground."

She came back and sat down and took hold of the wrist again.

---

He talked for the next five hours.

She had not asked him to. She had been most of the way through the
second unload before she understood that it was deliberate — that he
had worked out, somewhere in the first hour, that she was going to be
holding onto him until dawn and could not do anything else and could not
leave and could not, in any useful sense, reply.

And that he had eleven pages in a drawer that nobody in the world could
take.

*You appalling boy,* she thought, at about two o'clock, with her hands
round his wrist and her eyes shut. *You have got me for six hours and I
cannot get up.*

He gave her the whole of it.

The splint paper and the subordinate clause. Ochiai and four years of
declining to keep a list. The eleven-year-old's marginalia — *don't get
this*, and then *OH* — and a boy called Tarumi Ichirō who had made
chūnin and died at the Crush and whose mother had given the books back.
The two hundred and nine dishes and the fracture surfaces and the bulb
and the break in a lamp housing in Hot Water when he was thirteen. The
hundred and eight hours of nothing to report. The note. Four voices and
Handa's three and the exact evening in his fifties when a joiner had
stopped being able to hear a saw go off true.

Forty-one seconds and a left deltoid.

Eleven hundred and forty kilos and a woman of seventy-two who was going
to write *unknown* three hundred and eleven times in her own evenings.

A man with nothing on him he had chosen, and a button he had whittled
rather than grown.

Twelve roses on a table and a hundred and thirty-four grams and a sack
scale that read to fifty.

Nine years against six days.

---

She stopped him twice.

Once at about half past two, to say, "Say the bit about the button
again," and he said it again, and she said, "Right," and nothing else,
and put her hands back.

And once at about four, when he had got to the invasion streets and the
chalk squares and the eleven readings across four hundred yards, and
she said:

"You went and measured a battlefield."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Because he asked me what was in the ground," said Sazare, "and it was
the only end of it I could pull on by myself, and I had nine days and
nobody, and going to look at a road is a thing a person can do
alone."

Tsunade kept her eyes shut.

*Nine days and nobody.*

*And the reason he had nobody is that I was at the capital arguing
about a border, and Shizune sent him a slip asking whether it was a
fire, and he wrote "not a fire" on the back and sent it down, and it
was not a fire.*

*He was right. It was not a fire.*

*And here I am at four in the morning holding his arm.*

She did not say any of that. She said, "Go on," and he went on.

---

Somewhere in the fourth hour she noticed that she was enjoying it.

That was the one she had no defence against and it arrived without any
warning at all, in the middle of a passage about conductor gauge, when
she caught herself waiting — actively, impatiently, the way you wait
for the next line of something — for him to get to how he had worked
out that the pitch fell as the ways widened.

She had known it was going to be pitch. She had known four sentences
before he said it and she had been *pleased about knowing*, and she had
been sitting in a scrubbed room with her hands round a stone hand
grinning at a wall like a woman of nineteen.

*Oh, no.*

Tsunade had spent a very long time being extremely careful about a
short list of things.

She did not gamble on things she wanted, because wanting them made her
worse at reading the odds. She did not learn people's birthdays. She
did not go to the memorial. She did not take students, and then she had
taken one, and had spent three months constructing a solid professional
architecture around the taking of her that would not have survived nine
seconds of honest examination.

And underneath the whole apparatus, load-bearing, unexamined since she
was about twenty-four, was the actual rule, which was: *do not be
interested in how things turn out.*

Because that was the whole of it. Grief was not the mechanism. Grief was
the invoice. The mechanism was that at some point you started wanting
to find out what happened next — to a person, to a village, to a
stupid brilliant unfinished thing on a bench — and once you wanted to
find out, you were in it, and the world could take it off you.

She had been very good at this. Forty years.

And she was sitting in a chair at four in the morning with her hands
full of something she could not name, waiting to hear how a boy had
worked out a curve off a tower bell.

*I have been doing this since about the third month.*

*He put a stack of paper on my floor and I have been doing it ever
since and I have been calling it the work.*

She did not follow it any further, because there was another two hours
of arm to get through and she was not going to do the rest of that
particular arithmetic at four in the morning with the subject of it
four feet away describing a fishmonger's boots.

She put it where she put things.

It did not go, entirely, and she noticed that too, and put that
somewhere as well.

---

At about half past five he got it, and she was holding his arm and
could not do a single thing about it.

"It was drinking," he said.

She had her eyes shut. "What?"

"The rose. The whole time it was growing, and after it had stopped
growing, right up until I broke the stem. It was taking in. Continuously.
That's not a fault and it isn't the injury — that is simply what a
living thing does, it stands somewhere and it takes in from where it is
standing, and it does not stop, ever, until it is dead."

"Ruri, I am going to need you to—"

"A plant is *built by drawing*." He was not listening and she knew,
distantly, that he had stopped being able to. "Every part of it. There
is no part of a tree that was not made out of something that came in
from outside — that is the entire method of a plant, from the first
cell, and it means that every part of it is a *way in*. The structure
is the intake. It isn't a container with an intake attached, the
container *is* the intake."

Tsunade opened her eyes.

"And mine isn't," said Sazare.

"...No."

"Mine is laid. I put the whole of it in at the start, all of it, in a
second and a half or in seven hours, and then—"

He stopped.

She felt him stop. His arm was in her hands and something went through
it that had nothing to do with what she was doing.

"And then I close it," he said.

---

She could not move.

That was the thing she thought about afterwards more than any other
part of the night: that she had both hands full of an unknown substance
being drawn out of a boy's wrist at a fixed rate she could not alter,
and could not let go of it, and could not stand up, and could not do
anything at all except sit there while he said the following out loud to
a scrubbed table at twenty to six in the morning.

"Six days," he said. "The stones. The order slips before the quantity
does — I measured it fifty times out to nine days and I put three days
on the label and I have spent fourteen months calling it degradation
and treating it as a property of the medium."

"It isn't."

"It's a *battery*. It's a sealed jar. I put in everything it will ever
have and then I shut the lid, automatically, without knowing I'm doing
it, and it spends what's in it and then there is nothing left and the
order has nothing to stand up in. Six days is not a decay rate. It's a
capacity divided by a rate of use, and I could have worked that out in
the fourth month if I had ever once asked why anything I made was
finite."

"And his rose is nine years old."

"His rose is nine years old and dead and *porous*," said Sazare, "and
whatever it drew, it drew through an architecture that is still shaped
like an architecture for drawing, and I do not know whether it is still
taking in — I would have to leave one in a dead place and one in an old
place for a year — but I know that mine can't, because I sealed it."

Tsunade sat with her hands round his wrist.

"Say the last part," she said.

"Which last part?"

"You know exactly which."

There was a pause of about four seconds.

"The closing move," said Sazare. "The one that didn't fire tonight and
put a stone hand on the end of my arm. That's the same move. It's the
same automatic thing, done at the end of every course I have ever laid
since I was seven, and it is the reason nothing I make lasts."

"So."

"So the thing that protects me is the thing that kills everything I
build," he said, "and they are not two mechanisms that happen to be
related, they are one habit, and if I want an article that does not run
out then I have to not close it — which is precisely the thing that
happened to me at half past ten last night."

---

Tsunade looked at the window, where it was starting to be light.

*And that,* she thought, *is the biggest single piece of work anybody
has done in my hospital in two years, and he has just said it out loud
to a table, at twenty to six, with one hand, and there is nobody in
this building who will ever know he said it.*

*And I cannot write a word of it down.*

She made herself say the other half, because she was a medic before she
was anything else and because if she did not say it now she would say
it worse in a week.

"An unsealed article is an open route," she said.

"...Yes."

"In both directions. You have spent one night finding out what comes
through an open route into a person, and you are proposing to build one
deliberately and put it in a *ward*, into people with nothing left, and
I want you to have heard me say that before you have had any sleep,
because in about nine hours you are going to be extremely excited and
you will not hear it then."

"I've heard it."

"Say it back."

"An open article can be filled from outside," said Sazare, "which is
the point, and it can also fill something else from outside, which is
not, and the second one is a door in a body and I do not currently have
a way to make it one-way."

"Right," said Tsunade. "Good. Hold onto that when you don't want to."

---

The last hour was bad.

She emptied at the window for the fifth time at about half past five
and it did not go out of her properly — it went slowly, and left
something behind, and when she came back to the table her hands were
shaking badly enough that she stopped and put them flat on the wood for
a moment before she picked up his wrist.

She got the line down to the base of the metacarpals and then to the
carpus and then she hit the thing she had been waiting for since two
o'clock and had not mentioned.

It stopped coming.

Not slowed. There was a quantity in the deep tissue of the palm and the
base of the first two fingers that simply did not move, and she went at
it for forty minutes from three angles and got nothing, and at about
twenty past six, with the first grey coming up behind the tower and a
boy on a stool who had been awake for twenty-four hours and had not
once asked, she stopped.

"That's it," she said.

He looked at his hand.

The fingers were his again — colour, warmth, the nail beds coming back
the way they should — and there was a patch on the heel of the palm and
running up into the base of the index and middle, perhaps the size of a
large coin and going deep, that was not.

It was pale. Not stone-grey. Something between horn and quartz, matte,
with the skin whorls still perfectly legible across the surface of it,
and it was warm to her hand because the tissue round it was warm.

"How much movement," she said.

He made a fist. It made most of a fist.

"That's about eighty," he said. "Maybe eighty-five. It's the last
fifteen degrees of the index and there's — " He pressed the patch with
his other thumb. "There's nothing there. It's not numb, numb is a
sensation. There's just no report."

"I can't get it out."

"I know."

"I have been trying for forty minutes and I am not going to be able
to, and I want to say that out loud in front of a witness so that
neither of us spends the next four years wondering whether somebody
better could have," said Tsunade. "There isn't anybody better. That's
not arrogance, it's a very small field."

Sazare turned his hand over in the grey light coming up behind the
window.

"Hime," he said.

"Mm."

"It's a medium."

Tsunade put her face in her hands.
