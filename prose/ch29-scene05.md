# Chapter 29, Scene 5 — Almost Nothing

*Act 4, part C. Konoha General, rear yard. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen.
POV: Sazare. ~1,213 words.*

---

Kanzaki weighed the spent chip on the Thursday, the way she weighed
everything, on the small brass balance rather than either of the
larger scales, because a thing that had discharged completely no
longer needed a scale built for anything heavier than itself.

It came in under half a gram. Grit, colourless, indistinguishable by
eye from the granite it had started as, the store empty and the
notation with it, both spent in the same four seconds five days
earlier that Sazare had felt only as an absence, a door opening
somewhere he could not follow through.

"Half a gram," Kanzaki said, writing the figure down. "Less than the
seed you grew it from."

"It doesn't survive its own discharge. Urushibara's seal on the seal
holds the store shut until it fires, and firing is the last thing it
does."

"So this is not a device. It's a single use, entered once, spent
once, and gone." She set it in a labelled dish beside the ledger.
"You've made the least reusable thing you've ever brought me."

"I know."

"I'm not saying that as a criticism." She looked up, and there was
something in her face he had learned, over eleven months of
Thursdays, to recognise as the closest she came to approval without
actually deploying the word. "Every weapon-form fragment I have ever
weighed on this scale, I have weighed with one eye on what it could
do to somebody. This is the first thing you've made that I weighed
without that thought occurring to me once."

She turned the ledger round so he could see the entry: *0.5 g,
consumed, single use, patient N.D., outcome recorded.* Beside it,
in the column she had added for the crystal doctrine three months
before he had ever brought her a reason to need it, a single word:
*medical.*

"It's worth more to me than every weapon-form crystal I've ever
audited," she said, closing the book, "and it weighs almost nothing
at all. I'd sit with that a while, if I were you. It's not a
comfortable thought and it shouldn't be a quick one."

---

Yagi found him afterward in the rear yard, where the laundry lines
still crossed above the kitchen roof the way they had every day since
before the invasion, and sat on the low wall beside him without being
asked.

"Daichi's graft is healing clean," she said. "No further reopening in
five days. Whatever the eight seconds bought him, it bought the tissue
enough peace to finish the job it was already trying to do."

"That's the honest outcome, or the flattering one?"

"Both, this time. It happens occasionally." She looked at him
sideways. "You want to build more of these."

"Yes."

"How many people."

"I don't know yet."

"That's the correct answer, and I want you to notice you gave it
without me prompting you this time." She folded her arms against the
evening cooling off the yard stone. "One patient, one consent, one
result. That's what we have. It is not a programme, it is not a
ward-wide trial, and it is not going to become either of those things
because it worked once and you're impatient. Stage two happens when
somebody above both of us decides it happens, on evidence, not on
enthusiasm."

"I understand."

"I believe you do. I'm saying it anyway, the way I say most of the
things that matter most, because understanding a rule and wanting to
keep it the first time it's inconvenient are not the same muscle, and
you've never had much practice with the second one."

He sat with that, the light going long and gold across the yard the
way it had the night he'd charged the granite chip alone in his room,
a month and five days ago now, though it felt considerably longer than
that when he tried to count it honestly.

"Kanzaki called it the first thing I've made she didn't weigh with one
eye on what it could do to somebody," he said.

"She's right."

"I wanted to tell somebody that. Not enter it in a ledger. Say it out
loud, to a person, and watch their face while I said it."

Yagi did not answer immediately, which he had learned to read, by now,
as her deciding whether a silence or a sentence would serve him
better.

"You just did," she said, eventually. "To me. That counts, even if I
wasn't the person you were picturing when the wanting started."

He looked at her, and she looked back with the particular level
patience she brought to every observation she judged too large to
argue with directly.

"I didn't say I was picturing anyone."

"No," Yagi said. "You didn't." She stood, brushing the yard dust from
her sleeves, and did not press it further, which he understood,
correctly, as a kindness rather than an oversight. "Write it up
properly tonight. Both halves, the way you always do. And then come
and tell me tomorrow whether you slept, because I already know the
answer and I'd like to hear you say it honestly for once."

---

He wrote it up at the low table that evening, the persimmon well into
leaf now and the evenings still losing light from the wrong end, and
copied the whole of it out in order, exactly as it had happened,
without softening either half.

*Twenty-nine days from first attempt to first use. Five failures, each
one necessary. One patient, fully informed, fully consenting, able to
withdraw at any point and choosing not to. One discharge, four
seconds, felt at a distance and not otherwise. Outcome: bleeding
slowed, not stopped; time bought, not cured; the patient's own healing
given room to finish what it was already doing.*

*This is not better medicine. It is medicine that does not require a
person to be in the room.*

*Six people in this hospital know exactly what this device is worth
and none of them are impressed by the crystal. They are impressed by
the fact that nobody had to be asked to trust it more than the exact
amount they'd already earned the right to be trusted.*

He stopped, pencil above the page, and considered the last line for a
while before he let himself write it, because it was the kind of
sentence that could tip, with one careless word, from an observation
into a wink at nobody — an assertion of significance dressed up as a
fact, the exact failure he audited himself against every time the
flattering half of a ledger started sounding a little too pleased with
itself.

He decided, on balance, that it was true rather than performed, and
left it standing.

*Kanzaki weighed it at half a gram. It is the lightest thing I have
ever made and the only one I have ever wanted someone else to know
about before I finished counting the cost.*

He closed the notebook, put the tin away — the six grains from the
invasion still in it, untouched, kept separate from anything he grew
new for a bench — and sat for a while with the lamp guttering and the
wanting still there, unresolved, uncategorised, exactly as it had been
the night the chip first went warm in his hand and he had not, for
once, minded not having a word for it.
