# Chapter 44, Scene 2 — Nine Pages on the Approach

*Act 4, part D. Konoha General, the fourth-floor landing. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,573 words.*

---

He took the courtship book out of the drawer on the eleventh, after
four months of it being in there, and read the nine pages on the
etiquette of the approach in about twenty minutes.

They were useless.

They were not badly written. The man had plainly thought about it a
great deal, in the same flat prose as the other book, and there were
observations in it that were true — that an approach made in front of
an audience obliges the other party to answer for the audience rather
than for themselves; that a question is a smaller demand than a
statement; that the person who names the thing takes the risk and
should therefore be prepared for the whole of it.

All of that was procedure. Every line of it assumed you had already
arrived at the point where procedure applied.

Nine pages on how to approach. Not one line on why anybody would want
to.

---

So he did what he did, which was to go and get the part he was good at.

He was good at anatomy. He had been good at it for four years, in a way
that nobody in that building except Yagi had ever been mildly surprised
by, because he had come to it out of jewellery and stonework and had
therefore learned it as *structure* rather than as a list — a face was
not a set of features to him, it was a load path, and he could tell you
what a cheekbone was for and what it did to the skin over it at forty
and at seventy and what a broken one healed like.

If attraction was a response to a physical arrangement, then he had the
best vocabulary for physical arrangements in the room, and the problem
was tractable.

He ruled a sheet and wrote a specification.

He built it out of general observation rather than his own, because his
own was the thing under test and using it as an input would have been
the exact error he audited himself for. So: eleven years of listening
to men talk in workshops, in guardrooms, in a hospital canteen with
four hundred and eleven staff in it, and nine pages of a book, and a
village whose men had opinions about the Hokage they expressed in a
lowered voice at the second table and which he had catalogued at the
time as noise.

*Symmetry.*

*Ratio of the facial thirds.*

*Skin condition, i.e. an absence of visible history.*

*Youth, expressed as the absence of the specific changes I have listed
elsewhere and can date to within four years.*

*Secondary sexual characteristics, in the proportions most frequently
remarked on aloud in this building, which I have recorded and will not
be writing out because I am not going to have that sheet in my room.*

He looked at the list for a while and then began to test it.

---

It failed in about eleven minutes and it failed completely.

Every criterion on the sheet was present, in high measure, in at least
four people he could name who had never once produced the effect the
sheet was supposed to be predicting. Some of them worked on his ward.
One of them had brought irises to a committee room every Monday for
eleven years out of her own money and he had never in his life thought
about her for a second longer than the conversation lasted.

And when he ran it the other way — when he took the two people who *did*
produce it and checked them against the specification — the result was
worse.

Kurihara Saeko scored badly on symmetry, had the permanent frown of
close work, had a burn across the heel of her left hand shaped like
something that had gone off while she held it flat, and had the
narrowed eyes of somebody reading at eleven inches since she was small.
Nothing on the sheet predicted her.

The other one failed almost every line of it, in a direction the sheet
had no notation for at all — because the sheet's fourth criterion was
*youth expressed as the absence of specific changes*, and the subject
presented an apparent age of about thirty against a real one he could
not establish and which was categorically higher, and the entire
apparatus was a technique, and knowing it was a technique changed
nothing whatsoever about the result.

He did not write the second name down.

He wrote *Subject B* and then looked at what he had done, and crossed
out *Subject B*, and wrote:

*I have just given a person a letter instead of a name in my own
private notebook that nobody will ever read, and I would like to record
that I noticed doing it, because the reason I did it is not
methodological.*

---

*The specification does not predict anything,* he wrote, at the bottom.

*It is not a bad specification. It is a correct description of a
category — the criteria are real, people do respond to them, and I can
find nine hundred pieces of evidence in this building. It predicts
which people a room will look at.*

*It predicts nothing about which person I cannot stop thinking about.*

*Those are two different measurements and I have spent an evening
building an instrument for the first one under the impression I was
building one for the second.*

---

He took it to Sakura on the landing on the thirteenth, because she was
his instructor on this and had been since the second morning, and
because there was nobody else in the world he could show that sheet to.

She read the whole of it standing up, in the way she read everything,
which was fast and twice.

"Right," she said. "Two things. The first one's the marking and you're
going to hate it."

"Go on."

"That was a lecture." She held the sheet up. "Criterion two. You came
up here with eleven hundred words of prepared material and handed it to
me, and there's no door in it anywhere. I've had it for ninety seconds
and you've said four words. Two out of ten, and the two is for showing
me the failure rather than the finished version, which most people
wouldn't."

"That's fair."

"It's *generous*. The second thing's the actual thing." She sat down on
the step. "Your specification isn't wrong. It's answering a different
question and you've written the question at the top of the page
yourself. Look."

He looked.

*What makes a person attractive.*

"That's a question about a **category**," said Sakura. "That's — that's
the question the whole village is asking, all the time, out loud, at
the second table, and it's got an answer and the answer is boring and
everybody already knows it and it has never once been useful to
anybody."

"And the question I want."

"The question you want is *why that one.*" She handed the sheet back.
"And there's no specification for that, because it isn't a property of
the person. It can't be. If it was a property of the person then
everybody would want the same one, and the entire village would be
standing in one queue, and it obviously isn't."

Sazare sat down on the step below her, which put his head at about the
height of her knee, and did not say anything for some seconds.

"Say that again," he said.

"Which bit?"

"*It isn't a property of the person.*"

"It isn't a property of the person," said Sakura, slowly, because she
had worked out by now when he was doing this. "It's — it's what happens
between a particular person and a particular other person. It's not in
either of them. It's in the — I don't know the word."

"I do."

He was already getting the notebook out with the wrong hand and
transferring it, which was a thing he had started doing about two weeks
ago and had not examined.

*Not a property of the object.*

*Not a property of the receiver.*

*In the taking-up.*

"That's my four per cent," he said. "That is my four per cent, written
out by a fourteen-year-old on a staircase, in a completely different
material, and I have had the arithmetic on my own bench for three
months."

"I'm fifteen next month."

"That's criterion four and I'm stopping."

"No," said Sakura. "No, actually, go on, because you've gone a
peculiar colour and I want to know what the four per cent is."

So he told her, on the stairs, for eleven minutes, about a reject table
and a source column he had filled in three hundred times without
reading and a material that takes up an instruction faster if it has
held one before.

And she listened to the whole of it, and got there about four seconds
before he finished, and said:

"So if it's the same law, then the reason it's *that one* is—"

And Sazare said, "Don't."

And Sakura, who was fourteen for another three weeks and had more sense
than most of the people in that building, looked at him for a moment
and then said, "All right," and let it go, and marked him a six for the
second half and wrote *found a door, went through it, stopped when it
was finished* in the box, and did not write down the other thing at
all.
