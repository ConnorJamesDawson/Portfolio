# Chapter 18, Scene 4 — Handed Down

*Act 3, part A. The north shore. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare.
~3,400 words.*

---

The door opened before he had a hand on the rail.

The boy stood in it with the light behind him and a cloth in one hand, and
he was smiling, and the smile was so completely disarming that Sazare
found himself smiling back before he had made any decision about it, which
frightened him more than a drawn weapon would have.

Close up he was perhaps fifteen, and slight, and about Sazare's height,
with a face that was — there was no other word available and Sazare tried
several — *lovely*: fine-boned, wide dark eyes with long lashes, a small
straight nose, skin the pale even brown of somebody who works outdoors in
a cold country. His hair was very black and hung to his shoulders, tied
back with a plain band, with two shorter pieces left loose at the front.
He was barefoot on the boards. His hands were wet to the wrist and he had
a smear of something green on the back of one of them, and his sleeves
were tied up with a cord, and there was a thin white scar across the base
of his left thumb of the kind you get from a knife slipping in a wet fish.

"You've brought planks," he said.

"Six. Larch. Eight foot."

"We haven't ordered any planks."

"I know," said Sazare. "It's not an order. Your veranda's got eleven
boards missing and two more that'll go through the first time somebody
heavy stands on them, and the joists underneath are sound, which is
lucky, because if the joists were gone I'd have had to bring a lot more
than six planks and I'd have wanted paying."

The boy looked at him.

The smile did not go anywhere. That was the thing about it. It stayed
exactly where it was, warm and open and entirely pleasant, and behind it
something else came up to the window and looked out and went away again,
and Sazare stood at the bottom of a set of steps on a shingle beach with
his hands loose at his sides and understood, with total clarity, that he
had been about two words from dying and that he had no idea which two.

"You're the mender from Nami," said the boy.

"Yes."

"Four miles."

"It's flat."

"It is not flat," said the boy, "and you've something wrong with your left
knee." He said it kindly. He said everything kindly; it was the medium he
came in. "Why?"

And Sazare, who had spent two years learning to give people the answer
that closed a conversation, heard himself say the true one instead,
because he had four hours of walking behind him and there was nothing else
left in the bag.

"Because I couldn't think of a way to walk up to this house that wasn't
frightening," he said, "and a man with a cart isn't frightening."

There was a pause about a second and a half long.

Then the boy laughed — a real one, surprised out of him, with his hand
going up in front of his mouth — and stood back off the doorway.

"No," he agreed. "A man with a cart isn't frightening." He put his head
back through the door and said, to the dark inside, in a completely
different register, flat and quick: "It's the boy from the town. He's
brought decking."

And from inside the house, in a voice like a barrel going down a flight of
stairs, somebody said:

"Then let him in, and shut the door, you're letting the fire out."

---

The room took up most of the house and it had been a fisherman's room and
had been a sickroom for eleven days, and it smelled of both.

Woodsmoke, first, from an open hearth in the middle of the floor with an
iron kettle on a chain over it and a bed of ash a foot deep. Then the
green medicinal reek of the decoction, which he identified before he was
over the threshold and which was being made properly, in a covered pot, at
the edge of the ash rather than over the flame. Then, under those, the two
smells you get in a room where a big man has been lying still for a week
and a half — old sweat gone flat, and the specific sweetish iron of
dressings that have been changed often but not quite often enough.

The floor was earth, beaten hard and swept. There were two windows of oiled
paper, one of them mended with a strip of cloth, and the low horizontal sun
came through them the colour of weak tea and lay in two long bars across
the room with the smoke turning slowly in them.

Against the far wall, propped, taller than Sazare, was the sword.

He had known it was going to be there and he had still not been ready for
the size of it. It stood on its point with its back against the plaster
and it went up past his own head: a single slab of steel with a great
half-moon bitten out of one side near the top and a round hole through it
near the base, the whole thing broad as a door plank and grey as a rain
cloud, with a wrapped handle a foot and a half long and a smell of oil
coming off it. There was no guard. There was no scabbard anywhere in the
room.

And in the middle of the floor, on a mat, with a folded quilt behind him
and his back against the wall post and one knee up, was the man himself.

He was enormous. Not fat; long — long thighs, long arms, a chest like a
door, and even sitting down and eleven days into a wound he took up a
quantity of the room that had nothing to do with his actual dimensions.
His hair was black and cut short and stuck up in stiff spikes with no
particular arrangement to it. His skin was very pale for a man who lived
outdoors, the pale of the far north-west, and there was a long old scar
running up over his right shoulder and disappearing into the bandage. He
had no eyebrows at all, which Sazare had heard about and had put down to
gossip, and which turned out to be simply true and gave the whole upper
half of his face a permanently unimpressed look.

The lower half was wrapped. Bandage, wound twice round from under the jaw
to the bridge of the nose, grey with use, so that all of him below the eyes
was a shape rather than a face. There was fresh linen strapped round his
chest and across one shoulder, and two small square dressings on the side
of his neck, an inch apart, which Sazare looked at for slightly too long,
because they were not sword wounds and were not blade wounds of any sort
he had ever seen.

He was drinking something out of a cup with both hands, like a man at a
temple, and his eyes came up over the rim and went down Sazare from the
hair to the boots and back up in about a second and a half, and he lowered
the cup.

"Sit down," said Zabuza. "You're standing in my light."

---

Sazare sat down on the earth floor a respectful distance from the fire,
with his bad leg out straight in front of him because it would not do
anything else, and for a while nobody said anything at all.

Haku moved about behind him. Water, the pot, the small domestic noises of
a person who has organised a household out of nothing and keeps it going
around a sick man without being asked. He put a cup down by Sazare's knee
without a word and went away again.

"Eleven boards," said Zabuza at last.

"And two that'll go."

"The one at the top of the steps."

"That's one of the two."

"Mm." Zabuza put the cup down on the mat beside him. His hands were the
biggest Sazare had ever seen on a person and the knuckles were flat and
spread across the back the way they go on men who have hit things for
thirty years. "I nearly went through it on Tuesday. Would've been a fine
thing." A short exhalation through the bandage; it might have been a
laugh. "So. You walked four miles round the shore with a cart full of
timber to mend a veranda nobody asked you to mend, at a house nobody in
that town will go near, for a man they're all saying is dead."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Because I wanted to look at you," said Sazare.

The room went quiet in a way that had a floor to it. Behind him the small
noises stopped.

Zabuza's eyes did not move.

"Say the rest of it."

"I'm from the Land of Water," said Sazare. "Kiri. The lower district off
the fish market — the one with the drying frames. I got out when I was
thirteen. I'm in the book." He kept his hands flat on his knees where they
could be seen, because a man on the floor with his hands on his knees is a
man who is not going to do anything. "I've been in the trade for a year.
I'm not good at it and I know exactly how not-good at it I am. And I've
been hearing about you in inns since I was seven years old and I've never
once been in the same country as you, and this time I was, and I wanted to
see what it looks like at the far end. That's the whole of it. There isn't
a second reason."

Zabuza looked at him for a long moment.

Then he said, to the ceiling, with enormous weariness:

"Haku. Sit down, he's not going to do anything, he's *sixteen*."

---

"Lower district," said Zabuza. "Off the fish market."

"Yes."

"I've been down there." He shifted against the post and something in the
chest strapping caught him and he stopped for a second and went on as
though it had not. "Bought a fried thing off a woman on the corner by the
wall. She short-changed me and I let her, because I was twenty and I
thought that made me a hell of a fellow."

He did not remember.

Sazare had known, on the ridge, lying in the bracken, that he would not. He
had told himself so and had believed it and had come anyway, and it still
went through him like cold water — a man who had put a hand out and shoved
him bodily off a corner into a doorway and said *go home* in a voice that
had made him do it, in a street with people running in it, eleven years
ago, and it had been the largest thing that had happened to Sazare Ruri in
his whole childhood and it had been, for Zabuza Momochi, a Tuesday.

"Yes," said Sazare. "That's the corner."

And left it there.

"Right." Zabuza settled his shoulders against the post. "Now shut up a
minute and I'll tell you what you are, because you've come four miles for
it and I've been lying on this floor for eleven days with nothing to look
at."

"All right."

"You're a jobbing killer with a good cover and no training." He said it
the way a man says a river is wet. "The cover's real, which is why it
works — you're not pretending to mend things, you actually mend things,
and that's worth more than any false paper anybody ever bought. You do
your own scouting and you're good at it, better than most, because you
walked in here knowing what herbs I'm drinking, and there's about four
ways you could have got that and all of them took a fortnight.

"You've no jutsu. None. You've never been taught a hand seal in your life
or you wouldn't sit like that." A flick of two fingers at Sazare's flat
hands. "You've had somebody teach you a sword, badly, or well but late,
and you've not got one on you, which means you either lost it or you're
sensible. You've been hurt properly twice — the knee's a boot and the ribs
were somebody who knew where to put them — and both of them are older than
this year, and you're still walking about, which is the only genuinely
interesting thing about you.

"And you're carrying." He nodded at Sazare's coat. "Left side, lining,
sewn in. You've been sitting on it the whole time you've been in this
room."

Sazare said nothing, which was an answer.

"That's the lot," said Zabuza. "There's nothing else. You're not a
mystery, boy; you're a *type*. There's about four hundred of you between
here and Iron and you all turn up in the same shape, and I could pick any
one of you out of a market in a minute and a half."

It should have been humiliating. Sazare sat on an earth floor with his
hands on his knees and waited to be humiliated, and it did not arrive, and
what arrived instead was something else entirely: because in a year and a
half nobody had said out loud what he was, and he had begun in some
private way to suspect that this was because it was unspeakable, and here
was a man in a bandage saying it in forty seconds with his cup in his hand
and being *bored*.

There were four hundred of him. He was a type.

He had not known, until it was taken off him, how heavy it had been to
think he was the only one.

---

The sword he asked about because he could not not.

"Can I look at it?"

"No."

"All right." He looked at it anyway, from where he was, because that had
not been forbidden. "Who made it?"

The eyebrowless face came round.

"What?"

"Who made it. It's — " Sazare stopped, and started again, and heard his
own voice go into the register it went into over a bad seam or a pillar a
quarter-turn out, and could not do anything about that either. "It's a
single piece. That's what I can't get past. It's four feet of blade and
about eight inches across at the widest and there's no fold line I can see
from here and no weld, and I don't know how you'd even *heat* that. You'd
need a hearth like a shed. And the cut-out at the top isn't a break,
it's meant, it's got a finished edge on it — somebody took a bite out of
a finished blade on purpose and then dressed it — and there's a hole
through the base big enough to put your arm through, and none of it's
decoration, all of it's *for* something." He came back to himself and
stopped. "Sorry."

Zabuza was looking at him with an expression that was extremely hard to
read on a man with no eyebrows and half a face.

"Nobody knows who made it," he said.

"Nobody?"

"It's older than the village." He said it without any reverence at all; it
was a fact about a tool. "There's seven of them. They're older than the
Mist, most of them, and there's not a name attached to one, and there's no
smith alive who could make another and there hasn't been for two hundred
years, and that's not me being poetic, that's the plain state of it — we
lost how. So they don't get made." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on the
mat. "They get handed down."

"Handed down."

"Holder to holder. You get it off the last man." A pause exactly long
enough. "I got this one off Kuriarare Kushimaru, on a beach, in the ninth
month, when I was twenty-six, and he'd got it off somebody else, and when
somebody takes it off me they'll have got it off me, and in eighty years
some other poor bastard will be standing on some other beach explaining
where it came from. That's the whole of it. It's not a ceremony. It's a
*chain*, and the chain doesn't care who's on it."

Sazare sat very still on an earth floor in a fisherman's house on a
shingle beach in a country he had been in for six weeks.

*Handed down.*

Nine years old, on the step outside the workroom in the fog, with his
mother's frames going and the whole house smelling of hot copper, and his
grandmother's voice saying *when you graduate* — and a scroll on a high
shelf behind a lattice door, tied with a cord, that four hundred years of
his family had passed hand to hand down a line, and that he had been
promised, and that had gone into a fire or a strongbox or a collector's
catalogue in a district that no longer existed, unopened, without ever
once having been in his hands.

Nobody had handed him anything. Not one thing, in his whole life. He had
made everything he owned or bought it or been given it by strangers who
felt sorry for him.

He wanted it so much, sitting on that floor, that it went through him like
a hot wire from the sternum to the back of the throat, and he kept his
face absolutely still, and he put it in the tray, and he did not say one
word about it then or for a very long time afterwards.

---

"Can I ask you a thing?"

"You've asked me nine."

"Does it end?"

Zabuza looked at him.

Outside, the tide was coming up the shingle in small rushes, and the light
through the paper had gone from tea-colour to a dull rose, and Haku moved
across the far end of the room and set a lamp going and the room came in
closer round the fire.

"Two doors," said Zabuza. "That's it. That's all there's ever been and any
man who tells you there's a third is selling you something.

"First door: you get slow. That's what it is, every time — it's never a
better man, it's you at forty doing the thing that worked at thirty. And
you go down in a field somewhere with your name on a page and a number
under it, and whatever you were saving for, somebody else spends.

"Second door: you get a *village*." He said the word with no warmth and no
irony either, the way a man says the name of a country he intends to
invade. "You stop being a man with a price and become a man with a stamp.
Same work. Same hours. Only when you do it, it's policy, and there's a
building behind you, and they feed your people, and nobody puts your face
in a book."

"And you're going through the second one."

"I'm going through the second one."

"With that man's money."

The eyes came up.

Sazare had said it flatly, without any accusation in it at all, because
there had been none in him — it was arithmetic and he had done it on the
way over — and Zabuza held his look for a moment and then made the short
sound through the bandage that was as close as he got.

"With that man's money," he agreed. "Every soldier in the world is bought
with somebody's money, boy. The only question anybody ever gets asked is
whose, and the only people who get to be sniffy about the answer are the
ones who were born somewhere with a treasury."

And Sazare sat on the floor with a cup of tea going cold beside his knee
and understood, quite suddenly and completely, that he was looking at
himself.

Not the far end of the road. Not a preview. The *same* thing, at
twenty-six years' remove and about four hundred times the scale: a man
taking contract money off a species of person he had correctly identified,
in order to buy the one thing he needed, which could not be bought with
contract money and never had been.

He had done the sum on a bollard at Kasado in the dark and kept three
hundred and sixty thousand ryō, and this man was doing it with a country.

"I mended a woman's pulley," said Sazare, out of nowhere.

"What?"

"Nothing," said Sazare. "I'll do the boards tomorrow. It'll take a day."
