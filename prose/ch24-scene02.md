# Chapter 24, Scene 2 — Bed Eleven

*Act 4, part A. Konoha General, second floor. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen.
POV: Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

The corner bed by the window was bed eleven and the man in it was called
Handa Rokusuke and he had been a carpenter for twenty-six years.

Sazare had done his obs twice a day for three months and had not once had
a conversation with him beyond *morning* and *all right?* and *lift your
chin a moment*, and this was not because Handa was difficult. It was
because the whole ward had a way of being with him that everybody had
arrived at separately: extremely kind, extremely brisk, and never in the
room for one second longer than the task took.

Sazare had watched them do it for three months and had done it himself and
had known perfectly well what it was.

Handa was forty-four. He had come off a roof in the ninth month — not a
job; his own roof, in the rain, in the dark, at his own house, doing a
thing he had told four different people not to do — and he had landed
across a low wall, and there was a level in his back below which he was
not going to feel or move anything again, and everybody in that building
including Handa had known it since about the fourth day.

He had a wife who came at the fourth hour every single day and sat for two
hours and knitted and talked about the street, and two daughters of eleven
and fourteen who came on Sundays and were frightened of him now, which he
knew.

His hands worked. That was the thing that Sazare could not stop looking
at. From the chest up he was completely and entirely a fit
forty-four-year-old carpenter with a chest like a barrel and forearms like
a gate, lying in a bed with nothing whatever to do with them.

---

It started because of the window.

The window in eleven stuck — the same drop at the hinge stile as six and
nine, because the whole of that wing had been put up in a hurry in a bad
year and every window in it had the same fault — and on a Tuesday in the
second month Sazare came in with the box and took it down and did it,
because he had done the other two and it took forty minutes.

He had the sash on the floor and the hinge off and was packing the stile
with two slips of larch when a voice from the bed said:

"That's the wrong wood."

Sazare stopped.

"It's what I've got."

"It's what you've got and it's the wrong wood," said Handa Rokusuke.
"Larch'll take up in the wet and that's a north window with a bad sill and
it's wet nine months of the year. You'll be doing that again in four
years."

"What should it be?"

"Oak, and not new oak. Off a pallet, if you can get one that's been out in
the yard two winters, because it's done all its moving." A pause. "It'll
be a sod to cut."

"I've got a good saw."

"You have not got a good saw," said Handa, with feeling, "because there
isn't a good saw in this building; I've been listening to that one for
four months and it wants setting and it wants sharpening and whoever had
it last has been cutting a nail with it."

Sazare sat back on his heels on the floor of a hospital ward with a hinge
in his hand.

"Say that again," he said.

"Which part?"

"That you can tell it wants setting."

"Course I can. It's a *saw*," said Handa. "It goes off to the left when
it's binding on the—"

And then he stopped, and there was about four seconds of nothing, and the
big useless capable hands on the blanket moved once and were still.

Sazare Ruri, who had spent three months in that ward doing exactly what
everybody else in it did, put the hinge down on the floor.

"Would you look at it?" he said.

---

He brought the saw up the next morning before the shift and put it on the
bed table across Handa's chest where he could get at it, and then went and
did eleven beds' worth of obs and came back forty minutes later.

Handa had it in both hands and had been at it the entire time.

"You've been cutting something with a screw in it," he said. "There. And
there. And it's not been set since it was made, and it's a cheap one and
somebody's *ruined* it, and I'll tell you what to do about it and you'll do
it, because I can't hold a file at that angle lying down."

"Right."

"You'll want a saw set and a mill file and about an hour."

"I've got both."

"Course you have," said Handa Rokusuke, and looked at him properly for the
first time in three months, and something in his face changed a great deal
and he covered it by being rude about the handle.

---

So Sazare started bringing him things.

Not sentimentally, and not as therapy, and he was careful about that from
the start because he had seen what the ward's kindness did to that man and
had no intention of adding to it. He brought him things because Handa knew
more about wood than anybody Sazare had ever met and Sazare had eleven
things a week to mend in that building and about a third of them were
wood.

The saw. Then the ward door for the second time, which Handa diagnosed
from a description in about nine seconds and which had been the screws
pulling in end grain, exactly as he said. Then a chair. Then the ward's
trolley, which had a wheel that had been wrong for two years and which
Handa explained was not the wheel at all but a bent axle stub, and which
Sazare had been about to replace the wheel on.

Then the window in eleven again, properly, in oak off a pallet that had
been in the hospital yard for three winters, cut with a saw that a
forty-four-year-old carpenter had told a sixteen-year-old how to set.

Handa watched the whole of that one from the bed, six feet away, and said
about four things during it and one of them was, "*Now* you're cutting."

And on the Sunday, for the first time, his daughters came in and their
father was in the middle of an argument with a boy about whether you can
plane against the grain if you have to, and did not stop, and told them to
sit down and settle it.

The elder one, who was fourteen, said, "You *can't*," in the tone of
somebody who has been listening to this since she was four.

"You *can*," said Handa Rokusuke, "if the iron's sharp enough and you're
prepared to be humble about it, and that's the whole of the difference
between a joiner and a bodger, and if either of you had ever listened—"

Sazare went and did bed six and left them to it, and when he came back
past the corner forty minutes later the younger one was up on the bed and
they were all three of them looking at a piece of skirting board.

Nurse Yagi caught his eye across the ward and did not say anything at all.

---

The bedside talk was the thing he was worst at and it took him about four
months to be even adequate, and Sister Kaburagi told him so.

"You're a great deal better with the ones who can't hear you," she said,
in the third month, at the desk, without looking up. "Which is a
professional observation and not an insult, and I'd like you to think
about it."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"I do the hands and I get the dressing right and I say the thing about
keeping it dry," said Sazare, "and I've noticed that when they start
talking about themselves I go quiet, and I've noticed I get quicker at the
task while they're doing it, and I know exactly what that looks like from
the bed."

Sister Kaburagi put the pen down.

"Then why do you do it?"

And Sazare, who had made a rule about preferring true things, said:

"Because I spent two and a half years training myself not to know anything
about anybody, on purpose, as a discipline, because knowing about people
was dangerous to me. And it worked, and it's still working, and I can feel
it working when a woman in bed four starts telling me about her son."

Sister Kaburagi Setsu, who was fifty-eight and had been doing this since
before he was born and who had read his conditions at that desk four
months earlier and had put them straight back in his hand, looked at him
for a long moment.

"Right," she said. "Here's how you fix it, and it's not what you think and
it takes about a year.

"You don't have to *care*. I've had nurses on this ward for thirty years
who never cared about a single patient and were extremely good, and I've
had ones who cared about all of them and burned out in four years and I'm
still writing to two of them. It isn't caring.

"It's *asking one more question than the task needs*. That's all it is.
That's the entire technique and it takes nine seconds and you can do it
with a face like a shovel and it works anyway."

"One more question."

"One. Not four; four's an interrogation and they'll feel it, and you'd
know all about that. *One.*" She took her glasses off. "Bed four's son is
in Kusa and hasn't written since the spring and she's frightened he's
dead. You've done her dressing eleven times and you know his name and you
have never once asked her whether she's heard, and every single time you
don't ask, she notices."

Sazare stood at the desk.

"How do you know that?"

"Because I asked her," said Sister Kaburagi, "on a Tuesday, in nine
seconds, while I was doing something else."

---

He tried it the next morning and it was excruciating and he did it anyway.

Bed four was a woman of about sixty called Ōkubo with a leg ulcer that had
been open for four years and was, slowly and against everybody's
expectations, closing. He did the dressing, which took eleven minutes; and
at the end of it, with his hands still on her ankle, without looking up,
in a voice that came out about twice as flat as he meant it to:

"Have you heard from him?"

There was a pause.

"No," said Mrs Ōkubo. "Not since the fourth month."

"Right," said Sazare.

And then — and this was the part nobody had told him about and the part
that changed the entire way he worked for the rest of his life — she talked
for eleven minutes without stopping, and he sat on the stool with the
dressing tray on his knees and did not go anywhere, and at the end of it
she said, "Oh, listen to me," and he said, "It's all right," and she said,
"You've got other beds," and he said, "I've got bed five and he's asleep."

She patted his hand.

He went out to the sluice afterwards and stood at the sink for a minute
with the water running and his hands under it, and worked out — flatly,
with the tap going — that he had just spent eleven minutes doing a thing
that produced no measurable result whatever, that could not be entered in
any book, that had no weight in grams, and that could not have been done
faster by a better medic.

*And it was the work.*

*That was the work. That was as much the work as the dressing was, and
Yagi has been doing it for eleven years while she talks and I thought she
was just talking.*

---

Handa Rokusuke went home in the fifth month.

Not better. He was never going to be better; he went home because there
was nothing more that building could do for him and because his wife had
spent four months turning the front room into somewhere he could live, and
he went out on a stretcher through the same doors he had come in on and
about nine people came down to see it, which happens to about one patient
in fifty.

He had a length of oak on the blanket beside him. Sazare had cut it off
the pallet in the yard and had planed it, badly, against the grain, on
purpose, and had brought it up on the last morning and said, "Tell me
what's wrong with it," and Handa had told him for four minutes.

At the doors the carpenter got hold of his wrist with a hand like a gate
and held on.

"You'll want a proper bench," he said. "Not that table. You've been doing
fine work on a low table on your knees for four months and you'll be
crooked at thirty and you'll be in a chair at fifty and it'll be the table
that did it, and I'd know."

"I know."

"Thirty inches. Not an inch less and not an inch more, and put the vice on
the left because you're—" he stopped and looked at Sazare's hands. "No.
Right. Put it on the right."

"Yes."

"And come and see me," said Handa Rokusuke, "because I've got a shed full
of stuff I'm never going to use and my wife wants it out and I'd rather it
went to somebody who'd cut with it than to my brother-in-law, who is a
*bodger*."

He let go.

Sazare walked back up to the second floor and did the obs on eleven beds,
one of which was empty and had the mattress up, and did not put it in the
tray, because he had stopped putting things in the tray at some point in
the fourth month without ever having decided to.
