# Chapter 17, Scene 5 — The Arithmetic

*Act 2, part D. Kasado, and the road to the thin coast. Sazare Ruri, age
sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,750 words.*

---

He did the ledger that night, because she had said tonight and not
tomorrow, and because she had been right about that the way she had been
right about everything else.

The chandlery had a back window with a catch that had been mended by
somebody competent about nine years ago and he was through it in under a
minute. The thin ledger was where she had said it was. It was four
hundred names that were not real, in a plain hand, with a mark against
each one that meant *landed*, and it went into the stove in the back and
he stood and watched it all the way, turning it with a poker, because a
book that is half burned is worse than a book.

The jar was under the counter. Nine chips of cloudy quartz off the beach
at the point, worth nothing, which was the beauty of the system and always
had been.

He put them on the doorstep and broke every one with the heel of his boot
and swept the grit into the gutter with his hand.

Then he went out through the window and put the catch back the way it had
been, which took him four minutes and was better tradecraft than anything
he had done all week, and he was aware while he was doing it that he was
working carefully in the dark in a dead woman's shop to protect the
security of an organisation he had that morning destroyed, and that this
was not a contradiction, and that the absence of the contradiction was the
single most frightening thing that had happened to him in his life.

---

The town found her on the Thursday, three miles down the coast, in the
way everybody had known she would be found, and the whole of Kasado said
the same sentence for a week.

*I always said.*

Six mornings out of seven. At her age. On that beach. On the ebb. In the
eleventh month. There was a great deal of feeling about it, and most of
the feeling was genuine, and about a fifth of it was the specific relish
of people who have been proved right about something at last, and there
was no dispute of any kind, anywhere, ever.

The syndicate's second man came into the second boatyard on the Friday
morning to see about a rudder and said, to the yard foreman, that it was a
terrible thing and that she'd been a stubborn old article and he'd told
her himself about that beach.

He said it four feet from a bench where a sixteen-year-old was truing a
tally-frame, and did not look at him, and had no idea in the world.

---

The thirty-one were told on the Friday afternoon that the price had gone
up.

It went the way she had said it would go, precisely, in the yard of the
south quay inn at about the fourth hour: a man on the mounting-block with
a paper in his hand and thirty-one people standing in front of him in a
half-circle, looking up.

Sazare got as far as the gateway.

He had known for two days that he was going to have to do this. He had
planned it — where he would stand, what he would say, how many words it
would take, whether to do it before the man spoke or after. He had it
worked out. He came round the corner into the inn yard with it ready in
his mouth.

And there were thirty-one people standing in a half-circle facing one
thing, and a man up on a block above them with a paper, and the shape of
it went into him like a spike through the arm.

He did not get through the gateway. He got one hand on the gatepost and
stood there while the yard tipped, and the block was a platform, and the
paper was a paper, and there were four hundred people in that yard and
not thirty-one, and the fog was down, and his mother's sleeves were
pinned.

Somebody said, "You all right, lad?"

"Bad fish," said Sazare, and went away up the lane, and was sick behind a
net store, and sat on the ground with his back against it for a quarter
of an hour with his hands over his head.

Then he got up and did it the only way he had.

It took three days. He did it one at a time, in doorways, at the water
queue, at the bread stall, in the yard of the empty inn where the ones
with no money had ended up — a boy with a bad leg and a mender's roll,
saying the same eleven words to one frightened adult after another, in a
low voice, without stopping to be thanked: *don't pay it twice. Whatever
they tell you on the day. Don't pay it twice.*

Nineteen of them listened.

He knew it was nineteen because he counted, because he counted
everything, and because the other twelve paid it twice and he watched
four of them do it and could not stop them without standing on something.

---

He crossed the strait on the Wednesday on the syndicate's own ferry, with
the syndicate's own money in his coat, and paid the full fare of forty
ryō, and was the only man on that boat who was not either weeping or
counting.

He found the Ōgi road and he found the pines and he waited from the
seventh hour, and at a little after eight a cart came down with a load of
charcoal and a man of about forty-five walking at the horse's head.

Sazare stepped out where he could be seen from a distance and stopped and
put his hands out from his body.

The man stopped the horse.

"She said stop," said Sazare.

Bunzō stood in the road with his hand on the bridle for a long moment.

"She's never told me to stop anything," he said.

"That's why she said to say it."

And Bunzō, who had come down through those pines twice a week for a year
and a half without ever once asking anybody a single question about
anything, took his hand off the bridle and asked one.

"Was it quick?"

Sazare stood in a road in a foreign country in the eleventh month and had
a choice, and it was a real choice and it lasted about a second and a
half, and the whole architecture of the man he had spent two years being
came down on one side of it: *there is a difference between a boy who ran
and a man who left, and there is a difference between a man who lies and
a man who doesn't, and the second one was Tōan's and it is the only thing
of his I have kept.*

"No," said Sazare.

Bunzō looked at him.

Then he took the horse's head and turned the cart, in the road, in four
movements, without a word, and went back up toward Ōgi with his load of
charcoal, and did not look round, and was, as far as Sazare ever knew,
alive at the end of all of it.

---

The completion half came through Chōsei's counting-room on the Monday and
Chōsei was delighted with him.

"*Beautiful*," he said, counting it out. "Beautiful. Nobody's said a word.
Nobody will ever say a word. There's a fellow at the toll shed telling
everyone he warned her about that beach in the summer and by spring he'll
believe it." He pushed the stack across. "You'll take more off me."

"Not this season."

"They all say that." Chōsei beamed. "Come back in the spring, young man.
Bring your knee."

Sazare walked down to the rope-walk counter with four hundred ryō in his
coat, and stood in the queue behind a bargeman and a woman with a
consignment of lamp wick, and sent the tenth.

Forty ryō. Entered in a ledger, under a name that was not true, by a clerk
who did not look up, against the war in the Land of Water.

He stood there while the pen moved and did the arithmetic, because he was
not able not to, and the arithmetic was this: that for two and a half
years he had been sending a tenth of everything he earned down a chain of
counters and boards and pegs to fund the moving of frightened people out
of a country; and that the chain had a link at Kasado; and that he had
just killed it, for money, and was now sending a tenth of the money to the
chain, which would use it to fund the moving of frightened people out of a
country.

Round and round. His money and her work and his money.

He sent it. He did not make an exception. It had been settled in a room
over a stable in Tsuzura and he was not going to reopen it every month for
the rest of his life, and he understood, standing at that counter, that
the settling of it had never once been a virtue and was simply the last
piece of machinery still running in a building where the lights had gone
out.

---

Three hundred and sixty ryō, then, and eleven days, and this is what he
found out with it:

There was nothing to buy.

He worked it for eleven days through Chōsei's board and two brokers at
Ihoya and a name he had off a bargeman, and it came back the way it had
come back at Tsuzura and the way it had come back at Kanezaki, and it
came back the same because it was the same answer and it had never
depended on the number.

Nobody sells an unaffiliated boy an education in chakra. Not for two
hundred, not for four hundred, not for a thousand. The men who could teach
it want to *own* what they teach; the men who will take money teach bodies
and edges; and the one person he had ever met who could have stood behind
him for an hour and told him what he was doing wrong had bought him a meal
in a noodle shop and specifically declined to say where such a person
could be found, and had been right to, and was the only human being in
four years to have handed him a thing he actually needed and then refused
to sell him the rest of it.

He had priced the trap correctly.

He had gone into the sea at first light and held a woman down for a little
under three minutes to buy the thing that opened it.

And the trap did not open, because the trap had never had a price on it at
all; and he had simply not known that on the Wednesday, and had had no way
of knowing it, and it would not have made any difference if he had.

---

The last arithmetic he did at Kasado he did on the Thursday evening, on
the north quay, sitting on a bollard with his back to the chandlery, and
it was very short.

Forty ryō a head.

Three hundred and sixty ryō in his coat.

Nine.

He could put nine of them across at the syndicate's own price with the
syndicate's own money. There were twelve who had paid twice and were now
sitting in the empty inn with nothing at all, and thirty-one in total, and
he could buy nine.

He sat with that for about an hour and a half while it got dark.

Then he stood up and put his hands in his coat and walked back to the
boatyard, and did not do it, and the reason was available to him
immediately and completely and he never once pretended otherwise:

Because the money was the year. Because the year was the sentence. Because
the sentence was the four seconds. Because the four seconds was the reason
there was a piece of him in the ground on a hill above Kanmuri Street and
the reason a bar in a drain had lasted no time at all and the reason that
when the white faces came back he would have nothing to meet them with.

And because if he spent it on nine strangers in an inn yard, then he had
held a woman down in the sea for nothing.

He had killed her for four hundred ryō. He was not going to make it four
hundred ryō he had also given away. There is no name for what that would
have made him and he did not want to find one.

So he kept it.

That is the true account. He wrote no line in the ledger about it, because
the ledger was for work, and this had not been work; and he never told it
to another living person in any form whatsoever; and it stayed exactly
where he left it that evening, which was on a bollard on the north quay at
Kasado in the eleventh month, in the dark, with his hands in his coat, at
sixteen years and four days old, having done a sum.

---

He stayed four more days, because leaving straight after is a description.

He mended things.

He did it free, which he had never done, and he did it at a speed that
frightened the boatyard foreman, and he put the word out himself, which
he had spent two years learning not to do: forty-one items in four days,
kettles and hinges and a stuck window and a child's clog and a
bosun's whistle and the toll clerk's chair and eleven feet of guttering
on the empty inn that had been hanging since the summer, and every single
one of them well, and none of them for a copper.

It helped nobody. He knew that at the time and it did not slow his hands
for one minute. There were still twelve people in an inn yard who had paid
twice, and a strait nine hundred yards wide with nobody on the north quay,
and a rope-board with no chip on the second peg, and a woman three miles
down the coast; and against all of that he set a mended clog and a
straightened gutter, and the setting of it was not a decision, it was the
last thing in him that still worked without being switched on.

*He leaves them better,* said a man with a spit and a fire and forty words
a week, somewhere behind him on a coast. *Kindness has a gait, boys. You
can track it like anything else that walks.*

He got on a coaster on the Tuesday morning going south to the thin coast,
because south was not back, and because the border straits were behind him
and beyond them there were poor islands and poorer causeways where nobody
had a book with his name in it yet, and because a boy who has just made
himself famous in a town for four days had better be out of it.

Sixteen years old, and eight days.

Solvent, hunted, alone, and extremely good at his work, with three hundred
and sixty ryō in his coat that would not buy the only thing he wanted, a
medical roll, a jeweller's tools, a knee that clicked on stairs, a ledger
with five lines in it, and a tenth going home every month to a war he had
not seen in two and a half years and could no longer have told you the
name of a single living person in.

The coaster came out past the point on the ebb, and the set took her
sideways for a moment before the helmsman met it, and every man aboard
swore at the water in the ordinary way; and Sazare Ruri stood at the rail
with his hands on the wet wood and looked at a shingle spit going by four
hundred yards off the beam until it was out of sight, and then went and
sat down amidships out of the wind, and did not fortify anything, and did
not grow anything, and did not sleep.
