# Chapter 21, Scene 5 — The Word

*Act 3, part C. The Land of Waves, and the road. Sazare Ruri, age
sixteen. POV: Sazare. **End of Book One.** ~2,400 words.*

---

He had an hour and he used forty minutes of it.

O-Kiyo he told at the bottom of her own stairs, and she looked at him with
her head tilted to bring the good eye round and said, "Well, you'll want
food for that," and went and made him food for that, in about four
minutes, in a cloth, while telling him at length that eleven days on the
road was nothing and that her husband had once walked to the capital and
back and had come home with his feet in a state she was not going to
describe to a young man; and she pushed the bundle into his chest with
both hands and did not say goodbye, and did not mention the pan, then or
ever.

Tsunami cried, which he had not expected and had no idea what to do with,
and hugged him, which was worse, and then held him out at arm's length by
both shoulders and looked at him properly and said, "You'll write and tell
us if they let you stay," which he had absolutely no intention of doing
and which he said yes to.

Tazuna he found at the causeway head with a level and a piece of chalk,
already at work on the thing he was going to be doing for the next thirty
years, which was maintaining a road.

The old man straightened up and looked at him and rubbed his beard and
said, "Konoha, then."

"Konoha."

"Hm." He looked out along his own bridge for a bit. "You'll get to the
mainland side by tonight and there's a fork at the top of the second hill.
Take the left one. The right one's better road and it goes round by the
mill and there's a family up there my brother-in-law had words with in
about the year I was born, and they've long memories and short ways with
strangers." He put the chalk in his pocket. "That's the whole of what I
know about anywhere that isn't here, so make the most of it."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me, boy, it's a fork in a road." He put his hand out and
Sazare took it and it was like shaking a bag of walnuts, and the old man
held on about a second and a half longer than the handshake needed and
then let go and turned back to his level.

"Twelve to eighteen years," said Sazare.

"*Twelve to eighteen years*," Tazuna agreed, without looking up, "and I'll
be under a stone by then and somebody else can bloody well worry about
it."

---

Inari caught him at the top of the plank walk, running, without the hat.

He came up and stopped four feet away and stood there breathing hard and
scowling ferociously with his fists at his sides, and Sazare put his pack
down and waited, because that was clearly what was wanted.

"You were wrong," said Inari.

"Was I."

"You *were*." His chin came up. "You said it doesn't stop. You said it
gets further away and it doesn't get smaller and there isn't any point. And
that's — that's *rubbish*, that's a rubbish thing to say to somebody, and
you shouldn't have said it, and Naruto says—"

He stopped, and went red, and set his jaw and finished it anyway.

"Naruto says you don't have to just *stand there*. He says you can be sad
about a thing and still — and still get up and go and *do* something about
the next one. And that's not the same as it stopping, I know it's not the
same, I'm not *stupid*. But it's not what you said either. So you were
wrong."

Sazare Ruri stood at the top of a plank walk in the Land of Waves and
looked at a nine-year-old boy who had spent four years in a house with a
torn photograph in it, and who had come out at a dead run to argue with
him about it.

"I hope so," he said.

Inari blinked.

"I'm not going to tell you you're right," said Sazare, "because I don't
know that you are, and I'm not going to say a thing to you I don't know.
That's the whole of what I did wrong the other week, and I've thought
about it every day since, and I'd do it the same way again, and I'm sorry
it went into you like it did.

"But I hope you're right. I hope it very much. And you've got about
seventy years to find out and I've only had four, so you'd want to bet on
your figures over mine."

Inari thought about that with enormous seriousness.

"That's still a rubbish answer," he said.

"Yes," said Sazare. "It is."

And the boy laughed — a real one, surprised out of him — and then looked
absolutely furious about having done it, and shoved something at him, hard,
with both hands, the way his mother did, and went off down the walk at a
run without another word.

It was the frame. The cheap lacquer one, empty, with the two re-cut mitres
and the larch fillet.

Sazare stood holding it with about eleven minutes left.

Then he put it in his pack between the quilt roll and the medical roll,
where it would not get knocked, and carried an empty picture frame eleven
days to the Land of Fire, and had it for the rest of his life.

---

They named the bridge as the four of them were leaving, and Sazare heard
the whole of it from twenty feet away with his pack on.

He had assumed it would be a ceremony. It was not; it was Tazuna, at the
causeway head, with about a hundred people round him, saying that they'd
been arguing about it for a week and that he'd decided, and that anybody
who didn't like it could build their own.

"The Great Naruto Bridge," said Tazuna.

There was an enormous amount of shouting.

Sazare stood at the edge of it with the wind coming up the channel and
watched a twelve-year-old in an orange tracksuit go the colour of a
sunrise and put both hands on top of his own head and turn round in a
complete circle, apparently at random, and then start shouting at the
water.

*He'll be dead inside two years,* he had written, in a ledger nobody could
read, from behind a root plate, on the evidence, correctly, three weeks
ago.

Eleven attempts at a tree in twenty minutes. Four times the chakra for
half the height. Wrong coat, no finesse, no awareness, no idea in the
world.

And a road out over the sea with his name on it, inside a fortnight, put
there by a hundred and forty people who had voted, because a nine-year-old
had come out of a house with a crossbow he could barely lift and the whole
town had come out behind him.

Sazare stood in the crowd with his pack on and thought, with total
clarity and no comfort at all:

*Every single observation I made about you was correct.*

*I am going to have to find out what it is I do not know how to look at,
because it is not a small thing and I have been missing it my entire
life.*

---

The road took eleven days and the first four of them were the strangest of
his life, because the country kept changing and would not stop.

He came out of the Land of Waves onto a mainland coast of thin soil and
wind-cut pine, and by the second evening the pines had let go and there
were broadleaf woods with the leaf down thick and yellow underfoot; and on
the third day the road went up over a shoulder of hills and came down the
other side into the Land of Fire, and he stopped in the middle of the road
and stood there like a fool for about a minute.

It was *green*.

In the eleventh month. There was frost in the shade and green everywhere
else — deep, wet, unhurried green, in every fold of every hill as far as
he could see, with rivers in the bottoms of them and smoke standing up
out of villages and terraces of something still standing in the fields
that nobody had bothered to get in yet because there was no hurry.

He had been raised in a country where the ground was rock and the sea was
the colour of a knife and everything anybody owned had been fought over
twice.

He stood on a road above the Land of Fire and understood, in one piece,
what four years of a classroom in the fog had actually been for, and it
was not a lie about Konoha's character; the lie about Konoha's character
was the *decoration*. What Mizuho had been given to teach eleven-year-olds
in a district with no money, four times a week, patiently, in a flat
voice, standing at the front of a room —

— was that this did not exist.

---

They did not include him and they did not exclude him, and after about the
third day he stopped noticing which.

The dark-haired boy did not speak to him for eleven days and looked at him
twice, both times when Sazare's knee had gone and he had stopped, and both
times said nothing whatsoever and simply slowed down for about a mile.

The girl asked him nine hundred questions about medicine, starting on the
second morning and never really stopping, and took the answers seriously,
and knew what a tourniquet was for and had never seen one used, and had a
memory that frightened him: he told her the composition of the
bruise-and-bleeding decoction once, in passing, on a hill, and she gave it
back to him verbatim four days later including the proportions.

The blond one talked to him from the fifth day onward as though they had
known each other for years, on the grounds — as far as Sazare could
establish — that they had now walked more than a hundred miles in the same
direction, and asked him, in the first four minutes, three questions
nobody had asked him in four years, all of them enormous, all of them
delivered with his mouth full.

And the tall one walked behind them and read a small orange book and did
not teach him one single thing.

Sazare asked, once, on the seventh evening, carefully, expecting nothing.

"No," said Kakashi, not unkindly, without looking up. "Not one hand seal
and not one word of theory, and I want you to understand exactly why, so
you don't spend eleven days thinking it's about you.

"If I teach you anything at all, then the report I hand in says a Konoha
jōnin instructed an unregistered foreign kekkei genkai holder with a live
bounty, on the road, before assessment, on his own initiative. That is the
sentence they will read out. Not the medicine, not the bridge, not the
sword. That one." He turned a page. "And after that it is not a
residence question any more. It's a discipline question about me, and you
become the exhibit."

"Right," said Sazare.

"When somebody tells me you're allowed to be taught," said Kakashi, "I'll
teach you. Until then you're a civilian with a bad leg and I'm walking you
to a gate."

---

It was Sakura who gave him the word, on the ninth day, on a road, for
nothing, because he asked.

They were coming down a long hill in the afternoon with the sun in front
of them and the shadows going out across the fields, and he had been
turning it over for about four miles and finally did the thing he had not
been able to do in his entire life, which was to ask a person a question
he badly wanted the answer to.

"The tree," he said. "In the woods above Nami. When you walked up it."

"Oh," said Sakura. "You saw that?"

"From about sixty yards."

"That's just the exercise," she said. "Everybody does that one, it's
nothing." And then, catching herself, and going a bit pink: "Sorry. It's
not nothing, it took Naruto about a week and Sasuke nearly as long. It's
called *tree-walking* and it's the second control exercise; there's a
water one after it that's harder. What you're doing is you emit a fixed
amount from the soles and it has to be *exactly* the right amount, because
too little and you fall and too much and you blow the—"

"*Sakura*," said Kakashi, from thirty feet back, without any emphasis at
all.

She stopped instantly.

"Sorry," she said, to both of them, and went red, and looked at the road.

"No," said Sazare. "That's — no. That's plenty."

And it was.

He walked down the rest of that hill into the Land of Fire with an empty
picture frame in his pack and three hundred and sixty thousand ryō in the
lining of his coat and a bad knee and no jutsu at all, and turned it over
in his hands the way he turned over everything anybody ever gave him.

*Tree-walking. The second control exercise. There's a water one after it
that's harder.*

Not how. He had not been given how, and he was not going to be, and he
found that he did not mind at all; because for eight months on a bench
above a river he had been working in the dark on a thing he could not even
*name*, and a girl of twelve had just handed him, off the top of her head,
in about nine seconds, without being asked twice —

— that there was a name.

That it was the *second* one, which meant there was a first.

That there were enough of them to be numbered.

And that somewhere at the end of a road eleven days long there was a
building with a room in it, and a person standing at the front of the
room, saying the words out loud, four times a week, to children.

**End of Book One.**
