# Chapter 1, Scene 4 — Still Water

*Act 1, beat 5. Sazare Ruri, age seven. POV: Sazare. ~3,000 words.*

---

The Academy took them at seven, and the seventh year of Sazare's life ended in
the ninth month, so he started in the ninth month, along with about forty others.

His mother did not take him. His mother had four frames going and could not
leave them, and had said so at breakfast in the tone that ended things, and had
then come out into the yard at the last moment to straighten his collar with both
hands and say, "Don't tell them anything about the house."

"What sort of thing?"

"Any sort of thing. What we make. What it's worth. Who buys it." She got the
collar the way she wanted it. "It's not a secret. It's just nobody's business."

"Right."

"Go on then."

So his father took him, and talked the entire way, and stopped at the bottom of
the hill to buy him something he did not want and would not have admitted to
wanting, and left him at the gate with a hand briefly on top of his head.

---

There were parents all along the wall.

They stood about in twos and threes the way parents do, and they were mostly
mothers, and they were all watching the same door, and the noise they made was
the low, dense, slightly manic noise of adults being cheerful at each other about
something that has cost them a night's sleep.

Sazare edged past a knot of them on his way in.

"—well, it's not what it was," one of them was saying. She had a baby on her hip
and she was jogging it without noticing. "That's what I keep telling myself.
It's not what it was."

"Mm."

"My cousin went through. Her year, I mean. She won't talk about it. Twenty-odd
years and she won't say a word."

"No."

"But it's not what it was," the woman said again, more firmly, to nobody, and
jogged the baby, and watched the door.

Sazare had no idea what she meant and did not waste any time on it. Inside there
was a corridor with hooks along it at exactly his height, and he had been told
there would be hooks, and finding that there were hooks felt like an enormous
personal victory.

---

The boy on the next hook was called Jirō and had a nosebleed.

He had it under control — head back, cloth pressed, entirely matter-of-fact about
the whole business — and he was managing to hang up his bag one-handed at the
same time, which Sazare thought showed real quality.

"Did somebody hit you?"

"No." Jirō's voice came out nasal and bored. "It does this. It's done it since I
was little."

"You're little now."

"Littler." He checked the cloth, decided against it, put it back. "Are you a
Ruri?"

Sazare went cautious. "Yes."

"Thought so. My mum buys off you." Jirō said it the way you'd say *my mum buys
fish*. "She's got a thing with a blue bit in it. For weddings and that. She never
wears it."

"Why doesn't she wear it?"

"Because it's for weddings," said Jirō, as though explaining something to a
foreigner, "and nobody's getting married," and Sazare discovered that he had a
friend.

---

The room had thirty-eight children in it, and a slate wall, and a woman called
Mizuho who was somewhere past forty and did not look at any point as though she
had been surprised by anything in her life.

She started them on water.

Each of them got a shallow wooden bowl, filled about two fingers deep, set on the
desk in front of them. They were not to touch it. They were not to breathe on it.
They were not to speak, and — she said this with a certain weariness, as a woman
who had said it every ninth month for a long time — they were not to see what
would happen if they kicked the desk.

"You put your hands either side of it. Not touching. And you make it still."

"It's already still," said somebody.

"It isn't," said Mizuho. "Look at it."

Thirty-eight children looked at thirty-eight bowls, and thirty-eight surfaces
crawled very slightly with the tremor of the building, and the sea, and the
breathing of the room.

"You'll find you can push at it," said Mizuho, walking. "Most of you will push at
it. Pushing makes it worse. There's nothing to push. What you're doing is
noticing where it's moving and then not doing anything about it, and that's
harder than it sounds, and if any of you gets it inside the hour I shall be very
surprised."

Sazare put his hands either side of the bowl.

He knew this.

He did not know that he knew it, and he could not have said how, and it would be
years before it occurred to him that he had spent every morning of his life
watching a woman sit at a wooden tray with her hands flat and her forearms
completely motionless for four hours at a stretch. He only knew that the shape of
what he had been asked to do was familiar, in the way a word is familiar in a
language you do not speak.

He watched the surface. He found the place where it was moving. He did not do
anything about it.

The water went glassy.

It went glassy the way the sea goes glassy in a slack, all at once and without
ceremony, and it stayed that way for about the length of three breaths, and then
Jirō sneezed at the next desk and it broke.

Sazare sat back.

Mizuho had stopped walking.

"Again," she said.

He did it again. It took him longer the second time, because he was trying, and
he had learned enough already to know that trying was the problem.

"Hm," said Mizuho, and moved on down the row, and did not say anything else about
it at all.

But at the end of the hour, when they were putting the bowls away, she said, "Ruri.
Stop a moment," and waited until the room had emptied out into the corridor, and
then said: "Who taught you that?"

"Nobody."

"Somebody did."

"Nobody did," said Sazare, honestly. "I've never done it before."

Mizuho looked at him for a second longer than the answer needed.

"All right," she said. "Off you go."

---

The rest of the morning was the world.

Mizuho drew it on the slate wall in about eight strokes, freehand, badly, and
with enormous confidence: the whole continent, one squiggle for the coast, five
blobs, and the sea.

"Right," she said. "There's five that matter and about nine that don't. We're
here."

She hit the wall with the flat of her hand, on an island off the western edge.

"Land of Water. That's us. That's the Hidden Mist. Everybody in this room is
standing on it, so I'd get to know it."

Then she went round the others, and she did them properly, and she did them
*well* — she did voices, she leaned on the desks, she made the whole thing a
story — and thirty-eight seven-year-olds sat with their chins in their hands and
did not move.

"Land of Earth. Up here. Iwagakure." She thumped it. "Mountains. Nothing grows.
Hard people, and I'll say this for them, they're honest about it — an Iwa nin
will tell you to your face he's going to hit you, and then he'll hit you. You can
work with that."

"Land of Lightning. Over here. Kumogakure. Now these—" she pulled a face, and the
room laughed, "—these will take anything that isn't nailed down. Anything. If
it's rare, they want it. If it's *yours* and it's rare, they want it twice as
much."

"Land of Wind. Sunagakure. Poor as dirt and proud as anything, and slowly being
buried, which I don't say to be unkind, I say because it's true. Sand doesn't
care how proud you are."

She paused, and put her hand flat on the biggest blob, in the middle, the green
one.

"And Land of Fire," said Mizuho. "Konohagakure. The Hidden Leaf."

Something in the room shifted. Sazare could not have said what.

"Now they'll tell you they're the friendly ones," she said. "And they are. They
are *extremely* friendly. Nobody in the world is friendlier than a Leaf shinobi.
They'll shake your hand and they'll feed you and they'll talk to you about your
family, and they will mean every word of it, right up until the day it stops
being convenient — and then they'll do exactly what anyone else would have done,
and they'll be *sorry* about it. That's the difference. That's the whole
difference. The rest of the world does what it does. The Leaf does what it does
and then apologises, and people forgive them for it, because they're so
pleasant."

She wiped chalk off her hands.

"So. Which of those five is the most dangerous?"

A girl near the front said, "Iwa," with confidence.

"No."

"Kumo?"

"No."

"Us," said Jirō, thickly, through the cloth.

Mizuho laughed, genuinely, for the first time that morning.

"That's a good answer," she said, "and it's the wrong one, and I want you to
remember you gave it. No. It's the Leaf. It's always the Leaf. Because they're the
only ones you'll want to believe."

---

Sazare and Jirō walked back down the hill together as far as the fish market,
because Jirō lived on the other side of the cutting and it was on the way, and
they had established within about nine minutes of meeting that they were going to
do this every day for the rest of their lives.

"My brother says she says that every year."

"Says what?"

"The bit about the Leaf. The whole speech." Jirō had abandoned the cloth. There
was a certain amount of blood on his collar and he had entirely ceased to care.
"Word for word, he says. He can do it. He does it at dinner."

"Does he do the voices?"

"He does the *voices*."

"Then it's better when she does it."

"Obviously it's better when she does it," said Jirō, offended on Mizuho's behalf,
"he's fourteen," and they parted at the market with a complicated agreement about
where to meet in the morning that neither of them would remember.

---

At home the frames were down and his mother was cleaning the lattices, which was
the job she hated, and which she did on the step with a rag and a bowl of
something that stank.

"Well?"

"There were hooks."

"There were, were there."

"At the right height. And a boy called Jirō who bleeds." Sazare sat down on the
step next to her and started, without being asked, on the small lattices, because
that was the arrangement. "And we did water. In bowls. You have to make it still."

"Ah," said Hisui. "Did you?"

"Yes."

"First go?"

"Yes."

His mother went on cleaning. She did not say anything for a bit, and Sazare, who
was seven, took this for the absence of interest rather than what it was.

"And a map," he said. "Of everywhere. There's five that matter and nine that
don't."

"That's right."

"And the Leaf are the worst ones."

His mother turned the lattice over and started on the other side.

"That's right," she said.

The gulls were coming back off the water. The light had gone that flat grey it
went in the ninth month at about the fourth hour, and the fog was already
standing off past the harbour wall, waiting, the way it did.

"Because they're friendly," Sazare said, "and then they aren't."

"That's right," said his mother. "Do the corners properly, you're missing the
corners."

He did the corners properly.
