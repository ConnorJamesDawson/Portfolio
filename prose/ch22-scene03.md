# Chapter 22, Scene 3 — The Assessment

*Act 4, part A. Konoha, the quartermaster's fabrication yard. Sazare
Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,450 words.*

---

They assessed him on the twelfth day in a shed.

He had expected — he was never able afterwards to say what he had
expected, and suspected it had involved a hall — and what he got was a
long low workshop building at the back of the quartermaster's yard, with a
concrete floor and a corrugated roof and a smell of hot metal and cutting
oil, and a stove at one end with a kettle on it, and pigeons in the
rafters.

There was a bench down the middle of it about twenty feet long, scarred to
death, with a vice at each end.

There were four people.

**A jōnin called Toriyama**, who ran combat assessment: about thirty-five,
squat and wide, with a flat broken nose and a shaved head and a clipboard,
and the specific weariness of a man who has watched four thousand people
demonstrate something.

**A woman of about fifty-five called Kanzaki Uta**, from the
quartermaster's fabrication branch, small and round and grey with her hair
in a knot and a leather apron over her jacket, with reading glasses pushed
up on her forehead and four different pencils about her person.

**A clerk**, twenty-two, who never once spoke.

**And, on a chair against the far wall, doing nothing whatsoever for the
entire two hours, an ANBU in a mask**, whom everybody in the room
ostentatiously ignored and nobody explained.

"Right," said Toriyama. "This is a capability assessment, it's not a test
and you can't fail it, and I say that to everybody and nobody believes it.
Do what you're asked, at your best, and say so when something's your
limit. If you make it look better than it is I'll find out inside a week
and then we'll do the whole thing again with everybody cross."

"All right."

"Anything I should know before we start?"

"My left knee's finished," said Sazare. "It won't lock and it won't take a
load sideways. I can walk about eleven miles a day and I can't run more
than about forty yards."

Toriyama wrote that down without comment, which was, Sazare thought, the
first genuinely restful thing that had happened to him in Konoha.

---

The combat half took forty minutes and was, in the exact sense of the
word, a formality.

Taijutsu: he was put through eleven basic movements from the Academy
syllabus by a woman half his height who kept saying *and again* in a
completely kind voice, and at the end of it Toriyama said, "Where'd you
learn the guard?"

"A ronin in the Land of Hot Water. Bathhouse door-keeper. About eleven
months."

"He was good."

"He was."

"Doesn't help you much," said Toriyama, "because you've got no base and
you never will with that leg, but somebody taught you not to reach and
that's about four years of most people's lives."

Weapons: the wooden sword, and he did the forms, and Toriyama watched the
whole of it with his arms folded and then asked him one question, which
was what he had been taught to do at the end.

"Leave," said Sazare.

"Say the rest of it."

"Make careful cost him eleven seconds and careless cost him a hand, and
then leave."

"Mm," said Toriyama, and wrote for rather a long time.

Ninjutsu: nothing.

That took four seconds and was the worst four seconds of the morning. He
stood on a concrete floor in front of four strangers and said, "I don't
have any," and Toriyama said, "None at all? Substitution?" and Sazare said,
"I did the Academy forms at nine. I've never done one since and I'd not
say I could do it under load," and Toriyama said, "Right," and wrote
*NIL*, and the clerk turned a page.

Genjutsu: nothing. Sensing: nothing. Summoning: he had to ask what it was.

Chakra control, and here Toriyama stopped and looked up, because the
number was wrong.

"Say that again."

"Still water. Seven minutes in front of an examiner at ten. Three hours
once in a drainage cutting, and eleven in a culvert with a hunter-nin
about four yards off."

"Eleven *hours*."

"Give or take."

Toriyama looked at him for a moment, and then at the clipboard, and then
at Kanzaki Uta, who had taken her glasses down off her forehead.

"That's not a control score," he said. "That's a control score with a
decimal point in the wrong place. Who taught you that?"

"A schoolteacher," said Sazare. "In Kiri. Class of about forty."

"For what?"

"She never said."

---

Then they did the crystal, and the first part of that was a humiliation he
had walked four hundred miles toward and had somehow not thought about
once.

They had laid out media on the bench: a tray of grey sand, a flat of wet
clay, a box of quarry grit, two lengths of copper wire, a slab of dressed
granite, and a bucket of seawater somebody had carried up from somewhere
at considerable inconvenience.

"Show us what it does," said Kanzaki Uta.

He had not touched it since a kerb-line on a bridge and he had not grown
anything properly on a bench since a rope-walk in Tsuzura in the winter,
and it took him about ninety seconds to get his hands to stop.

Then he did the sand tray, because the sand tray was the first thing he
had ever done and his hands could do it without him.

Blue-grey came up out of the grey sand in a line and went along the tray
about nine inches, low, even, smoothing as it went, and stopped where he
stopped it; and the shed was very quiet; and he took it out and put it on
the bench and it was a strip of crystal the length of a hand, four
courses of nothing whatever, and he had made better with his mother
standing over him at eight years old.

"Time?" said Kanzaki.

"Forty seconds."

"How big can you go?"

"Depends how long you'll give me."

"An hour."

"Then about the size of a door," said Sazare, "and it would be rubbish, and
you could put a hammer through it."

"Under load," said Toriyama. "In a fight. How long does it hold?"

And there it was, in a shed, in front of four strangers and a mask.

"Four seconds," said Sazare.

Nobody reacted at all. The clerk wrote it down.

"Four," said Toriyama.

"Four. I've had it at four since I was fifteen and I've worked on nothing
else for eight months and it hasn't moved, and I know why it doesn't move,
and it's that nobody has ever told me what I'm doing wrong."

"Right," said Toriyama, and wrote, and Sazare stood in the middle of a
shed and watched a man write down the humiliation at the exact centre of
his life in about four words, in pencil, with a ruled line under it.

Toriyama read it back over. Then he said, not unkindly:

"That's a *no* on combat application, and I'll be recommending the
restriction stands, and I want you to hear the reason from me rather than
off a form. It's not the four seconds. It's that a man with no jutsu, no
speed and a bad leg who has to stand still and touch something to make it
work is a man who dies in the first exchange of anything, and putting him
in the second rank doesn't help either, because he can't run afterwards."

"I know," said Sazare.

"Yes," said Toriyama. "I could see you knew. That's the only thing in the
whole assessment I'd have argued about if you hadn't."

---

He had assumed that was the end of it, and had got as far as thinking
about what a man does with the rest of his life in a country that has
written *NIL* next to him four times in one morning, when the small grey
woman in the leather apron picked the strip of crystal up off the bench
and turned it over twice and said:

"Now stop talking about fighting, both of you, and let me have him."

---

The next hour and ten minutes were the strangest of his time in Konoha and
he did not understand a third of it while it was happening.

Kanzaki Uta did not ask him one single question about what he could do to
a person. She asked him about *material*.

Could he grow it into a hole. Yes. Into a hole he couldn't see. Yes, if he
could get a finger to it. Onto a curved face — yes. Round a wire — yes,
and better than round nothing, that was the whole of the family trade. Would
it take a polish. Yes. Would it take an engraved line? Yes.

"How fine?"

He looked at her.

"How fine do you want?"

She put a thing on the bench in front of him. It was a paper tag about
three inches long, with a black inked figure on it that ran the length of
it and turned twice, and it looked, to Sazare, like a thing an educated
person had written.

"That's a storage seal," said Kanzaki. "That's the whole of it, actual
size. Every line has to be continuous, every crossing has to be a crossing
and not a join, and the width has to hold to about a hair or it does
nothing at all — or, occasionally, does something *else*, and we lose a
shed."

"All right."

"It's on paper. Everything's on paper. It has been on paper for four
hundred years because paper takes ink and holds a line and nothing else
does." She tapped the bench. "Paper burns. Paper gets wet. Paper folds in
a pack and cracks along the fold and then it isn't continuous any more and
some poor sod in the field has a tag that doesn't work and no way of
knowing until he needs it.

"So." She put her glasses back up on her forehead. "Can you grow me a flat
about that size, one millimetre thick, with a face I can cut a line into
that won't chip out at the edge?"

Sazare stood very still.

"You want a *medium*," he said.

"I want a medium."

"That's — " and something in his chest did a thing he had no name for.
"That's what my family did. That's the whole business. We grew it and cut
it and polished it and the guild put the marks on and it went out as
ornament that a ninja could use. Four hundred years. Nobody in my family
ever wrote a single one of them; we weren't allowed and we weren't taught
and it wasn't our part of the job—"

He stopped, because his voice had gone somewhere he had not authorised.

Kanzaki Uta looked at him over the top of her glasses with an expression of
enormous professional patience.

"Then," she said, "you'll know what I'm asking for, and we can get on."

---

He made her fourteen flats in forty minutes.

They came out badly, most of them: too thick, or with an edge that broke
away when she put a graver on it, or with a face that went cloudy about
half a millimetre down where he had rushed the feed. He got three that
were good. She took one of the good ones and put it in a vice and cut a
line into it, freehand, with a graver and a glass, about an inch and a
half long with a turn in it — Sazare watched her hands and stopped
breathing about a third of the way through — and lifted it out and looked
at the burr under the lens and said, "Hm."

"Is that hm-good?"

"That's hm-I-need-four-more-and-I'll-tell-you-
Thursday," said Kanzaki Uta.
"Don't fish."

She turned round to Toriyama, who had been leaning on the far bench for
twenty minutes with his clipboard down at his side.

"Put me down as endorsing," she said. "Not for what he can do — for what
he *is*. I've eleven people in this village who can inscribe and I have
never once had a substrate that wasn't paper or a sealing scroll, and I
have been complaining about it in writing since before that boy was born."

"It's not my call and it's not yours," said Toriyama.

"No," she agreed. "But it's what goes in the box, and mine's the box the
Hokage actually reads."

---

Sazare walked back to the intake block that afternoon between two people
who did not speak to him, past a training ground where about thirty
teenagers were being shouted at, in the sun, in the eleventh month.

He had a piece of paper in his hand, because Toriyama had given him a copy
without being asked to and had said, "You'll be shown it eventually, you
may as well have it now and get the flinch over."

It was a single sheet, printed, filled in in pencil, in a very clear hand.

*RURI, Sazare. Foreign national, unranked. Taijutsu: below Academy
graduate; footwork sound, no base, permanent left knee. Weapons: forms
only. Ninjutsu: NIL. Genjutsu: NIL. Sensing: NIL. Summoning: NIL.*

*Chakra control: exceptional (see note).*

*Kekkei genkai (Shōton, crystal): no combat application. Sustained hold
under load, 4 sec.*

*Fabrication: see attached, Kanzaki.*

*Recommendation: combat restriction to stand.*

He stood outside a barracks in a foreign village and read it four times.

It was the fourth ledger of his life and he knew it while he was standing
there. There had been men in an office at Kiri counting his mother's
output. There had been a fat sweat-swollen book with a boxed price at the
bottom of the entry. There had been a fat cheerful man in a lamp-oil
warehouse with a second list four deep.

And now there was a printed sheet with *NIL* on it four times and one line
that said *exceptional*, in pencil, by a tired man with a clipboard who
had been perfectly fair to him.

Nobody in that shed had been cruel. Nobody had even been unkind. Kanzaki
Uta had gone out of her way and Toriyama had told him the reason to his
face and had given him the paper early so the flinch would be over.

That was the thing he took away, and it stayed with him for eleven years:

*They are not doing anything to me that Kiri did.*

*They are doing exactly the same thing to me that Kiri did, correctly, and
with better manners, and this time I walked four hundred miles to ask them
to.*
