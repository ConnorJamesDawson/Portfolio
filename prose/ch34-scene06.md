# Chapter 34, Scene 6 — You Could Use a Drink

*Act 4, part C. Konoha. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen. POV: Sazare.
~4,202 words.*

---

She came to the hospital, which nobody had ever known her to do
without four days' notice and a corridor cleared, and she came at the
end of his shift, and she did not go anywhere near the wards.

Sazare came out of the sluice at eight minutes past the fourth hour
with his sleeves wet to the elbow and found the Fifth Hokage standing
in the rear yard by the low wall, alone, in a plain coat over the
green one, looking at the laundry lines.

"How long have you been there?"

"About twenty minutes. Emiko offered me a chair and I said no and then
she brought one anyway and I've been standing next to it out of
stubbornness." Tsunade did not turn round. "You've read it."

"Yes."

"When?"

"Nine days after. Two nights ago."

"And you were on at six the next morning."

"Yes."

"Of course you were." She turned round then. In the yard light she
looked, he thought, about her actual age for once, which he had never
seen. "Right. Come on."

"Where?"

"Out of this building, to begin with, because you have been in it for
ten hours and you smell of carbolic and you are about to tell me you
have work to do, and I am the person who signs your establishment
line, so we can skip that part."

---

She took him to a place off the west end of the market row that had six
tables and no sign, and the woman behind the counter looked up, and
looked at Tsunade, and looked back down again without a flicker, which
told Sazare everything about how often this happened.

Corner table. Her back to the wall, which he noticed, and his to the
room, which he noticed her arrange.

She ordered without asking him.

"You don't drink," she said, when it came. "That's not a question, I've
read your file and there's a note about it from the amnesty
examination."

"I've drunk four times. Three of them were medicinal."

"And the fourth?"

"A boatyard foreman in Nami put a cup in my hand and I didn't know how
to refuse it."

"Then this is the fifth and you can leave it if you want to and nobody
in this room including me will comment." She poured two. "But I've
found over about thirty-five years that there's a specific kind of
evening where a person needs something to do with their hands while
they're not talking, and you've got the worst case of it I've seen this
year."

He put both hands round the cup.

They sat there.

The place smelled of hot oil and soy and the particular sourness of a
room that had been serving alcohol to the same forty people for
decades. Somebody at the far end was arguing about a horse. It was
raining, which he had not noticed until he sat down, and the window was
running with it and the light off the street came through in moving
bars.

"You want to know what's in it," he said, eventually.

"No."

"You—"

"I want to know what's in it *enormously*, because I am a scientist and
you are sitting in front of me holding three hundred years of a bloodline
nobody has ever documented, and if you told me right now I would be
here until the fourth hour of the morning asking you questions about
it." Tsunade turned her cup a quarter turn on the table. "Which is
exactly why I'm not asking. That's the trade, and I want it said out
loud, because you're a man who does better when the terms are stated."

"What's the trade?"

"You don't have to tell me anything tonight. Not one word about the
scroll, the meeting, your family, the woman in the green coat, any of
it. And in exchange, when you do want to talk about the technical
content — and you will, because you cannot help it — you come to me
and not to a notebook."

Sazare looked at the cup.

"That's not a trade. That's you giving me something and taking
nothing."

"It's a trade because I'm getting the technical content eventually and
I am extremely willing to wait for it." She drank. "Also because I have
sat where you're sitting and somebody did this for me and I have never
once been able to pay it back to the person who did it, so I pay it
sideways, which I'm told is how this works."

---

He drank about a third of it and it was foul and it did something to
the back of his throat and he set it down and did not push it away.

"Can I ask you something," he said.

"You can ask. I've a very long history of not answering."

"Your grandfather."

Tsunade's cup stopped about four inches off the table.

"Careful," she said, without any edge in it.

"I'm not asking about him. I'm asking about—" He stopped, and started
again, because the first version had been a question and the true thing
was not a question. "You were given a name that everybody in the world
has an opinion about. And a necklace, which was his, and which you wear
every single day and which has been repaired twice and which you would
not let a man take the mount off. And I have watched you for two years
and you have never once used the name to win an argument."

"Is there a question?"

"No," said Sazare. "I've been told I do that. I get to the end of the
observation and there's nothing after it."

Tsunade put her cup down.

"Ask it anyway. Badly. I'll allow badly."

He looked at the window with the rain running down it.

"Did it help?" he said. "Being handed all of that. Did any of it
actually help, or is it just a thing you carry."

---

She did not answer for a considerable time, and he understood — slowly,
and about four rungs up a ladder that a man had taught him in a cold
field and had then walked away from — that she was not deciding whether
to answer.

She was deciding whether to answer *honestly*, and that the honest
answer was going to cost her something, and that she was going to do it
anyway.

"No," said Tsunade. "It's a thing I carry."

"Right."

"I want to be precise, because you'll do something stupid with an
imprecise version of this." She turned the cup another quarter turn.
"The name opened doors. That's real and I'm not going to pretend
otherwise; I got teaching at nine that other people don't get at
twenty, and I have never in my life had to prove I belonged in a
room. That's an enormous thing and I know exactly what it's worth
because I've watched people without it."

"But."

"But it did not save one single person I loved, and it did not tell me
anything I didn't work out myself, and every time somebody has died in
front of me the name has been worth precisely nothing, and I have been
told at four separate funerals that my grandfather would have been
proud, by people who meant it kindly and who I wanted to put through a
wall." She said the last part entirely evenly. "The inheritance is a
thing you carry. It is not a thing that carries you. Anybody who tells
you otherwise has not had to test it."

Sazare sat with his hands round a cup.

"It's not in there," he said.

"What isn't?"

"Anything. There's nothing in it. Three hundred years and six hands and
I already knew sixty-one of the entries, I do nineteen of them better,
and out of the eleven things in it I didn't know, two are worth
anything at all." He heard his own voice do something and kept going.
"Kiri gave it to me as an investment. They said so, in the room. *Of
practical assistance in the exercise of his craft.* It's worth about
four per cent of a monthly quota and I'm the only person alive who
knows that."

Tsunade did not say anything.

"I spent nine years thinking it was the thing I was missing," said
Sazare. "Every bench. Every country. There was a version of me that had
it and knew what he was doing and I have been comparing myself to him
since I was nine years old, and he doesn't exist, and I made him up,
and the actual thing is thirty-one feet of paper in a box on my shelf
and it is *worse than what I worked out on my own*."

"Yes," said Tsunade.

"That's it? Yes?"

"What did you want me to say?" She met his eye. "You've just described,
accurately, the single most common finding in the whole of medicine and
the whole of everything else, which is that the thing people spend
their lives reaching for is usually already behind them and smaller
than they thought. I'm not going to tell you it isn't. It is. I'm
certainly not going to tell you it doesn't matter, because it plainly
does, you've gone the colour of the tablecloth."

"Then what."

"Then nothing. Then you sit there and hold a cup for an hour and I sit
opposite you and don't ask about a scroll." She refilled his without
being asked. "That's the entire offer. It's not much of one. It's what
I've got and it's more than I had at your age."

---

They stayed until the ninth hour.

They did not talk about the scroll, or the meeting, or the woman in the
green coat, or ward eleven. Tsunade talked, at some length and with
considerable heat, about a Council member who had spent two hours that
morning objecting to a bridge, and about the fisheries, and about a
mission report so badly written she had returned it twice. Sazare
listened, and asked four questions, and did not once look at the
window.

At some point after the seventh hour he told her about the roller.

Not the contents. Just the chip out of the left end, and that he had
recognised it, with no memory attached to it at all, and had not been
able to move for four seconds in a room with thirteen people in it.

Tsunade listened to the whole of it and did not say a word when he
finished, and then, after about eleven seconds, said:

"That's the part that gets you. It's never the big thing. It's the
handle of a door or a chip out of a roller and it goes straight through
everything you've built."

"Yes."

"There isn't a technique for it."

"No."

"Drink your drink," said Tsunade.

He drank it.

---

The interesting thing about it was the gap.

He identified it about eleven minutes later and spent a further four
minutes examining it before he understood that examining it was itself
evidence, and by then it was too late to do anything about either.

There was ordinarily a gap. He had never once thought about it because
it had been there since he was about seven — a stage, between the
sentence arriving and the sentence leaving, in which the sentence was
checked. What does this tell them. What can be done with it. Is there
a shorter version. Is there a version that gives away less.

It ran in about a quarter of a second and it had never failed.

It was not running now.

"You've gone thoughtful," said Tsunade.

"There's a stage missing."

"A what?"

"Between having a sentence and saying one. There's normally a check."
He turned the cup. "It's not there. I've just watched three sentences
go past without it and two of them were fine and I have no idea about
the third."

Tsunade looked at him for a moment and then laughed — properly, once,
with her head back — and it was the first time he had heard it and it
changed her face by about eleven years.

"That," she said, "is the single most Ruri-shaped description of being
one drink in that I have ever heard in my life."

"It's accurate."

"It's *extremely* accurate, that's what's wrong with it." She refilled
her own. "Right. New rules, because you're going to spend the rest of
the evening auditing your own mouth and I'd rather you didn't."

---

"One question, one shot," said Tsunade. "I ask, you answer, you drink.
You ask, I answer, I drink. Anybody can pass. A pass costs two."

"That's a considerable structural advantage to whoever asks better
questions."

"Yes."

"You've done this before."

"I have done this for thirty-five years and I am extremely good at it
and you are eighteen and one drink in, and I want you to understand
that I am not going to go easy on you, because going easy on people is
patronising and I have been on the receiving end of it since I was
nine." She set the bottle down. "Nothing about work. That's the only
other rule. No crystal, no hospital, no ward, no forms."

"That's most of what I have."

"I know. That's why it's the rule."

He thought about it.

"All right."

"Good. Me first." Tsunade put her elbows on the table. "What do you eat
when nobody's watching?"

Sazare opened his mouth and found nothing in it.

He tried for what he later established was about nine seconds, which is
an extremely long time in a conversation, and what he arrived at was:

"I eat what's in front of me."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the true one. I eat at the hospital at the second hour and the
sixth, and whatever's in the pot, and on my day off I eat at O-Shige's
and she gives me whatever she's got most of." He frowned. "I don't
think I've chosen a meal since I was thirteen."

"Pass," said Tsunade. "That's two."

"It wasn't a pass, it was an answer—"

"It was a report on your supply chain. Two."

He drank two, and it went into him faster than the first one had, and
somewhere around the bottom of the second he registered that this was
almost certainly the point of the rule.

---

"Mine," he said.

"Go on."

"You've got a mason's callus."

Tsunade's eyebrows went up.

"Inside of the right hand, across the top of the palm, about here." He
put two fingers on his own to show her. "I saw it the first time I was
in that office, two years ago, and I've had no explanation for it for
two years and it's been extremely annoying. A pen puts one on the
outside of the index finger, which you've also got. That one's a haft
callus. I've seen it twice, both times on men who worked with a
hammer."

"That's your question? A callus?"

"That's my question."

"You could have asked me anything."

"I know what I could have asked you. I've wanted to know about the
callus for two years and I've wanted to know about most of the other
things for about eleven days." He looked at her steadily, which the
missing stage had a great deal to do with. "Also you'd have passed."

Tsunade laughed again, shorter this time, and drank.

"It's from hitting things," she said.

"With a hammer?"

"With this." She turned her hand over and showed him the heel of it.
"Chakra strike. It lands here, on the pad, and the shock goes back up
through the palm exactly the way a haft does, and after about thirty
years you get a callus in a place that makes jewellers stare at you in
diplomatic meetings."

"That's — " He stopped, and heard the sentence go past without being
checked, and it was already out. "That's a beautiful mechanism.
Everybody's got a fault line where the shock goes. Mine's the elbow,
it's why my arms give out before my hands do. You've put yours
somewhere it can be armoured with skin."

"I didn't *put* it anywhere, it's just where the technique lands."

"Then somebody designed the technique to land there, and if it was you
then you're better at engineering than at explaining it, and if it
wasn't then whoever taught it to you was, and either way somebody
thought about it for a long time."

Tsunade sat back.

"Nobody has ever said anything remotely like that to me about it," she
said.

"About the callus?"

"About any of it." She turned her cup a quarter turn. "Drink. That was
three sentences and none of them were a question."

---

"My turn," she said. "And this one's real, so brace."

"All right."

"What did you want to be? Before. When you were nine, in the workshop,
before any of it."

The gap did not run.

"A jeweller," said Sazare.

It came out of him whole and instantly and without any of the
scaffolding he would ordinarily have built around it, and it landed on
the table between them and he heard it land.

"I was going to have the bench," he said. "My mother's bench. Not the
whole workshop, my uncle had the trade side and he had sons — I was
going to have the *bench*, which was the fine work and the settings and
the small stuff, because I had the hands for it and everybody said so,
and I'd been doing stock lists since I was seven and my mother made me
copy them until I cried and I was going to have that bench by the time
I was twenty."

He stopped.

"I never wanted to be a shinobi," he said. "Not for one day. Not one
day in my life. I went to the Academy because Ruri go to the Academy
for four years and then come back to the trade, that's what it was
*for*, it was four years of paperwork so the state had a file on you
and then you went home and made earrings."

Tsunade did not say anything.

"I told a man on a boat that," he said. "When I was thirteen. He asked
me what I did and I said *I make earrings*, and it was the last
completely true sentence I said to anybody for about six years, and it
was true because I was still going to. In my head I was still going to.
It took me until about fifteen to notice I'd stopped."

He drank without being told to.

"Do you still want it," said Tsunade.

"Yes."

It came out before the question had finished.

"Not instead," he said. "That's the — I want to be clear, because I've
signed things. I'm not going anywhere and I'd do the ward again
tomorrow and the stones are the best work I've ever done. But you asked
what I wanted and I have a bench in a room over a persimmon tree that I
built out of scrap in nine evenings and I sit at it four nights a week
and I am *happy* at it, and I have never once said that out loud to
anybody, and I'm going to be extremely embarrassed about this in the
morning."

"Probably," Tsunade agreed. "Drink."

---

He asked her four more and she asked him five, and somewhere in the
middle of it the rain stopped and neither of them noticed.

He asked whether she liked being Hokage and she said *no* and then
spent six minutes explaining exactly which parts, which was not a pass
and was ruled a pass anyway on the grounds that he had enjoyed it too
much. She asked him whether he'd ever been in love and he said he did
not think so and then said *I don't know how I'd tell* and drank two
without being asked, which she noted and did not comment on.

He asked what she gambled on. She said everything, badly, at length,
and with feeling.

She asked him what the worst thing was that anybody had ever said to
him, and he passed, and drank two.

She did not ask it again.

And at some point, well after the eighth hour, when the woman behind
the counter had begun the particular unhurried tidying that means a
place is not going to throw you out but would like you to know the
hour, Tsunade said:

"You know you're not unique."

"I'd assumed not."

"I mean it precisely. I have met about forty of you." She said it
without any unkindness whatsoever, which was what made it land. "Not
crystal. Not the specifics. The *shape* — clever, wrecked young,
figured out a thing nobody taught them because there was nobody to
teach them, and can't put a thing down. I met the first one when I was
twenty-two. I've met about forty since."

"What happened to them?"

"Nineteen burned out inside ten years. Not dramatically — they got
tired, and then they got tired of being tired, and they went and did
something else and most of them were fine." She turned her cup. "About
a dozen got mean. That's the one to watch. It's very easy, when you've
done everything alone, to decide that everybody else is lazy, and once
that's in a person it doesn't come out."

"And the rest?"

"Two were extraordinary." She looked at him. "One of them is dead and
one of them runs a hospital in the Land of Rivers and has never been
heard of by anybody, and both of those are good outcomes and I'd take
either for you."

"That's nine unaccounted for."

"Yes," said Tsunade, "it is," and did not elaborate, and he understood
that this had been an answer rather than an evasion.

"So I'm interesting," he said.

"You're interesting. That's the whole of it and I'm not going to dress
it up. I'm not fascinated, I'm not moved, I've read your file four
times and I've read forty like it." She finished her cup. "You're
interesting, and I have very little in my week that is, and I'd rather
spend an evening arguing about a callus than another one being told
what the Council thinks about a bridge."

---

They came out at the ninth hour into a street that had stopped raining
and was full of the smell of it, wet stone and wet dust and somebody's
drain, and the lamps had haloes on them.

She turned left and he turned right and that was the whole of the
geography of it.

And Sazare Ruri, at eighteen, three drinks in, on a wet street in the
Land of Fire, with the editing stage still not entirely running and no
particular confidence that it would be back before morning, heard
himself say:

"Next week?"

It was two words.

He had, over four years, negotiated a fee schedule with a broker in
Tsuzura, argued a valuation with a quartermaster forty times, refused
Kanzaki a test, written six handwritten amendments into a village
integration agreement, and told the Fifth Hokage to her face that her
degradation-curve interval was too coarse.

He had never once asked a person for their time.

Tsunade stopped, and turned round, and looked at him from about eleven
feet away.

"Yes," she said.

"You've got the—"

"I've got the Council on Thursdays and about nine hundred other things
and I have had a standing table in that place for eleven years and I
use it about twice a year because there's never anybody worth taking to
it." She put her hands in her coat pockets. "Next week. Same night.
Don't be early, I'm always late, and don't apologise for anything in
the morning because I'll have forgotten most of it and you'll have
remembered all of it, which is the fundamental unfairness of drinking
with somebody who counts things."

"Right."

"Go home, Ruri."

He went home.

He got about four streets before he stopped under a lamp and stood
there for a while, working out — with considerable difficulty, and
badly, and with the machinery not fully back online — what had actually
happened.

*The check wasn't running. That's all. I said eleven things I would not
have said sober and about nine of them were fine and one of them was a
bench and one of them was a callus, and I have watched myself do a
thing I have not been able to do in eighteen years and the only reason
it worked is that a chemical switched off the part that stops me.*

*That is not a solution. That is a fault I found a way around.*

He stood under the lamp with the water still coming off the eaves.

*And I asked her for next week, and the check wasn't running for that
either.*

*And I'd have asked anyway.*

He was, on the whole, fairly sure that the last part was true, and he
made a note to check it in the morning when the machinery was back, and
in the morning it was still true, which he found more disturbing than
the alternative and wrote down anyway.
