# Chapter 53, Scene 5 — No

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the Hokage's office. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen.
POV: Sazare. ~1,636 words.*

---

"Go home, Ruri."

He did not move.

He had heard that sentence — or *get out of my office*, or *go to bed
first*, or *I've a delegation at nine* — perhaps forty times in three
years, and he had gone forty times, and it had never once occurred to
him that going was a decision.

He sat in the chair and looked at her.

---

*She has been in the register for eleven minutes.*

*She went into it about half a second after I said the thing, and she
has done the whole of the promotion out of it — the board, the
recommendation, the establishment, the sign-off, the dates — and every
sentence of it was correct and she was more formal with me than she has
been in three years and I noticed at the ninth second and let her have
it.*

*And she has just closed a folder and squared it against the edge of a
desk and told me to go home, which is the last form in the room.*

*There are no more forms.*

He looked at her hands, which were on the folder and had not moved off
it.

*Rung one: the folder is squared and it did not need squaring, and she
has squared it twice.*

*Rung two: she has not picked the pen back up, and there is a stack on
the floor beside her chair that she put there at twenty-five past four
and has not retrieved, and it is now past six.*

*Rung three: she went into the register because the register is a room
with walls in it, and she has just run out of register, and the next
thing after go home is being in an office on her own.*

*Rung four.*

*I have been told for two years not to skip to rung four. Kakashi said
it on a training ground in the fifth month of last year and he said it
because a man who takes the top of a ladder without the middle of it
gets everything wrong and is confident about it, and I have gone on
being fluent at the bottom and useless at the top and I have written
that down about nine times.*

*I have the middle now. I have had it since about the fourteenth of
last month and I have not once used it for anything that mattered.*

*She is going to be on her own in a minute and she has had four things
happen to her in eleven minutes and three of them were mine.*

"No," he said.

---

Tsunade's head came up.

"...I beg your pardon?"

"No. Not yet." He kept his hands on his knees. "I'll go. I'm not
arguing about the going and I'm not going to be in this room in ten
minutes. I'd like four of them."

She stared at him.

He watched a woman who had run a village for two years and a hospital
for two before that and had been giving instructions to people since
before he was born, sitting behind a desk with her hand flat on a
closed folder, entirely unable to locate the next thing to say.

"You've never done that," she said.

"No."

"Not once. In three years. I have sent you out of this room at least
forty times and you have always—"

"I know."

"Then why—"

"Because you've had four things happen to you in eleven minutes," said
Sazare, "and three of them were mine, and if I go now you'll be on your
own with all four, and I'd rather not do that to you."

---

There was no object in it anywhere and he heard that as he said it.

*Chapter two. Of Speech without Object. Cannot do at all, in a column,
since the ninth month of last year — and I have now done it twice, in
four weeks, and both times it came out at about half the length I would
have made it.*

Tsunade did not say anything.

She took her hand off the folder, which was the first thing that had
moved on that side of the desk in some minutes, and she sat back very
slightly.

"Four minutes," she said.

---

He had not prepared it and had nothing constructed and had, for the
first time in his life, decided that the constructing was the problem
and not the solution.

So he said it in the order it was true in, and it took under a minute,
and it was three things.

"I stopped dreaming about the yard in Kiri."

Tsunade went completely still.

"I don't have the date," he said. "That's the part I'd want somebody to
have. It's in the book by the fourth month of this year as a
sleep-quality note and I'd been recording it since I was fifteen
because Yagi asked me to, and it goes twice a week, twice a week, twice
a week for four years and then it stops. So somewhere in the third
month. I noticed in the seventh and I have not written it down
anywhere as anything except a change in a symptom."

He turned his hand over.

"And that's where I saw them."

---

The brazier ticked.

He heard it land about a second after it was out and he did not take it
back, and he made himself finish it accurately, because a report with
the worst part missing was not a report.

"Twice a week for five years," he said. "It's a yard and it's the same
yard and there are two of them and I am about nine feet away and I
cannot get any closer, and it is the single worst thing that has ever
happened to me and I have had it two thousand times.

"And it's the only place I've seen either of them since I was thirteen
and a half.

"I don't have a picture. There isn't one — there was one in the house
and the house went. I have got a voice for my mother that I am about
sixty per cent sure of and I have nothing for Kohaku at all any more,
and the yard was where they *were*.

"It stopped in the third month, and I did not notice for four months,
and when I noticed I was pleased."

He stopped.

"That's the first thing," he said.

---

"The second is that I have looked at you, and I'd rather you had that
from me than worked it out.

"The fifteenth of the eighth month. On the stair, when you came down —
I wrote it. At the time, at five past eleven, with my hand going. Four
clauses, in a bracket, in the ordinary notation.

"I ruled a line through it the next morning and wrote underneath that
striking a thing out doesn't remove it, and then I did not read the top
of that page for eleven weeks, and I have gone into that entry nine
times and started four inches below it every single time, which is a
manoeuvre and not an oversight.

"I read it on purpose on the twenty-sixth of last month.

"I'm sorry about it. I've written twice in a book that I decided not to
look and it wasn't true when I wrote it either time."

Tsunade opened her mouth.

Nothing came out of it, and she shut it again.

---

"And the third," said Sazare.

He had got there without any of it being planned and he found that the
third one was not what he had thought it was going to be about ninety
seconds earlier.

"You asked me what I'd lost and I gave you four steps and a paper and
you said *that's the paper, where's the rest of it*, and nobody has
ever done that to me."

He looked at the desk.

"That's it. That's the whole of the third one. Kanzaki takes a thing
and weighs it. Yagi tells me what it does to a patient. Ibiki tells me
what it can be used for. Katsuyu tells me what my hands did while I was
making it and it is the most useful thing anybody has ever done for me
and it is still the object.

"You take the object and you put it on the floor and then you ask for
the rest of it.

"You have done that eleven times in three years and I have written down
every single one, and it is the only thing that has ever happened to me
that I could not have got at a bench on my own."

He stopped.

And then, because he had said he would take four minutes and had used
about two and a half, and because there was nothing else that was true:

"That's it. That's all three. I'm going to go now."

---

He did not go.

He had said it and he had meant it and he had put his hands on the arms
of the chair, and then he had looked up and she had not moved at all,
and something in the not-moving stopped him about a quarter of the way
out of the seat.

The room was quiet for a long time.

He counted it afterwards from the tower bell and put it at somewhere
between forty and fifty seconds, which was longer than the gateway and
which he had no instrument for whatsoever.

Tsunade had both hands flat on the desk.

She was looking at the middle of it, at nothing in particular, at the
place where a folder had been squared twice, and her face was doing
absolutely nothing at all — not the still thing, not the closed thing,
none of the four he could name.

And then she said it.
