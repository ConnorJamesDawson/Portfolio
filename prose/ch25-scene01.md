# Chapter 25, Scene 1 — The Window

*Act 4, part B. Konoha, the morning of the Chūnin Exam finals. Sazare
Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,207 words.*

---

At the sixth hour the hospital had eleven people in it who could not walk and
one window that would not shut, and Sazare Ruri knew this because Sister
Kaburagi had told him both things twice.

The eleven were on the second floor. The window was at the men's end, above
the empty bed by the washstand, and had swollen in its frame during the last
rain. Somebody had planed the inner edge in winter and taken too much off the
bottom rail and not enough off the top. The result was a sash that travelled
freely for eighteen inches and then put its left shoulder into the jamb and
stopped.

Sazare set his tin on the sill, took the screwdriver out of his pocket, and
found Handa watching him from bed eleven.

"You've brought the good one," Handa said.

"I own one."

"Then you've brought it."

Handa had fallen from a roof in the second week of the month and broken his
pelvis in three places. He was a carpenter, which had made being unable to get
at the window personally difficult for him in ways the medical staff had not
entirely appreciated.

"Hinge side," he said.

"It hasn't got hinges."

"Frame side, then."

"Every side is a frame side."

"You know what I mean."

Sazare did. He put the screwdriver into the first screw and found that
somebody had painted over it six or seven times. Behind him, the ward made its
morning noises: enamel bowls touching trolley shelves, bedding shaken once and
drawn straight, the soft complaint of a man being rolled onto a hip he did not
like. There were eighteen beds at the men's end and fifteen of them occupied.
Three patients could walk to the lavatory without help, four could do it with
help, and eleven could not do it at all. The number had been on the staffing
sheet at the desk in Sister Kaburagi's square hand, underlined once.

He had passed her at the gate at five minutes to six. She had been wearing
outdoor shoes and the expression she used when asked to do something she had
already said was a bad idea.

"Second floor," she had said. "You are not assigned to casualties from the
arena unless they come through our doors. You are not assigned to the arena.
You are not assigned to anything with a forehead protector on it."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"It was on the roster."

"Good. Be here."

Then she had gone downhill with Nurse Yagi and four orderlies, towards the
stadium with most of the village, leaving behind one duty medic, two nurses,
Sazare, a porter with a bad back and the people who did not get to choose
whether they stayed.

The upper screw came out with paint packed into its slot. The lower one had
bent. Sazare put both on the sill beside the tin.

Handa said, "You've got something in there."

"Nineteen things."

"Screws?"

"No."

"Useful things?"

"Possibly."

Handa considered the answer and let it go, which was why Sazare liked him.

The window came out of the track after he lifted it three millimetres and
pulled the bottom towards him. The left-hand stile was dark where rain had got
under the paint. He pressed his thumbnail into it. Sound underneath, soft on
top. It needed drying and a new length of timber, neither of which he had. He
pared the swollen fibres with the screwdriver blade, wrapped a strip of folded
gauze round the loose runner, and reset the sash two millimetres to the right.

It shut.

Handa watched the meeting edge disappear into the jamb.

"That isn't a repair," he said.

"No."

"How long?"

"Until it rains twice."

"Once."

"The overhang is better than you think."

"The sill falls outwards."

"By one degree."

"One and a half."

Sazare looked at the sill, then at Handa.

"It may only rain once," he said.

The carpenter smiled without showing his teeth and closed his eyes.

Sazare put the bent screw in his pocket, the straight one back into the
bracket, and picked up the tin. It was a shallow tobacco tin with a sliding
lid, scrubbed until the old red lettering had become a pink shadow. Nineteen
grains lay in the fold of black cloth inside it. Together they weighed seven
and a half grams. Kanzaki had weighed them on Thursday, and Ashida had
watched, and Sazare had signed the line under a description that read
*experimental substrate, pre-ordered; personal control study; no seal
inscription*.

He had written *nineteen* in the margin although the form had not asked.

At four minutes past six the porter brought the admission boards up from the
desk. At twelve minutes past, the duty medic came through and checked the four
patients most likely to deteriorate. At twenty-one minutes past, one of the
nurses discovered that the hot-water urn in the women's end had not been
filled and said something quiet and comprehensive about the stadium.

By half past six the morning had acquired its ordinary shape.

The second floor ran east to west with the stairwell in the middle and a ward
at either end. The men's ward looked downhill across the road; the women's
ward looked into a square rear yard where the laundry lines crossed above the
kitchen roof. Between them were the sluice, the locked medicine room, two
single rooms, Sister Kaburagi's office and a narrow treatment room containing
a sink that drained reluctantly in cold weather. Sazare knew the route without
looking now. From bed eleven to the sluice was thirty-eight paces with both
hands free, forty-three with a bowl, and between fifty and sixty with a
patient because patients objected to being carried round corners at useful
speed.

He began at bed one and went clockwise.

Bed one was a stonecutter with a crushed left hand who had spent three days
refusing to look at it and now watched every dressing change without blinking.
Bed three had pneumonia and disliked the breathing exercises. Bed five had
been kicked by a horse and claimed the horse had started it. At bed seven
Sazare emptied a drain, measured thirty millilitres and wrote twenty-eight
because two remained in the tube. At bed nine he changed the sheet with the
patient still on it, rolling the man towards him, pulling the linen flat with
the free hand and rolling him back without putting torsion through the pinned
hip.

None of these were medical techniques. They were work. The distinction
mattered to the examination board and not at all to the person being rolled.

Handa at eleven required water, pulse and an argument about the window. Bed
twelve wanted the bedpan and apologised four times. Sazare apologised once for
the temperature of it, which was not his fault, and did not answer the other
three.

At the far end he checked the wheeled evacuation chairs. There were four. One
had a tyre soft enough to put a thumb into, one had a brake that only held on
the left side, and two were serviceable. The cupboard contained three folded
canvas carry sheets and six pairs of lifting straps. He counted them because
Kaburagi had underlined eleven.

Two chairs, three sheets, six straps.

The arithmetic did not improve when done twice.

On an ordinary day there would have been eighteen people on the floor between
nurses, orderlies, cleaners, students and medical staff. That morning there
were seven, if the porter downstairs was included and the fever-ward team
behind the sealed doors was not. The village had not emptied the hospital. It
had removed exactly enough people that every ordinary task still had a name
beside it and no task could go wrong.

At six forty, Nurse Emiko found him testing the bad chair's brake with one
foot.

"It was reported last week," she said.

"It still doesn't hold."

"Reporting isn't a repair."

"No."

"That wasn't permission."

He had already taken the screwdriver out. He put it away.

"After observations," she said. "And if anybody asks, it was dangerous."

"It is dangerous."

"That's why the lie will be easy to remember."

The brake cable had stretched. He took up half an inch at the clamp, cleaned a
clot of hair and floor wax from the left castor and put the chair back with
its wheels turned out so the next person would see it had been moved. It took
nine minutes. When he returned to the ward, Handa had finished his tea and the
sun had reached the repaired sash.

The glass showed the road clearly enough to count carts.

That was when Sazare began to notice what was missing.

There were no handcarts on the east road.

He could see the upper half of the road from the men's-end window when he
crossed to take Handa's pulse. On an ordinary market day there were two or
three carts in a minute: vegetables coming uphill, laundry going down,
charcoal, bottles, the paper merchant's boy steering badly with one hand. On
the morning of the finals there should have been more. Six hundred visitors
ate more than a village did without them, and what they ate travelled on
wheels.

For a full minute there was nothing on the road except people walking
downhill.

"Sixty-eight," said Handa.

Sazare looked at his watch.

"Your pulse."

"Mine."

"Yes."

"You stopped counting."

"I didn't."

"You were looking out of the window."

"I can do two things."

"Not three."

"Nobody can do three things."

He wrote sixty-eight on the observation sheet, drew a line through the
previous evening's figure, and went to bed nine.

The missing carts were not evidence of anything. The west market had extended
its hours for the exams. The big inns had taken deliveries overnight. Festival
mornings changed the direction of everything. A village was not a workshop,
and even in a workshop an empty bench could mean the joiner had gone to the
privy.

At seven, a woman from the kitchens came up with breakfast and said the egg
delivery had been short by four trays.

At seven seventeen, the porter complained that the linen cart had not come.

At seven twenty-six, Sazare found yesterday's copy of the admissions list
under the desk and looked at the nationalities column. Kusa, Suna, Ame, Taki,
Oto. The last had nine recorded presentations in five weeks: one sprain, two
stomach complaints, four examination certificates, a cut thumb and a fever.
Nine people through the hospital doors from a contingent large enough to
require three floors of the Takahashi lodging house.

Hospitals filled up first, Nurse Yagi had said.

Theirs had not.

He put the list back where it had been.

At seven thirty-two, Ashida Kōhei came through the ward doors carrying a paper
packet dark with grease.

"You're here," he said.

"Yes."

"I knew you would be."

"You asked at the desk."

Ashida looked offended. "I could have known it independently."

He had been assigned to the hospital because Sazare was assigned to the
hospital, which had turned a monitoring duty into an indoor seat and a view of
the road. His wife had objected to him taking an arena post while their
daughter was three weeks old. The objections of Ashida's wife appeared, in
practice, to outrank the Chūnin Exam committee.

He put the packet on the trolley and opened it. Two rice cakes, one for him
and one he pretended not to have bought for Sazare.

"Quiet," he said.

"Yes."

"Good, isn't it?"

Sazare looked out of the repaired window.

"The Takahashi house took thirty-six Oto lodgers," he said. "Possibly more."

Ashida bit into the first rice cake. "Did it?"

"Their registered hospital use is nine presentations in five weeks."

"Healthy people."

"Possibly."

Ashida chewed. He was not stupid. This was one of the things about him that
disappeared under the spelling.

"Is there a reason you're telling me?"

There were several answers. None of them had enough underneath.

"You said it was quiet," Sazare said.

Ashida waited. Then he nodded once and ate the rest of the rice cake.

It was not a report. It was not nothing either. Sazare did not know what to do
with the fact that the distinction made him feel worse.

At eight fifteen the first distant cheer rolled uphill from the stadium. It
arrived thinned by roofs and distance, not as voices but as pressure in the
glass. Three patients raised their heads. Somebody at the women's end clapped
once and was told to lie down.

At eight twenty the road emptied completely.

At eight twenty-three a flock of pigeons came over the eastern roofs so low
that Sazare could see the pale undersides of their wings.

At eight twenty-four the repaired window moved in its frame.

Not wind. The water in Handa's cup made a ring against the enamel.

Sazare put two fingers on the sill.

The second movement came up through the wall rather than through the air. A
long, low displacement. Masonry carrying something from a long way off.

Handa opened his eyes.

"That," he said, "wasn't the window."

"No."

From somewhere downhill came a sound too large to be a cheer and too uneven to
be thunder.

The duty bell began ringing at the desk.
