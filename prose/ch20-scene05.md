# Chapter 20, Scene 5 — Snow

*Act 3, part B. The bridge, afterwards. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~2,100 words.*

---

He worked for two hours and it was the first time in his life that the
thing Tōan had spent two years putting into him was used for the purpose
it was for.

There were, on that bridge, when the shouting stopped: two men with
broken forearms; a woman who had gone down under the scrum and had four
ribs and was breathing wrong; a boy of about nineteen with a puncture
through the meat of the thigh from somebody's fish gaff that was bleeding
in a way that had frightened everybody including the boy; nine people with
hands, because a rout on a concrete road takes the skin off hands; a
dislocated shoulder; and an old man who had simply sat down and gone grey
and was not going to be all right for a while.

Sazare came up the deck with his roll open on his forearm and started at
the near end and worked his way along, and nobody asked him a single
question about anything.

That was the extraordinary part. He had spent two years arranging his life
around not being asked questions, and it turned out that if you kneel down
next to a bleeding stranger and put your hands on them like somebody who
has done it before, the entire world stops wondering who you are.

He did the thigh first, because that was the only one that was actually
going to kill anybody, and he did it the way it is done: pressure high in
the groin with his fist while somebody else got the belt off, then packed,
then wrapped, then the leg up on a coil of rope, then *told the boy he was
not going to die*, which is a medicine in itself and is free.

*A body mends from the middle out. Most dying is done waiting.*

He splinted both forearms with laths off a formwork panel and a shirt. He
strapped the ribs and told the woman not to let anybody tell her to breathe
deeply, because they would, and it would do her harm. He got the shoulder
back in on the second try, with the man's own weight and a boot in the
armpit and about four seconds of the worst noise on that bridge, and then
the man laughed at him, which they do.

The hands he did last, nine of them in a row on a length of kerb, sitting
on the concrete in the sun cleaning grit out of grazed palms with water
from somebody's canteen; and by the fourth pair there was a queue, and
somebody had brought him a cup of tea from somewhere, on a bridge, forty
minutes after a battle, because that is what people do.

He never afterwards remembered feeling anything at all during those two
hours. He remembered the sequence, exactly, in order, every patient, and
that he had run out of clean cloth at the sixth pair of hands and had gone
on with a shirt.

---

The old man came and found him at about the second pair.

Tazuna sat down on the kerb beside him with a grunt and put his elbows on
his knees and did not say anything for a while, and Sazare went on
cleaning somebody's palm.

He had a cut down the side of his face and one lens gone out of the round
spectacles, and the white cloth was off his head, and his hair was
standing up all over the place, and he was — Sazare glanced at him once
and then again — he was shaking, steadily, from somewhere in the middle,
and had almost certainly been doing it for half an hour without noticing.

"You were under it," said Tazuna.

"Yes."

"On the bracing."

"Yes."

"Hm." He rubbed his face with the flat of his hand. "Ties still in?"

"All the way up. You'll get spalling on that seaward face in about twenty
years and it'll go on for the rest of the bridge's life."

"I *know*," said Tazuna, with enormous feeling, "I *know*, and there
wasn't a day in it, boy, there wasn't a single day anywhere in the whole
of it—" and then he stopped, and put his hand over his eyes, and sat there
on the kerb of his own bridge with his shoulders going.

Sazare finished the palm he was doing and started the next one and let him
get on with it, and did not say one word about it, which was, as far as he
could work out afterwards, the only correct thing he did to another human
being that whole day.

---

He saw the end of it from about a hundred and eighty feet, over the top of
a woman's bandaged hand, and he saw perhaps four things.

He saw the tall man with the silver hair walking out toward the seaward
end without the crutch, slowly, carrying something very large in both
arms.

He saw him put it down on the concrete beside something small.

He saw the four of them standing there afterwards in a rough line — the
tall man, and the girl, and the dark-haired boy with his arm strapped
across his chest, and the one in orange.

And he saw the one in orange, at about the fourth minute, sit down on the
deck where he was standing, and put his fists against his eyes like a
much younger child, and stay like that.

Sazare knelt on the concrete with a stranger's hand in both of his and
watched a twelve-year-old cry, at a hundred and eighty feet, for a boy who
had spent an hour that morning trying to kill him.

He did not understand it.

That was the honest account and he gave it to himself immediately, in the
flat voice, with the woman's palm still open in his hands: *I do not
understand what that is. I can see what it is. I have never once in my
life had it and I do not have it now and I would not have been able to
manufacture it if somebody had put a price on it.*

Then he did understand it, about six seconds later, and it went through
him like the cold under the bridge.

The boy was crying because he had liked him.

That was all. There was no more to it than that and there never had been.
He had met a boy in the woods who had told him he would catch cold, and he
had liked him, and that morning the same boy had come at him through a
wall of ice with a needle in each hand and had very nearly killed him, and
now he was dead on the concrete, and the boy in orange was sitting down in
the middle of a bridge with his fists in his eyes because *he had liked
him*, and there was no arithmetic in it anywhere, and no part of it had
been weighed against any other part.

Sazare had spent an hour on a beach that morning building an argument in
three parts with the objections anticipated, and he had lost it, and he
had lost it because it was an argument.

*I don't want you to die*, he had said, at the end, when everything else
had been taken off him.

*That's the best thing anybody's said to me in about four years.*

He had had it the entire time. It had been the only thing he had that was
worth anything and he had put it last, behind the reasoning, as a fallback,
because he was a person who solved things.

---

It began to snow at about the ninth minute of it.

Nobody on that bridge could account for it. It was the eleventh month in
the Land of Waves, on a coast where frost is an event and snow is a thing
that happens twice in a childhood, at half past ten in the morning under a
washed blue sky with the sun on the concrete; and it came out of the air
anyway, in big slow separate flakes, not many, drifting sideways more than
down.

The whole bridge stopped.

A hundred people who had been shouting or bandaging or arguing or sitting
on the kerb being sick all stopped and put their faces up, at once, in
silence, and somebody laughed with pure surprise, and a woman said *oh*.

Sazare put his head back with everybody else.

It came down through the blue in ones and twos, turning over, catching the
light; it went over the whole length of the road and out over the water on
either side and into the fog still lying below in the channel. It landed
on the concrete and stayed for perhaps two seconds and then was a dark
spot. It landed on the bandaged hand he was holding and the woman closed
her fingers on it, gently, the way you do.

And it landed on the low ridge of blue-grey grit along the kerb-line where
he had knelt an hour before — the whole eight feet of it, ground to
gravel by a hundred and thirty pairs of running feet, that no living
person on that bridge had seen him make or seen come apart — and lay on it
and did not melt for a while, because stone holds cold; so that for about
four minutes the only white line on the whole of the Great Naruto Bridge
was the one along the edge where a boy had put his hands down.

Sazare knelt on the concrete in the snow and looked at it.

Then he let go of the woman's hand, and said something to her about
keeping it clean and dry for three days, and reached for the next one in
the queue, because there were four people left and one of them was a child
with a split eyebrow, and the light was good, and the work was there.

---

He did not go up the bridge.

He got as far as thinking about it once, at about the second hour, when
the queue thinned and there was a gap and his hands were free and the four
of them were still standing up there in the snow at the far end with the
two shapes on the concrete.

He put the roll down and got about as far as his feet.

And then he did the thing he had done to himself on a bollard at Kasado
and in a lime kiln and at every genuine crossroads of his entire life,
which was to ask, flatly and without any mercy whatever, what he was
actually going to *do* when he got there.

And he had no answer.

He could not treat anybody; they were dead. He could not say anything to
the tall man that would not be a foreigner with an accent volunteering
himself to a Konoha jōnin at the worst possible hour of the worst possible
day. He could not tell the four of them who the boy had been, because they
had found that out an hour ago and rather more thoroughly than he ever
would. He could not stand there.

He wanted to be near it. That was the entire content of the impulse and he
looked straight at it, on his feet, on a bridge, in the snow.

*And a man who cannot name what he would do when he got there is a man who
is going for himself.*

So he sat back down on the kerb and picked up the roll and did the child
with the split eyebrow, who was eleven and furious about it and wanted
everybody to know he had hit somebody with a boat-hook, and who held
extremely still, in the end, when he was told he had to.

And the snow came down on both ends of that bridge without any preference
at all — on the living and the dead and the wounded and the mender, on the
water going out under the piers, and on a boy sitting on the concrete a
hundred and eighty feet away with his fists in his eyes, who had been
given nothing whatever to work with that morning except the fact that he
had liked somebody, and had turned out to need nothing else.
