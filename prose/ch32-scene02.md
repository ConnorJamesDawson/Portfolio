# Chapter 32, Scene 2 — Thursday Week

*Act 4, part C. The Hokage's office. Sazare Ruri, age seventeen. POV:
Sazare. ~1,404 words.*

---

He brought the degradation curve on eleven sheets and she read all
eleven, which he had not expected.

"Timing first," Tsunade said, halfway down the fourth. "Not quantity."

"Yes."

"You're sure."

"Sixty-one loaded chips, eleven of them discarded for other faults,
fifty measured at intervals of six hours out to nine days." He had the
figures without looking, because he had spent five weeks producing
them and could not have made himself forget them if he had tried. "The
total stored chakra falls off slowly and roughly evenly — about four
per cent a day, and it's still got sixty per cent of it at day nine.
The *sequence* goes much earlier. By day four the eight seconds is
still eight seconds of output but the order inside it has slipped, and
by day six the constrict-then-support pattern has become something
closer to both at once."

"Which is worse than nothing."

"Which is worse than nothing. A pulse that constricts and supports
simultaneously in a vessel that's already clotting is a device for
making a clot in the wrong place." He turned the sheet round. "So the
shelf life isn't the shelf life. Urushibara said a week and he was
right about the energy and wrong about the useful life, and the useful
life is four days, and I'd write three on the label."

Tsunade sat back.

The office was warmer than it had been in the autumn and smelled of
charcoal from a brazier in the corner that had not been there last
time. The desk was still at its angle to the window. There were nine
arrangements of paper on it today rather than twelve.

She had had the necklace repaired.

He saw it in the first four seconds and had spent the whole of the
conversation since deciding what to do about that. The bead of excess
solder at the base of the bezel was gone — cleaned back and polished
out, competently, by somebody who had taken the trouble to match the
finish, and the join was now visible only as a hairline where the two
golds had aged differently. It had been done without taking the mount
off the stone. That was the difficult way to do it and it had cost
somebody about four hours.

She had not mentioned it. He understood, with total clarity and no
idea what to do with the understanding, that she was not going to.

"This is very good work," Tsunade said.

---

"There are three problems with it," said Sazare.

He heard it leave his mouth. He heard the shape of it, which was
identical to the shape of every sentence he had produced in that room
on his previous visit, and he kept going anyway, because stopping
would have required a manoeuvre he did not possess.

"The interval's too coarse. Six hours between measurements means the
slip could start anywhere in a six-hour window and I've recorded it at
the end of one. Second, they're all my crystal and all Yagi's pattern,
so I can't separate whether the sequence degrades because the medium
lets go of it or because her particular pattern is complicated — a
simpler one might hold longer and I've no comparison. Third—"

"Stop."

He stopped.

Tsunade put the sheet down and looked at him for what felt like a long
time and was probably four seconds.

"Do you know what you just did?"

"Gave you the limitations."

"You did that too, and they're all correct, and Shizune will have the
six-hour one fixed by Monday because it's a rota problem and not a
science problem." She leaned forward with her forearms on the desk. "I
said *this is very good work*. That was a complete sentence. It was
not a question, it did not have a *but* coming, and it was the first
unqualified thing I have said to anybody in this building in about
nine days."

"...Yes."

"And you took it, and you handed me back a defect list, inside a
second and a half. Not because you're modest. You're not modest, you're
the least modest person I've dealt with this month — you told me
straight out that the useful life is four days and I should ignore a
man with forty years on you, and you were right, and you didn't
hesitate for a heartbeat." She tapped the desk once. "So it isn't
modesty. What is it?"

Sazare found that he did not know.

He looked for it the way he looked for everything, which was
methodically and from underneath, and got as far as *because a fault is
useful* and could not get any further than that in the time available,
and said so.

"Because a fault's useful," he said. "You can do something with a
fault. I don't know what a person's supposed to do with the other
thing."

"Put it in their pocket," said Tsunade, "and get through the following
Tuesday on it."

---

She let it go after that and went back to the work, which he was
grateful for at the time and stopped being grateful for about an hour
later when he realised she had let it go *deliberately* and had made
her point in the one place where it could not be argued with.

They spent forty minutes on her question from the autumn, which she had
not forgotten and which he had not been able to answer.

"The cross-medic test."

"Ran it four times." He got the sheet out. "Yagi loads the pattern,
device works on Daichi. A different medic loads the identical pattern —
same wound, same sequence, same dose, keyed the same way — and it takes
about seventy per cent as well, measured by time to haemostasis, and
that figure was consistent across all four."

"Seventy."

"Seventy, plus or minus about six. Which isn't nothing and isn't the
difference you'd want."

Tsunade was quiet.

"So I was right," she said.

"You were right that his body knows Yagi's hands. You were right about
the direction and I can't yet tell you why it's seventy and not ninety
or forty, and I can't rule out that it's about the medic's skill
rather than the patient's familiarity, because the medic I used isn't
as good as Yagi and I couldn't get one who was."

"There isn't one who is." She said it absently, already writing.
"That's a real confound and I don't know how to design round it either.
Note it and leave it open."

"You'd normally tell me how to design round it."

"I would normally know." Tsunade put the pen down. "I've been doing
medicine for forty years and about a fortnight ago a seventeen-year-old
put a result on my desk that I cannot explain and cannot design a clean
test for, and I have thought about it in three Council meetings, and if
you tell anybody that I will deny it and I have the entire village's
paperwork on my side."

Sazare, who had spent the previous ninety seconds trying to construct a
sentence that was not a defect list, opened his mouth.

What came out was:

"The join's been cleaned."

Tsunade's hand went to the necklace before she had decided to let it.

"...Yes."

"Whoever did it went round the mount rather than taking it off. That's
about four hours' work and most people would have argued with you about
it."

"He did argue with me about it. For twenty minutes." She let go of it.
"Then he did it my way and charged me for the argument."

"He should have. It's good work."

There was a pause about a second and a half long, which was, Sazare
noted afterwards, exactly as long as the pause he had failed to leave
before handing her the defect list.

"Thursday week," said Tsunade. "Bring the seventy."

He was out on the stairs before he understood that he had done it.

He stopped on the third landing, with a folder under his arm and eleven
sheets in it, and stood there long enough that a clerk coming up had
to go round him, and thought, with something close to alarm:

*That was one. That was one and I didn't plan it and I have no idea
how I did it.*

*It was still about a piece of workmanship.*

*But it wasn't hers, and there was nothing wrong with it, and I said it
anyway.*
