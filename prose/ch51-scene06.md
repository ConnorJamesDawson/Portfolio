# Chapter 51, Scene 6 — Not Tonight

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,663 words.*

---

"That is not true," said Katsuyu.

Tsunade did not look up.

"What isn't."

"That there is nothing for you to do." A slow settling on the corner of
the bench. "Forgive me. You have said a thing which is a difficulty,
and I have been listening to you say it, and there is an obvious remedy
and I have been assuming for about a minute that you had thought of it
and had reasons, and then I remembered that I have assumed that four
times this year and been wrong every time."

Sazare did not move.

He had about a second and a half of knowing what was coming and no
mechanism at all for stopping it, and he understood in that second and
a half that even if he had one he would not have been entitled to use
it.

"I could do it with you," said Katsuyu.

---

"Tsunade-sama, I could do exactly what I do with him. It is not a
difficult thing and it does not require anything from you in advance —
it is not a technique that must be got first, it is only somebody
holding some of it while you learn to hold the rest, and I have been
doing it for eight weeks and I am now moderately good at it and I was
not good at it in the eighth month and he did not mind."

She said the next part in exactly the same voice.

"I could have done it at any point in forty-one years."

Nobody said anything.

"I am not saying that to reproach you. I would not know how to." The
mantle shifted very slightly. "I am saying it because it is a fact
about the situation and I have found this year that facts about the
situation go unsaid for extraordinary lengths of time and then turn out
to have been the whole of it. Nobody asked. I did not offer. I did not
know until the twentieth of last month that offering was a thing I had
never done, and I have been thinking about very little else since."

Tsunade was looking at the three pieces of stem on the felt.

"And I do not know whether you can," said Katsuyu.

That got her head up.

"You don't know."

"I do not know. He could not, in the eighth month — he had a count of
two and it was not capacity, it was that he was holding twice as much
twice as hard, and it took eight weeks to find that out and it might
have taken four evenings if I had been quicker." Katsuyu was
straightforwardly earnest, which was somehow the worst of it. "You
might be able to hold nine tonight. You might be able to hold nothing
at all and we should find that out and then it would be finished and
you would know. I could not say in advance and I am not going to
pretend."

"How long would it take to find out?"

"An evening," said Katsuyu.

---

Sazare sat on the yard stone in the cold and looked at the ground.

*An evening.*

*Four hundred summonings. Forty-one years. Two wars, one of which was
somebody else's. A hospital roof in the rain. Eleven days on the second
floor when there were four hundred on the books and six staff.*

*And the whole of the finding-out was an evening, and it has been an
evening the entire time, and it is an evening now.*

He kept his eyes on the ground.

---

Tsunade did not answer for a long time.

He measured it afterwards against other things he had measured and put
it at somewhere between forty seconds and a minute, and it was the
longest silence he had ever been in the middle of, and about halfway
through it he stopped being certain what the answer was going to be.

That was the part he wrote down four times and never got right.

*I thought she was going to say yes.*

*Not immediately. At about the twentieth second. She had not said no,
and she is not a woman who takes twenty seconds to say no to a thing
she does not want, and she had gone still in the way she goes still
when she is working, and I sat on a stone in a cold yard and thought:
she is going to do it, and it is going to happen here, tonight, on
this bench, and I am going to be in the room for it.*

*And I want to record that I was pleased. Not for her. That came about
four seconds later and it was real. The first thing was that I was
pleased to be about to see it.*

The lamp guttered on the stool at the end of the bench.

"No," said Tsunade.

---

She said it quite gently.

That was the thing that made it final. There was no edge on it
anywhere, and she did not stand up and she did not fold her arms and
her hands stayed exactly where they were on her knees, and it came out
in the voice she used for telling a family in a corridor at four in the
morning.

"No. Thank you, Katsuyu."

"...Ah," said Katsuyu.

And then, because she was Katsuyu:

"May I ask why?"

---

Tsunade did not answer that either.

She sat on the low wall in a cold yard at about twenty past ten with
her hands on her knees and looked at nothing in particular, and the
silence went on for long enough that Sazare understood — slowly, the
way he understood everything about her — that it was not a pause before
an answer. It was the answer.

"Not tonight," she said.

Sazare's head came up.

*She said that in the eighth month.*

*In the room at the end with the table, at about twenty to twelve, when
Katsuyu said "your grandfather was—" and she said "Katsuyu. Not
tonight. Please." Three words and then please, and I had four questions
constructed behind it and I wrote down three facts and left it
standing, and it was the hardest thing I did that year.*

*Same three words.*

*And it is not a deferral. I have known her two years and eleven months
and I have heard that phrase from her exactly twice and both times were
about the same man, and it is not a word about tonight at all, it is
her word for—*

"Then another evening," said Katsuyu.

Sazare shut his eyes.

"Whenever is convenient," she went on, entirely reasonably. "It does
not have to be soon. I am aware that you have a great deal on and I am
not in any hurry, and it would keep. Any evening at all."

Tsunade did not say anything.

She did not say no and she did not say yes and she did not say
*Katsuyu* in the level voice, and the not-saying went on for about four
seconds, and then she stood up.

---

She picked the protocol up off the bench.

She read the top sheet again — the whole of it, standing, in the cold,
which took about a minute and which nobody interrupted — and then she
turned it over and put it flat on the wood and took a pen out of her
sleeve and signed the bottom of it.

Then she dated it, in the ordinary way, in the ordinary place.

*14/10.*

She looked at it for a second.

"Authorised," she said, "retrospectively, which is not a thing I do,
and I want that in the record as an irregularity and not as a
kindness. It's a sound protocol and it had an operator section and I
would have signed it on Friday if you'd asked me to sign it on Friday."

"I didn't think—"

"No. You asked me to *come*, which was correct, and it did not occur to
either of us that a piece of paper was also required, and that is what
happens when two people are working on something nobody has a form
for." She put it in his hand. "File it with Kaburagi. Not the
Division."

"Yes."

"Do the wood in this yard, with her, or not at all. Not in your room.
Not at the hospital. Not on anything living, and I mean anything —
not a cutting, not a seed, not a thing in a pot on a windowsill,
because you will get to that in about three weeks and I would rather
have said it now."

"I'd got as far as a cutting on Sunday."

"I know you had," said Tsunade.

---

She was four paces from the gate when he did it.

He had been trying to work out what to say for about a minute and had
constructed nine things and rejected eight, and the one he was left
with was true and was small and he had checked it twice, and he said it
to her back in a cold yard at half past ten at night:

"It'd only take an evening."

Tsunade stopped.

She stood in the gateway with the lamp behind her and the green haori
and her hands at her sides, and she did not turn round, and she was
still for about three seconds.

"Yes," she said. "I know."

Then she went out, and the gate did not bang because it never did, and
there was a yard and a lamp on a stool and three pieces of stem lying
on a felt that nobody had touched, and Katsuyu on the corner of the
bench saying nothing at all.

After a while she said, "Did I do something wrong?"

And Sazare, sitting on a stone in the cold with a signed protocol in
his hand, found that he had absolutely nothing to say to that, in any
register, at any length, for the first time in his life.
