# Chapter 33, Scene 3 — In a Flat Voice

*Act 4, part C. Konoha, the intelligence building. Sazare Ruri, age
seventeen. POV: Sazare. ~1,397 words.*

---

The clerk's name was Hazama and she was about forty-five and she read
for two hours and eleven minutes without once looking up to see how he
was taking it.

She was small and square-built and wore a grey cardigan over the
uniform with the cuffs pushed back to keep them off the paper, and she
had the flattened first joints on the first two fingers of the right
hand that Sazare had seen on exactly one other person in his life, a
tally clerk at a fish market in Kasado — thirty years of holding a pen
in the same grip for six hours a day. Her reading glasses were on a
cord. She wet her thumb every eleventh page or so and turned it
without breaking the rhythm of the sentence.

There was a jug of water on the table and two glasses. She filled both
before she started and did not touch hers.

*Rung two: she's done this before. Often. And the second glass is for
the person on my side of the table, which is a thing she thought about
in advance.*

The room was on the north side and had no window, and the light came
off two lamps with green glass shades, and it smelled of paper and
lamp oil and the cold radiator under the wall. Behind Hazama the wall
carried a map of the Land of Water with nothing marked on it.

A T&I officer sat by the door and read a book and did not look up
either. Sazare never learned his name.

"Land of Water, consolidated digest," said Hazama. "Compiled from
station reporting, defector interview, trade intercept and open
source. Confidence levels stated where they vary. Shall I do the
summary or the whole thing?"

"The whole thing."

"It's two hours."

"I know."

"Right," she said, and started.

---

He had expected to hear it in an order.

It did not come in an order. It came in the order a file accumulates,
which is to say by source and by date of receipt rather than by event,
so that the same six months arrived four separate times from four
different directions and each pass added something and contradicted
something else.

He heard the end of the war before he heard the end of the purges, and
heard the end of the purges twice, once as a trade-intercept
inference in the second month of the previous year and once as a
defector statement eleven months later that put it three months
earlier than the first.

He heard about the harbour tax at Nagihama and the reopening of two
academy intakes and a dispute about fishing grounds.

He heard the phrase *Fourth Mizukage, deceased* the way a man hears a
name in a list, which is to say without any warning at all, in the
middle of a paragraph about administrative continuity.

Hazama read on for another two sentences before she registered that
something behind her had changed, and stopped, and looked up for the
first time in forty minutes.

"Do you want that one again?"

"Yes."

She read it again, in exactly the same voice.

"*Fourth Mizukage, deceased. Succession contested for approximately
four months. Terumī Mei confirmed Fifth Mizukage. Administration
proceeding on a reconciliation footing: bloodline restrictions
repealed, standing purge orders rescinded, general amnesty extended to
exiles and to combatants on both sides subject to individual review.*"

"Thank you."

She went on.

---

He heard about his own district in the eleventh minute of the second
hour and it took him three sentences to recognise it.

They did not call it by its name. They called it by the ward number,
because the name had gone when the district went and the reconstruction
schedule was written by people working off a survey grid.

*Ward eleven, cleared. Scheduled residential reconstruction, phase
three, deferred. Ground presently in use as materials storage for the
harbour works.*

"Stop," said Sazare.

Hazama stopped.

"Ward eleven."

"Yes."

"Does it say what was there before?"

She looked down the page, and up the page, and turned back two.

"No," she said. "The survey grid postdates the clearance. There'd be a
pre-war register somewhere but it isn't in this and it wouldn't be in
this."

"Right."

"Would you like me to note the query? It goes to the station and they
sometimes come back."

He thought about a workshop with a copper lattice on the bench and
about a step his sister had sat on and about four hundred years of
people who had all lived inside about nine streets.

"No," he said. "Thank you."

Hazama made a mark on her own margin anyway — small, in pencil — and
carried on reading, and he did not find out until much later that she
had submitted the query on her own initiative that afternoon and that
it came back nine weeks later with nothing in it.

---

It was in the last forty minutes, in a section on internal Kiri
politics, sourced to a defector interview at confidence *moderate to
high*, and Hazama read it in exactly the same voice she had used for
the harbour tax.

"*Persistent and now widely repeated within the Kirigakure command
structure: that the Fourth Mizukage was subject to external control for
a substantial portion of his tenure. Source describes a senior hunter
officer, identified as Ao, presenting evidence of a genjutsu of
extraordinary duration and sophistication, exercised by an
unidentified third party. Assessment: the account is internally
consistent, is now the operative explanation within the successor
administration, and is being used to frame the purge period as the
policy of an occupying will rather than of the state.*

"*Konoha assessment: the political convenience of this account does not
by itself make it false. Corroboration limited. Confidence moderate.*"

She turned the page.

"*Consequent: rehabilitation proceedings have been opened for a number
of officers convicted or executed under purge-era orders—*"

"Stop."

She stopped.

Sazare sat in a room with no window under two green lamps and looked
at a map of the Land of Water with nothing marked on it, and the T&I
officer by the door turned a page of his book, and the radiator did
not work, and the second glass of water sat untouched in front of him
where a woman he had never met had put it two hours earlier because
she had thought about it in advance.

"Read the first part again," he said. "Not the assessment. The first
part."

She read it again.

He listened to the whole of it a second time, and then he said,
"Again," and she read it a third time without any comment whatsoever,
and the third time he heard the phrase he had missed twice, which was
*for a substantial portion of his tenure*, and which meant that nobody
knew when it had started, and which meant that there was no date
anywhere in the world before which the thing had been a man's decision
and after which it had been somebody else's.

"Thank you," said Sazare. "You can go on."

"There's eleven pages."

"Go on."

She went on, and finished at four minutes past the fifth hour, and
closed the folder, and squared it.

And Sazare Ruri, who had been told by a man with a bandana that he
should open his mouth at least once before he left the room, and who
had spent the last hour and a half assuming he would fail to, heard
himself say:

"How long does it take you to prepare one of these?"

Hazama looked up.

"This one? About four days, originally. It's been updated eleven
times since, so — a fortnight, altogether, spread over eighteen
months."

"Who asked for it?"

"Nobody." She took her glasses off and let them drop on the cord.
"That's not how the section works. We compile against a standing
requirement. Yours went up in the fourth month because somebody in
personnel flagged that there was a Water-origin shinobi on the
establishment and there was no current digest he could be briefed off."

"So you've been keeping a file on my country for ten months."

"For ten months," Hazama agreed, "in case you ever asked."

She put the glasses back on.

"I'd assumed you had," she said. "Somebody comes in about twice a year
and reads it. I never see who. I just do the pages."
