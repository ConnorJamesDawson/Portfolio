# Chapter 46, Scene 4 — Where It Comes From

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,724 words.*

---

He weighed the rod on the twelfth, before and after, because weighing
things before and after was what you did and he had picked it up off a
woman in a leather apron without ever deciding to.

*Rod, before: 41.62 g.*

*Rod, after drawing and holding one foot six of edge for forty-one
seconds: 41.62 g.*

He did it four times over two days with different draw durations and
got 41.62 every time, which was the limit of the balance, and then took
the rod up to the sealing office and had it weighed on theirs, which
read to a thousandth and belonged to a clerk who did not ask why.

*41.618 g. Unchanged to three places across nine draws.*

He walked back down the hill with it in his coat and the whole thing
standing up in front of him.

*The blade is not made of the rod.*

---

He built the test that night and it took him four attempts to design
because the obvious version was impossible.

*Problem: I cannot weigh the blade. To have a blade I must have my palm
on the seat, and to weigh a thing I must put it on a balance, and a
balance will not weigh an article that runs back through my hand into
the ground I am standing on.*

*Solution: break a piece off.*

*Ch. 41 — a seeded article fails to pieces and every piece is a seed.
Confirmed nine hundred times. Six of them are in a tin on a shelf in a
treatment room and one of them is this rod.*

*So: draw. Snap the last two inches off the point. Take my hand off the
seat. The blade goes. The broken piece is no longer part of the blade
and should therefore stay.*

It stayed.

He did it at about eleven and stood looking at a two-inch shard of pale
crystal on a felt for rather longer than the experiment required.

*7.11 g.*

*Rod: 41.618 g, unchanged.*

*Stock consumed: none. Tin untouched. Bench stock untouched. Nothing on
this bench weighs less than it weighed at ten o'clock.*

*Seven point one one grams of crystal exists that did not exist an
hour ago and I did not take it from anything.*

---

He tested it properly over eleven days, because he was not going to
write *from nowhere* in a book with his name on it.

*Candidate 1: the air.*

*Method: draw and snap on four nights of measured difference — the wet
of the sixth month against the dry spell that started on the fourteenth
— and compare fragment mass at matched draw volume. If the article is
condensing something out of the air, the wet nights should be cheaper
and the dry nights should be dearer.*

*Result: 7.11, 7.09, 7.12, 7.11. No effect. Also, and I should have
thought of this first: I have laid crystal in a corridor with the doors
shut and in a cellar in the Land of Hot Water and neither was
noticeably different from a wet yard.*

*Candidate 2: the ground.*

*Method: repeat the draw standing on the yard stone, on the plank
across the trestles, and on a bench with my feet off the floor.*

*Result: no effect. Also I fell off the bench.*

*Candidate 3: me.*

He weighed himself.

He wrote it down and then wrote a bracket underneath it that said
*(this is not as stupid as it sounds — I have been putting something
into the world for eleven years and it has to come from somewhere, and
the least exotic explanation available is that it comes out of the man
doing it, and if it does then I ought to be able to find it on a
scale)* — and then, honestly, a second bracket: *(it is also somewhat
stupid and I felt stupid doing it in front of Emiko, who asked, and to
whom I said "study" in a voice that did not work)*.

*Result. Before: 61.4 kg. After a session producing 88 g of retained
fragment: 61.4 kg. I do not lose mass. I lose something — I am wrung
out for about two hours and my hands shake at the end of a long draw —
but it does not weigh anything.*

*Candidate 4: the seed.*

*The rod came out of one grain of seeded stock off the invasion
streets, mass at issue 0.31 g by Kanzaki's entry in the second month of
last year, and it is now 41.618 g and has produced a further 88 g of
fragment that I have kept and an unknown quantity that I have let go.*

*0.31 g in. Something over 130 g out and still going.*

*The seed is not the source. The seed is the instruction.*

---

He took it to Kanzaki on the twenty-second, because she was the only
person he knew whose entire profession was the question.

He put the shard on the day book.

"That's seven grams of crystal," he said. "I made it on the eleventh.
Nothing on that bench weighs less than it did before I made it. Not the
stock, not the rod, not the tin, not me."

Kanzaki looked at the shard for a moment.

Then she put her pen down, which she did not do, and sat back on the
stool, which she did not do either.

"Say the last part again."

"Nothing weighs less."

"Sazare." She said it quite quietly. "I have been a quartermaster for
thirty-one years."

"I know."

"Do you understand what I have just been told, or shall I say it in the
language of this office?"

"Say it in the language of the office."

"You have been issuing me articles for three years," said Kanzaki,
"against a source-medium column, in my ledger, in your own hand, three
hundred and eleven entries — and I have been reading that column as
provenance. That is what the column is *for*. It exists so that when a
thing goes wrong in four years I can say: that came from the yard heap,
issued on the ninth, four kilos, second batch."

"Yes."

"And what you have just told me is that the column does not record
where the material came from. It records what you *started at*." She
picked the shard up and turned it. "You have been giving me the
provenance of the *seed*. There is no provenance in my books for one
gram of the actual substance of any article you have ever made, and I
have signed off eleven hundred kilos of it."

"Eleven hundred."

"Eleven hundred and forty, and it is in that press behind you, and I
have countersigned every sheet." She put the shard down. "Thirty-one
years. I have refused stock over a missing chit. I once sent nine crates
of perfectly good wire back to the Land of Rivers because the man could
not tell me which of two mills it came off."

"I didn't know."

"I know you didn't know. That's worse and it isn't your fault, and I
want you to notice that I am not angry with you, which you will
otherwise spend a fortnight assuming." She picked her pen back up.
"I'm going to tell you what I would do if this were wire."

"Please."

"I would stop the line," said Kanzaki, "and I would not restart it
until I knew. And I am not going to say that to you, because it isn't
wire, and there are four people breathing in that building because of
articles you made out of it, and if I stopped this line the first thing
that would happen is a child would bleed out in the second month for
want of a chip."

She wrote something in the day book.

"So we do the other thing," she said. "We keep issuing, and we open a
column, and we write *unknown* in it in ink, on every entry, from
today, and we do not tidy that word away when it becomes inconvenient
and we do not let anybody else tidy it away either. If you find out in
four years, we go back and fill in three hundred and eleven of them."

"That's a lot of entries."

"It is my handwriting and my evenings and I have already decided,"
said Kanzaki. "Now go away. I have eleven hundred and forty kilos of
something to think about and I would rather do it without you standing
there."

---

He wrote it up that night and it took him two hours and he did not get
it right.

*Statement of the problem, fourth attempt, because the first three were
about how remarkable it is and that is not a problem, that is an
adjective.*

*I have been asking one question since I was seven years old. How does
the crystal work. What is a course. Why does one medium take it and
another refuse. Why four seconds. Why does it degrade at six days.*

*Every single one of those is a question about what happens after the
material exists.*

*I have never once asked where the material comes from.*

*Not as an oversight. It never occurred to me that it was a question,
because it comes when I ask for it, and it has come when I asked for it
since I was seven, and a thing that has always been there is not a
thing you ask about. Nobody in four countries has asked me either.
"Bloodline" was the answer and the answer was accepted by every
quartermaster, every clerk, every intelligence officer and every
hospital in three nations, and it is not an answer, it is a place where
an answer is supposed to go.*

*So: state it properly.*

*I am not making crystal.*

*I am collecting something that is already there, from somewhere, in
quantities I have never measured and have now put at over eleven
hundred kilos, and I am giving it a shape.*

*I do not know what it is. I do not know where it is. I do not know
whether there is a finite amount of it or whether anybody else is
taking any.*

*I have been doing it since I was seven and the only reason I have
found out at eighteen is that I finally made an article good enough to
weigh twice.*
