# Chapter 40, Scene 1 — The Winter

*Act 4, part D. Konoha General, the respiratory ward. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,553 words.*

---

Urushibara Gen died on the fourth of the second month at twenty past
four in the morning, and Sazare was not there, and had not been asked
to be, and found out at six from the night sister's handover sheet in
the ordinary way that everybody in that building found out about
everything.

*Resp. — bed 4 — Urushibara G. — d. 04.20. Family notified. Bed
stripped.*

He read it standing at the desk with his coat still on.

Then he did the six o'clock observations, because there were eleven
people on that floor who needed them, and he got every one of them
right and wrote them all down in his ordinary hand, and at twenty past
seven he went down two floors and stood in the doorway of the
respiratory ward and looked at bed four, which had been stripped and
remade and was empty and would not be empty by the afternoon.

The oxygen cylinders were still lined against the far wall like a row
of quiet soldiers. The light came across the beds in the long warm bar
it came across them in every morning. It smelled of camphor and the
sweet syrup the kitchens sent up and, under both, the flat mineral
smell of the cylinders.

The tray was at the foot end of the bed.

Somebody had not moved it back.

---

He had known for eleven weeks and had not written it down, which he
found afterwards to be the only entry missing from four years of a
notebook that had eleven hundred of them.

The signs had been unambiguous and he had catalogued every one of them
without ever once assembling them.

*Eighth week: lesson shortened from ninety minutes to sixty. Stated
reason: "you are slower than you were and I am not paying for it."*

*Tenth week: sixty to forty. No stated reason.*

*Eleventh week: he stopped correcting my hand.*

That was the one. He had turned it over about four hundred times since
and it did not get better: for two years Urushibara Gen had corrected
his notation on every single element, every session, without exception,
in a fast slanted hand that ran to the edge of the paper — *the gap in
that is a mouth that is already shut, that is a closed circle with
ambitions* — and in the eleventh week he had looked at four badly drawn
elements and said, "Yes," and moved on.

Sazare had recorded it as *he did not correct the hand* and had not
asked why.

*Because I did not want the answer, and I knew there was one, and I
have spent this entire year finding out that I have been doing that my
whole life and it has not stopped me doing it.*

---

Kaburagi found him in the doorway.

She did not say anything for a moment. She had her hand bandaged again
— a different hand, a different reason, a kitchen accident she had been
extremely terse about — and she stood beside him and looked at bed four
with him.

"Nine days ago," she said, "he asked me to move his appointment with
you from the Thursday to the Tuesday."

"He didn't tell me that."

"He told me it was a scheduling matter and I did not believe him and I
moved it, because a man of seventy-nine who is asking for something is
asking for something." Kaburagi's voice did not change. "The Tuesday
lesson was the last one."

"It was forty minutes."

"Was it the ordinary forty minutes?"

Sazare stood in the doorway.

He made himself go back through it properly, in order, the way he went
back through everything, and what he found on the second pass was what
he had missed on the first because he had been busy taking notation.

*He did not teach me anything new on the Tuesday.*

*He went back over the twelve elements. All twelve, in order, from the
beginning, which we had not done since the fourth month of the first
year, and which I recorded at the time as revision and was mildly
impatient about.*

*And at the end of it he said: "That is the whole of the notation. You
have it. Everything after this is composition and you will be better at
composition than I was, because you have a medium and I only ever had
paper."*

*And I said: "That's not true, you've got forty years."*

*And he said: "Forty years of paper. Go away, I am tired."*

Sazare put a hand flat on the door frame.

"It was not the ordinary forty minutes," he said.

---

He did the arithmetic that night at the bench because it was the only
thing available.

*Two years and one month. Ninety-one lessons by the duty book, of which
I attended eighty-eight and he cancelled three, all three in the last
eleven weeks.*

*Twelve elements. A thirteenth. The seal on the seal. Open and closed
structures. Instruction, store, condition. Activation by time, contact
or key, and the distinction between a key that is present and a key
that is recognised, which he withheld for four months on purpose and
gave me on a Tuesday in the spring when I had built a lock that only
opened for the locksmith.*

*He gave me the whole of it.*

*He gave me the whole of it in the order that mattered most first, from
the beginning, and he did that on purpose from the first session,
because he knew what his lungs were doing before I ever walked into
that ward.*

He stopped.

*I cannot prove that.*

He wrote that down, because it was true and because leaving it out
would have been the flattering version.

*I cannot prove it. He never said it and I never asked and there is
nobody to ask now, and the honest position is that a man who taught for
forty years may simply teach in that order because it is the correct
order to teach in.*

*I would put it at about eight in ten that it was deliberate.*

*I am aware that eight in ten is what I want it to be. I have checked
the reasoning twice and I cannot get it below seven and I cannot get it
above nine, and this is the fourth thing this year that I have not been
able to resolve and will not be able to resolve, and I have started
keeping them in one place.*

---

There were four people at the funeral and one of them was a clerk from
the sealing office who had not known him and was there because
somebody has to be.

The other three were Sazare, the intelligence specialist with more
storage seals than visible cloth, and a woman of about sixty who
turned out to be a former student from the years when Urushibara had
still been teaching at the Academy, and who had travelled from the
Land of Rivers on four days' notice.

She said one thing to Sazare afterwards, standing on wet gravel.

"He wrote to me in the autumn," she said. "He hadn't written in eleven
years. He said he'd got a boy from the hospital who asked the wrong
questions in the right order and that it had been extremely
inconvenient and that he had not enjoyed anything so much since the
war."

"He said that."

"He said *the wrong questions in the right order*. I've been thinking
about it the whole way here and I still don't know which half is the
compliment." She pulled her collar up. "You're the boy."

"Yes."

"Then that's yours and I've delivered it, and I'm going to go and sit
down somewhere warm, because I'm sixty-one and it's a long way."

---

He went to the sluice at the end of that week and did not do anything
in it for about eleven minutes.

Then he went up to the second floor and got the tin down from the shelf
in the treatment room where he kept the seeded stock, and took out one
grain, and did not enter it against anything, because it was already
entered — everything was already entered, it had been entered on a
Thursday by a woman in a leather apron three years ago and every
Thursday since.

And on the following Tuesday, at the hour the lesson would have been,
he sat at the bench in his room and drew the twelve elements out from
memory, in order, in his own hand, without the sheet.

He got eleven of them right.

The twelfth he got wrong in a way he could hear Urushibara being rude
about, which was that he closed the circle when the element required it
open, and the moment he saw it he could hear the exact sentence — *you
have drawn a mouth that is already shut* — in a voice with oxygen
tubing crossing one cheek and considerably less behind it than there
had been two years earlier.

He fixed it.

Then he sat with twelve elements in front of him on a sheet of hospital
requisition paper with the back blank, and understood, with total
clarity and no comfort at all, that there was now nobody alive who
could tell him whether he had them right.
