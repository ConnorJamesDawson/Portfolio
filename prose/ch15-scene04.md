# Chapter 15, Scene 4 — The Audit

*Act 2, part D. Two miles inland of Tomari. Sazare Ruri, age fifteen.
POV: Sazare. ~2,600 words.*

---

He came out of the road end of the culvert at the fourth hour of the
night, at slack water, on his belly, and lay on the flats path in the
dark for a while getting used to the size of the sky.

Then he went inland.

It took him until first light to do two miles, which he would have said
was impossible, and which he did in the following manner: forty paces on
the drove road with the left leg swinging from the hip like a gate, then
down on the bank until the grey went out of his vision, then forty more.
He counted the forties because counting was the only thing he had that
still worked properly. He did ninety-one of them.

The building he stopped in was a lime kiln above a played-out chalk pit,
cold, dry, half-roofed, and disused for long enough that the nettles had
made a room of the loading floor. There was no water in it. There was no
food in it. There was nobody within a mile, which was the entire
specification.

He got in under the arch, and got his back against the warm-coloured
stone that had not been warm for thirty years, and did the first sensible
thing he had done since the jetty, which was to take stock in order, out
loud, in a voice that came out wrong.

*A body mends from the middle out. Eat first. Every time.*

He had nothing to eat.

"Right," said Sazare, to a lime kiln. "Then we'll do it badly."

---

The inventory took a long time because he kept losing the thread of it.

**Left knee.** Not broken. The joint had gone sideways under a boot heel
and had come back on its own, which is the difference between a man who
does that for a living and a man who is angry. There was fluid in it
already, a fat hot bag of it, and the leg would not straighten and would
not fold, and he could put perhaps a fifth of himself on it. Six weeks,
he thought, on somebody else. Then he revised it upward for the road and
the cold and the nothing-to-eat, and stopped revising it, because that
way lay the grey.

**Ribs.** Two on the right, low, from the shed roof or the shingle; he
could not sort out which and it did not matter. Breathing had a floor
now. He could take about two-thirds of a breath and the last third was
somebody else's.

**Scalp.** A split above the left eyebrow from going through the customs
shed roof, four inches, gaping, sanded through with slate grit and rot.
It had bled the way scalps bleed — enormously, theatrically, harmlessly —
and had stopped hours ago in the culvert, and had glued his eye shut, and
he opened it with spit and his thumb and lost some hair doing it.

**Hands.** Nothing.

**Forearms.** Nothing.

**Wrists, palms, fingers, the backs of the hands, the pads, the joints,
the two inches above the wrist where a man protecting his face takes
everything.** Nothing. Not a mark. Not a graze. The old scar, wrist to
elbow, three exits, exactly as it had been for two years, and around it
skin as unmarked as a child's.

He sat in the arch of a lime kiln with his own two hands turned over in
his lap and looked at them for a long time.

He had put those arms into three separate strikes. He had done it on
purpose, out of the coldest and cheapest arithmetic he had ever done, and
every one of the three had *stopped*, and one of them had gone past his
ear into the shingle hard enough to throw stones, and the man who threw
it had made a short irritated sound that Sazare could still hear, the
sound of a professional whose tool has been fouled.

Everything else had been placed. That was the thing he could not get past.
The knee — the outside of the knee, the survivable line. The strap across
the throat and not the throat. Not one edge, all morning: no blade came
out on either side, and men like that do not go about the world
unarmed, so the blades had stayed where they were because an office had
specified the packaging.

He had not been fought. He had been *handled*. Somebody had come down a
coast for eleven months in order to break as little of him as the job
allowed, with the care of a carter roping a mirror, and the parts of him
that had been left completely untouched were the parts that made things,
because those were the parts that were worth money.

He was sick, twice, and there was nothing in him for it, and it hurt his
ribs a great deal.

---

Then he did the other audit, because he was not able not to.

*Careful, a better man needs about seven seconds to kill you.*

He had counted. He had counted the whole way, from the man in the alley
to the culvert mouth, because a ruined ronin in a bathhouse yard had made
him count for a year until counting came in ahead of fear, and the count
had held.

Eleven.

He had made a careful man take eleven seconds. He had done, at fifteen,
on a bad morning, alone, exactly the thing the entire year of doctrine had
been for, to the number, first time.

And then he did the four seconds, which was the part Sakai had actually
cared about, and which took him the rest of the morning, and which he did
the way he did everything: item by item, out loud, on his fingers.

The spur in the store-door rebate — grown on a Thursday in his second
week because the door opened inward and an inward door wants a shoulder
for a wedge. That was the plaster coming off the frame. Call it a second
and a half.

The rung six feet up the render across the alley — a rung the size of two
fingers, blue-grey, put there for nothing, in a wall he had no business
climbing. His foot had gone out and found it before any part of him knew
it was there. Without it, that wall was six feet of wet render and a
gutter he could not reach, and there was no roof, and there was no
morning after this one. Call that all of it.

The ridge on the slate under the eave, a coin's thickness. Wet slate at
that pitch. Call it a second, and the rest of his life.

The cleat in the joint of the haunch, twenty-two feet in, under eleven
tons of ferry road, grown on a wet Tuesday with water round his wrist for
a purpose he had been faintly ashamed of before he was out of the drain.

Four fixtures. Two years. Eleven lodgings across two countries, twenty
minutes a night for a week in each, a habit he had never once mentioned
to another human being — not to Tōan, who would have named it, not to
Sakai, who would have approved of it, not to Gonta, who would have found
it hilarious — because he had been embarrassed by it. Because he had had
a diagnosis ready for himself in a flat dosage voice: *this is a man who
cannot sleep in a room he has not fortified, treating himself for a
condition he will not name.*

He sat in the lime kiln with his hands over his face and laughed, which
was a disaster with two ribs gone, and stopped, and sat with his hands
still over his face.

The diagnosis had been right. That was the joke. It had been exactly
right, and it had also been *the four seconds*, and it had never once
occurred to him that a symptom and a weapon could be the same object
looked at from two ends. Sakai had told him. Sakai had told him in the
first month, in the rain, and he had written it down in his head as
encouragement, which is what you do with a thing you are not ready to
own: *you fight ground the way other men fight people.* He had thought
that was a compliment about walls.

---

And when all of that was done — the eleven, the four, the itemising, the
small terrible satisfaction of having executed a doctrine correctly — he
made himself sit still and say the rest of it, because a man who audits
only the flattering half of a ledger is running a fraud on himself, and
he had watched a whole country do that for four years.

The rest of it was this.

He had done everything right. Everything. He had read the town, he had
refused the money, he had refused the fight that was offered, he had
gone to ground he owned, he had not panicked, he had not been drawn, he
had kept the count, and he had spent the entire capital of two years of
the best teaching an accident could have given him — and the yield on
all of it, the total return, was eleven seconds and a hole in the ground.

He had not hurt anybody. He had not made anybody *hurry*. He had not, at
any point in eleven seconds, been anything to those three men except an
awkward parcel down an alley. The one thing he had grown under pressure
had come out off-key and had lasted four seconds against a man hitting
it with his hand.

And he was not alive because of any of that.

He was alive because of two sentences that other people had written.

The first was in a fat sweat-swollen book in an oilcloth packet: *damage
to hands or forearms voids premium.* Take that sentence out of the world
and he was face-down on the shingle at the ninth second with a broken
arm, and the whole morning was four men and a boat.

The second had been said out loud on a ferry road in a flat voice through
porcelain: *nothing under the road. He's away north.*

Take that one out of the world and he was in the culvert until the tide
came, and then out of it in a net.

That was the honest account. He had survived by a clerk's wording and a
lie, and everything he actually possessed — every hour in a bathhouse
yard, every night off the lattice, every scrap of the whole hard-won
competent person he had spent two years assembling — had bought him
eleven seconds inside somebody else's mercy.

---

It took him until the afternoon to say the name, and he did it the way
he did everything, which was to take it apart rather than feel it.

The voice was nothing. Porcelain flattens a voice to a plank; it was not
a voice, it was a fact arriving through a hole. He could not have
identified his own mother through that mask.

So: the information.

*Seven's fine* was two words, said once, in the third month of the year
he was ten, on a wall above a market, with the fog off, to one person,
who had come down off the wall to say *you can do fourteen*. It had never
been in a file. An examiner had had the seven minutes and a clerk had had
the sheet and neither had ever heard the phrase in their lives. Mizuho
had built the seven and had not been there when it was spent. There was
one person in the world who owned those two words, and Sazare had last
seen him running back down a street in his indoor things without looking
round.

Then the rest of it came in on its own, in order, whether he wanted it or
not.

Three men, working a coast for months, asking nothing, better than the
last lot. Not bounty men. *Buying its strays back — paying proper rates,
cash on the border.* Water's own retrieval, out past the treaty line
where masks were allowed, and Jirō had graduated the year Sazare's
graduation stopped existing, and Jirō was clever, and Jirō was
*likeable* — the most likeable person Sazare had ever met, the boy who
had been fed by half the district and had known every stallholder's name
on the hill — and if you were an office looking for somebody to walk a
coastline for a year and be told things by strangers, you would not have
to look for very long at all.

And Jirō had come in eight feet into a drain without a sound.

He had been close enough to reach. He had been closer than the boot had
been. He had stood four yards away in the dark and had not put a hand
out, and had said two words, and had gone out and lied to his own detail
in the road.

Sazare put his forehead on his good knee and stayed like that.

Because it was not the hunting that was the problem. He had known for two
years that people were coming; he had read his own price in an inn
doorway and been sick behind a woodpile and gone on eating his dinners.
The hunting was weather. The hunting had never had a face on it and had
therefore never had to be answered.

The problem was that Jirō had stopped.

A man who stops has chosen. A man who has chosen once can choose again,
either way, and will be on the coast again in a month or a season, older,
better, with the whole apparatus behind him and a professional's
irritation at his own lapse — and Sazare had watched, this morning, from
eight inches of dead air, exactly how good that apparatus was at breaking
a boy carefully.

There would be a second visit. Tōan had told him prices like that always
find feet, and they had found feet, and feet come back.

And when it came, if the white face at the culvert mouth reached out
instead of standing still, then Sazare Ruri — who had, on the best
morning of his fighting life, achieved eleven seconds and a hole —

was going to have to be able to do something about it.

He sat with that in a lime kiln for a long while and could not find the
end of the sentence. Every version of the end of it was unspeakable, and
every version of *not* being able to do something about it ended in an
office, in a room, at a trestle, being useful.

Two miles behind him, in a wall, was every coin he had earned in two
years, which he would never see again, because the holder's back room
was now the most watched square yard on the strait.

Two miles behind him, in the render of that wall and in a doorframe and
under an eave and in the joint of a drain-haunch, were four small pieces
of blue-grey stone that had saved his life and that nobody in Tomari
would ever notice, and that would still be there in eighty years.

And two miles behind him, on a bollard on the jetty, was a nine-year-old
with a stammer who had been told, by every adult he had ever met, that
people who wait for you are rare — and who was going to sit there, on
and off, for a considerable time.

Sazare got up on one leg at dusk, using the kiln arch, and went inland.
