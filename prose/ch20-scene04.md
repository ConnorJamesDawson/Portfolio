# Chapter 20, Scene 4 — Four Seconds

*Act 3, part B. The bridge. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,550 words.*

---

He came up the outside of the fourth pier at about the twentieth minute
past nine, on the seaward face where nobody was looking, using the
shuttering ties.

They were still in the concrete: short lengths of steel that had held the
forms together during the pours and had been snapped off flush afterwards,
in rows, four feet apart all the way up, because breaking them out
properly costs a day per pier and Tazuna had not had a day per pier. Every
one of them stood about a quarter of an inch proud and had gone orange.

They were the worst thing on that bridge — they would weep rust down the
face of every pier for fifty years and start spalling by the twentieth —
and they were also, that morning, a ladder, and Sazare went up forty feet
of them with a bad knee in about ninety seconds and did not think one word
about the irony until much later.

He came over the deck edge at the fourth pier flat on his stomach and lay
in the gap between the deck and the untrimmed ends of the last beams, and
looked out along a road he had walked in the sun eleven days before.

---

The fog had gone off the top and lay in the channel below in a flat white
layer, so that the bridge stood out of it into clean cold air with the sun
just getting onto the concrete and a sky washing pale blue overhead.

And on the deck, out toward the unfinished end, there were about a hundred
and fifty men.

Not soldiers. He had them in a glance and the glance was the only piece of
professional work he did that entire morning that he was afterwards
satisfied with: dock hands, quarry men, two or three actual bravos and a
hundred and forty people who had been given a stick and a day's money.
Wrong footwear. No spacing. Three quarters of them at the back with their
hands in their sleeves.

At the very front of it, on a section of stacked formwork about two feet
high so that he stood above them, was a very small man in a very good
suit, with round dark glasses and a walking stick, talking.

Sazare could not hear the words at that distance and did not need one of
them.

He knew the shape from four hundred yards. A raised thing. A man on it. An
audience arranged toward him in a half-circle. Something on the ground in
front that he was pointing at with the stick, and the crowd looking where
the stick went, and the little swell of noise every time he paused,
because that is what the pause is *for*.

*A crowd facing one thing.*

He lay in the gap under the deck edge with his cheek on cold concrete and
the whole of it going through him — the fog, the platform, the paper, the
pinned sleeves, four hundred people in a half-circle who had come out
because it was a Tuesday — and found that it had, for the first time in
four years, absolutely nowhere to go, because it was not a memory. It was
happening, six hundred feet away, at half past nine in the morning, in the
sun.

---

What happened after that took perhaps two minutes and Sazare saw about
half of it and understood, at the time, almost none of it.

There were two men down on the deck at the front of that crowd, near the
formwork, and one of them was very large and was moving and one of them
was small and was not.

The large one got up.

He should not have been able to. Sazare could see, even at that distance,
what had been done to him — the way the arms were hanging, both of them,
dead from the shoulder — and he got up off the deck anyway and stood there
swaying with his head down, and something was said, and one of the Konoha
children threw him something, and he caught it in his teeth.

And then he went into a hundred and fifty men.

It was not a fight and it did not look like one and Sazare never described
it to anybody as one. It was a large object entering a crowd at speed, and
the crowd came apart around it and closed behind it and came apart again,
and men went off the sides of that bridge in ones and twos and threes into
the fog, and the noise coming off it was not a battle noise; it was the
noise a market makes when a horse bolts.

He got to the formwork.

The very small man in the very good suit was on it for perhaps another
second and a half, and then he was not on it, and then he was not on the
bridge.

And the large one went down on his knees in the middle of the crowd, and
stayed there, and did not get up again.

---

The rout started about eleven seconds later and it started for the reason
routs always start, which is that the people at the back could not see and
the people at the front could.

A hundred and forty men who had been given a stick and a day's money
discovered, more or less simultaneously, that the man who had the money
was in the sea; and they turned round.

And at that exact moment, at the town end of the bridge, four hundred feet
behind Sazare's shoulder, a great many people began to shout.

He got his head round.

They were coming out along the causeway from the town: the whole of Nami,
or enough of it to be the whole of it — sixty, eighty, a hundred people,
coming out onto that deck at a run in their working clothes with
everything they owned that had a handle on it. Fish gaffs. Mattocks. A
boat-hook. Two crossbows and a pitchfork. The man from the second boatyard
with a caulking maul. O-Kiyo's brother-in-law with a saw.

At the front of them, twenty feet ahead of anybody, going flat out with a
crossbow he could barely hold up, in a white bucket hat, was Inari.

---

And then the two crowds ran into each other, and it went wrong in the way
that these things always go wrong, which is not the way anybody plans for.

The mob did not fight. That was the thing. There was no charge and no
clash and nobody in that hundred and forty had the least intention of
going near a pitchfork; they were running for the boats at the seaward
end and the boats were at the seaward end and the entire disaster came out
of a single piece of arithmetic that nobody on that bridge had done.

There were eleven boats and a hundred and forty men.

So about thirty of them, at the back, doing the sum in the half-second it
takes a frightened man to do it, broke the *other* way.

They came up the deck toward the town end at a dead run — not attacking;
running, with their sticks thrown away and their arms going — straight
into a hundred villagers coming the other way, and the two crowds hit and
folded and stopped being crowds at all and became one solid churning scrum
of about a hundred and thirty people across the full width of a
thirty-foot road.

And that bridge had no parapet.

Sazare had known that for eleven days. He had walked the deck and noted it
the way he noted everything: the kerb-line was cast but the balustrade was
not, because balustrade is the last thing you do and Tazuna had never got
to the last thing. There was a four-inch upstand along each edge and then
there was nothing, and then there was forty feet of air, and then there
was a falling tide over rock.

He was up and moving before the thought finished.

---

He got about sixty feet.

That was all his knee gave him and he had known it would be, and he took
it along the very edge of the deck outside the scrum, in the eighteen
inches between the churn and the drop, going against the flow with his
shoulder to the crowd; and he got sixty feet, and it was enough, because
sixty feet was where the edge was going.

He saw the whole of it from about eight feet away.

Three of the runners came out of the side of the press — squeezed out, the
way a stone comes out of a fist — and they were not attacking anybody,
they had simply been extruded, and they came out sideways and hard and
took four villagers off their feet with pure weight.

And the four went back, and back, and the upstand caught the first one at
the ankle, and Sazare saw, in an instant of appalling clarity, the whole
geometry of the next second and a half: two men going over backwards, and
a woman going down on her knee at the very lip, and, coming through the
gap they had opened, at a full run, with his head turned the wrong way and
a crossbow in both hands, a nine-year-old.

There was no time to reach him.

Sazare Ruri went down onto both knees at the deck edge, and put both palms
flat on the concrete, and opened his hands.

---

It came out of him the way it always came out of him.

Wrong. Fast. One course and no smoothing, unshaped, ugly, a thing that
wanted to be a wall and was made by something screaming — a lip of
blue-grey stone that came up out of the concrete along eight feet of the
kerb-line to the height of a man's hip, in about a third of a second, with
its top edge as rough as broken bread.

It was the worst piece of work he had ever done in his life.

The first man hit it across the small of the back and stopped.

The second hit him and stopped.

The woman on her knee got a hand on it and stayed on the bridge.

And Inari, coming through the gap at a dead run with his head turned,
went into it hip-high and folded over the top of it and came down on his
back on the concrete on the *inside*, with the crossbow going off into
the sky and his hat coming off, four feet from a forty-foot drop and
still on the bridge.

Sazare knelt with his palms on the deck and his arms shaking and counted,
because he could not not.

*One.*

Somebody trod on his hand.

*Two.*

The scrum shoved and the whole eight feet of it took a load that would
have gone through a fence, and held, and he felt the note start to run
in it — that thin bright wrongness he had known since he was seven years
old, the sound of a piece finding out what it was made of.

*Three.*

The two men came off it and got up and went the other way. The woman
scrambled inboard on all fours. Somebody dragged the boy backwards by the
collar of his smock into the crowd, and he was shouting, and he was
shouting *at people*, which meant he was fine.

*Four.*

It went.

It did not shatter and fly; it simply stopped being a wall. The whole eight
feet of it let go at once along its own note with a sound like a dropped
cup, and came apart into blue-grey grit, and about a third of it went over
the edge into the fog and the rest lay along the kerb-line in a low ridge
of gravel that a hundred and thirty pairs of feet had ground into nothing
within about a minute.

Nobody saw it.

That was not luck. He had known it would be that way before he put his
hands down, in the same instant he decided, because he had been reading
crowds since he was thirteen: a hundred and thirty people at the moment of
a rout are a hundred and thirty people all looking at the same thing, and
the thing was six hundred feet away at the other end of the bridge, and
the one direction nobody in a panic ever looks is *down at their own
feet*.

---

He sat back on his heels at the edge of a road over the sea with his hands
in his lap, and the crowd went past him both ways, and he began to shake
so hard that he had to put a fist against his sternum and hold it there.

*Four seconds,* he thought.

He had had four seconds since he was fifteen years old.

He had had four seconds on a bench above a river with a hammer and a spoon
and thirty-four worthless offcuts; four seconds in the mouth of a culvert
at Tomari with a professional's hand on the other side of it; four seconds
at the bottom of every single one of the four hundred nights he had spent
trying to make it five. He had costed a man's life at four hundred
thousand ryō to buy the sentence that would have made it more. He had sat
in a noodle shop opposite the only other person in the world who did what
he did and had had to say, out loud, to her face: *four.*

Four seconds had been the humiliation at the exact centre of his life.

And he knelt on that bridge in the sun with grit under his knees and
worked out, at last, four years and one country too late, that four
seconds had never once been a defect.

It was a *specification*. That was all it had ever been. It was a number,
like the width of a chisel or the reach of a plane, and there is no such
thing as a bad number — there are only jobs it fits and jobs it does not,
and every single job he had ever chosen for himself had been one it did
not fit, because he had spent four years choosing jobs by what would keep
him alive.

Nobody had ever given him one it fitted.

He had had to walk onto somebody else's bridge, in somebody else's
country, in a fight that had nothing to do with him and that he had
changed by not one syllable, and put his hands down on a kerb at the exact
second a child came through a gap with his head turned —

— and it had been *enough*. It had been exactly, precisely, and entirely
enough, with nothing to spare and nothing wanting.

Sazare Ruri knelt on the Great Naruto Bridge with his hands shaking in his
lap and cried, briefly and without any noise at all, in the middle of a
hundred and thirty people, and not one of them noticed that either.
