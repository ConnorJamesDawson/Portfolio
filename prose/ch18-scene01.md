# Chapter 18, Scene 1 — The Thin Coast

*Act 3, part A. The crossing to the Land of Waves. Sazare Ruri, age
sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

The coaster ran south for four days under a sky the colour of dishwater
and put him off at a place called Ubagi, which was not the Land of Waves
but was the last place with a proper harbour before it, and there he
waited nine days for a boat because nobody wanted to go.

Ubagi was warm. That was the first strange thing. He had come down out of
a country where the eleventh month meant frost on the inside of the
window and the sea the colour of a knife, and four days later he was
standing on a quay in his coat sweating, with the light coming off the
water in flat white sheets that made him squint, and gulls sitting on
every bollard with their beaks open in the heat like dogs.

The water here was not grey. It was green — a thick jade green over sand,
so clear at the harbour steps that he stood and looked down at his own
boots' reflection wobbling over a bottom eight feet under, with the shells
and the ribs of an old sunk skiff and a small shoal of something silver
going round and round the mooring chain in a slow circuit; and he had been
looking at the sea since he was born and had never once in his life seen
the bottom of it.

The smell was different too. At Kasado the harbour smelt of tar, weed and
cold. Ubagi smelt of tar, weed, hot pitch, frying oil, and the sweetish
rotten-fruit stink of the fish waste they threw off the end of the quay
for the gulls, and under all of it a thin green smell off the trees on the
hill behind the town, which came down at evening when the wind dropped and
put a taste in your mouth like cut grass.

He sat on a coil of rope in that smell for nine days, in the sun, with his
sleeves rolled and his bad leg out straight, mending things for the
harbour, and by the fourth day he had stopped noticing that he was warm,
and by the ninth he had almost — almost — stopped waking at the third
hour.

---

Nobody wanted to go to the Land of Waves.

He got the reason out of six different people and it was the same reason
six times, given in slightly different words with the same shrug at the
end of it, and the words were *there's nothing there any more*.

"Was there ever?" he asked the sixth one, a woman with a boat.

She was about forty, burnt to the colour of a saddle by thirty years on
the water, with grey coming in two streaks off her temples and a
sun-bleached cotton wrap over her head knotted at the back like a
fishwife's, and forearms with the deep hard tendon-lines of somebody who
had hauled net every day since she was nine. She had four fingers on her
left hand and the stump of the fifth was old and white and perfectly
healed.

She was called Sada and she looked at him over a coil of line she was
whipping and laughed with her mouth shut.

"Was there *ever*." She bit the twine. "Ten years ago that channel was so
thick with hulls you couldn't cross it under sail. Sardine, mackerel, dried
squid, and the finest bloody oyster on this whole coast. I've run into
Nami with a deck load and had four men bidding on it before my line was
fast." She pulled the whipping tight. "Now I go in twice a season with
lamp oil and salt and I get paid in what they've got, and last spring what
they'd got was a pair of brass candlesticks and a girl's wedding chest,
and I took them, because if I don't go in with the oil they'll be burning
fish fat by winter."

"What happened?"

"A man happened," said Sada. "You'll see him written up on the side of
every warehouse in the place. Get in the boat."

---

They went out on a Tuesday at first light in a sea like grey silk with the
sun not up, and it was the best crossing of his life and he never got over
it.

Sada's boat was twenty-six feet, single-masted, tarred black to the
waterline and above it painted a red that had gone the pink of an old
brick, with her name in white on the bow in a hand that had been done by
somebody in a hurry about a decade ago and touched up twice since by
somebody worse. The sail was patched in four places, the patches all
different weights of canvas, and one of them had been a rice sack once and
still had a smudge of the merchant's mark ghosted on it.

Sazare sat forward with his back against the mast partner and watched a
country he had never seen come up out of the sea.

It came up in layers. First a smell — pine, and mud at low water, and
woodsmoke — arriving twenty minutes before there was anything to look at.
Then a smudge that was not cloud. Then, as the light came up behind them
and turned the sea from grey silk to hammered pewter to blue, the smudge
went green: hills, dark green, dropping straight into the water with no
beach at all in most places, the pines coming down to the tideline and
standing in it, and white mist lying along the folds of them in long
horizontal bars that broke up and reformed as the sun got at them.

Then the islands. Dozens of them, hundreds, humped and steep and every one
of them wooded, some of them no bigger than a house, with a single wind-bent
pine on top and a fringe of orange weed round the bottom like a tidemark on
a bath. Between them the water went from green to almost black where it was
deep and the channels wound in and out and back on themselves so that four
separate times Sazare was certain they were sailing into a closed bay and
four times a gap opened in the trees at the last minute.

"Nobody comes here who hasn't been here," said Sada, at the tiller, with
the wind off her right cheek and the sun on her face and thirty years of
this on it. "That's the whole country in one sentence. There's fourteen
ways into Nami and thirteen of them will put your keel through the top of
a rock at half tide."

The sea slapped and knocked along the strake beside his ear. The pines
went past close enough to smell. Somewhere in among the islands a
cormorant came up off the water with a great deal of effort and flogged
away low, and the air was already warm at seven in the morning, and Sazare
sat in the bow of a stranger's boat with his hands on the wet thwart and
thought, with a clarity that startled him:

*I have never in my life been anywhere beautiful.*

He had been to fourteen towns since the fog came down. Every single one of
them had been a place he was assessing. He had walked into all of them
counting doors, and out of most of them at speed, and it had never
occurred to him that a place could be a thing you simply looked at, and
he sat with that for about a mile and could not make it go anywhere, and
in the end put it in the tray with everything else.

---

The bridge came round a headland at about the ninth hour and he stood up
in the boat.

"Sit down," said Sada.

He sat down. He did not stop looking at it.

It came out of the trees on the mainland shore about four hundred yards
out over the channel and then it stopped, in mid-air, over open water, in
a squared-off unfinished end with the reinforcement rods standing up out
of it like the bristles of a brush. Grey concrete, blackened along the
underside with weather-run. Two ranks of piers marching out under it into
the deep, each one a fat pale column with a green-black beard of weed
round its foot, and the last three piers standing beyond the end of the
deck with nothing on top of them at all, waiting.

It was enormous. That was the thing about it that he had not been ready
for. He had been told there was a bridge being built and he had assembled
in his head a bridge — a decent country bridge, a hundred feet, a couple
of spans — and this was a *road*, a road out over the sea, wide enough for
two carts to pass, going out from a poor country toward a rich one across
a mile of open water, and about a third of it existed.

There was a crane on the end of it, a wooden one, hand-cranked, with a man
sitting in the shade of it doing nothing.

"That's it, then," said Sazare.

"That's it."

"How long's it been going?"

"Two years and a bit." Sada put the tiller over and the boom came across
and the island slid away behind them and the whole bridge swung round and
lay along the horizon like a sentence with no full stop. "Longer if you
count the arguing."

"Who's paying for it?"

Sada laughed her shut-mouthed laugh again.

"Nobody's paying for it, love," she said. "That's why it's not finished."

---

Nami itself sat in a bay at the end of a channel with the hills coming
down round it on three sides, and it was built half on land and half on
water: the shore-side houses on the mud with their backs to the hill and
their fronts on stilts, and beyond them a whole quarter of the town
standing out over the shallows on piles, connected by plank walks, so that
the tide came in underneath everybody's floor twice a day and went out
again and left a smell.

He had never seen anything like it and he looked at it the only way he
knew how, which was as a set of problems somebody had solved.

The piles were larch, or something like it, black below the waterline and
silver-grey above, and they had been driven at some point by people who
knew exactly what they were doing, in ranks, with cross-bracing, and the
bracing was mortised and pegged and not one peg of it was iron, because
iron in salt water is a five-year mistake.

And every single one of them was overdue for work.

That was what he saw. He stood in the bow as Sada brought her in under the
walkway and read the underside of that town like a page: two dozen piles
scarfed and patched with whatever had come to hand; a walkway plank
replaced with a bit of somebody's door, still with the latch mortise in
it; a section of handrail gone entirely and a rope strung in its place,
and the rope not new; and everywhere, on everything, the particular
grey-green fur of neglect that a wooden town gets when its people stop
being able to afford tar.

The whole place was standing on a fifteen-year-old good idea that nobody
had been able to maintain for four years, and it was going to be fine, and
fine, and fine, and then one winter it was not going to be fine.

Under the walkway a child of about seven sat on the cross-bracing with her
feet in the water, fishing with a hand-line and no float, and watched the
boat come in with the flat unembarrassed stare of children everywhere.
She had a scabbed knee and a red cotton shift washed almost white and hair
cut short by somebody using a knife rather than scissors. There was a
bucket beside her.

Sazare looked at the bucket as they went past, because he was constitutionally
incapable of not looking at things.

There was nothing in it.

---

They came alongside at the town quay, which was stone, and old, and the
best-built thing he had seen since he arrived, and Sada made fast to a
ring set into it and swore about the ring, which was rusted almost through.

The warehouses stood along the quay in a row: five of them, big, wooden,
with wide sliding doors and the roofs sagging in the middle the way roofs
do when nobody has been up there. Four of the five had their doors shut
and padlocked and the padlocks were bright.

Every one of them had the same thing painted on the side, six feet high,
in black, in a hard commercial hand, over the top of whatever had been
written there before:

**GATŌ COMPANY**

Sazare stood on the quay in the sun with his pack at his feet and looked
at it.

It was very well painted. That was the detail his eye went to and would
not leave: the letters were plumb, evenly spaced, properly cut in at the
corners, the black laid on in two coats over a primer, on a wall that had
not been primed for anything else in a decade. Somebody had been paid
properly to do that lettering, and had brought good paint, and had taken
their time, and had done it on five buildings in a town where a child sat
under the walkway fishing into an empty bucket.

He had spent a year in the border country working for exactly this. He
knew the species. He had taken two hundred ryō off a fat man in a
counting-room to remove an inconvenience from a strait, and the men who
had paid for it had needed to go on standing on that quay afterwards and
looking people in the face, and had managed it perfectly well.

"You said a man happened," he said.

"I did."

"He's not hiding it."

"Hiding it?" Sada came up the steps with a coil over her shoulder and
stopped beside him and looked up at six feet of very good black lettering
with an expression he would think about for a long time afterwards,
because it was not anger. It was the face of a woman reading a weather
sign. "Love, he had it *painted*. He paid a man from the mainland to come
over on a boat with a ladder and do it nice. That's not a man hiding. Do
you know what that is?"

"Yes," said Sazare.

And he did, and he could have said it in one word, and the word was
*confidence*; and he stood on a stone quay in the warm with the green
hills going up behind the town and the unfinished bridge lying out across
the mouth of the bay four hundred yards away, and understood that he had
finally arrived in a country where he was not the most dangerous thing
walking about.
