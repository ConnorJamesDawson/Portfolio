# Chapter 61, Scene 3 — What Happens To It Now

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the Hokage's tower, the long room. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~2010 words.*

---

They left at ten on the second and they did not want a send-off, and got
one anyway, because Kanzaki had heard about the second-floor room from
somebody and had come up the hill at half past nine with a covered
basket and a face that dared anybody to say anything about it.

"It's dressing," she said.

"...I beg your pardon," said Fukasaku.

"Not that sort. Chicken and rice and a thing with plums in it." She put
the basket on the table. "You have walked four hundred miles and you
have been in this village five days and nobody has fed you properly
because they have all been busy being clever at each other, and I am
seventy-two and I do the stores."

Fukasaku looked at the basket for a moment.

"How did you know we were here?" he said.

"I did not know you were here," said Kanzaki, "and I still do not, and I
have not been up this staircase this morning, and I am going back down
it now."

She went back down it.

Shima watched her the whole way to the door.

"I like her," she said.

---

"Right," said Fukasaku. "Before we go. There's one thing and it isn't a
condition, because the conditions are finished and I got all four of
them and two of them were better than I'd hoped.

"It's an ask, and you can refuse it, and I'd rather have it refused this
morning than agreed to and then quietly not done."

Tsunade was at the head of the table.

"Say it."

"I want a copy of the minute."

---

Sazare had known it was coming since about half past two in the morning
and had been round it four times and had got nowhere both ways, and he
watched Tsunade's face do absolutely nothing at all.

"Go on," she said.

"Nothing has ever gone out of Myōboku unrecorded," said Fukasaku. "I
said that in a letter and I meant it and I have been keeping to it since
before this village had a wall.

"And I have not got a record. I have got five days in a tower and a
minute book in your hand in your building, and if I walk out of here
with nothing then in four hundred years the only thing Myōboku has got
about the second of the twelfth month is *me*.

"And I am four hundred and some. I do not know what that is in your
terms and neither does anybody. It is not for ever."

He tapped the stick.

"There is a second half and it is the honest one.

"I have sat on a wall in the rain and watched a man make a thing nobody
has a word for, and he died, and it went out, and I have carried it on
my own for four hundred years and told nobody, and last night I found
out what it was worth in a room, which is nothing.

"I am not going to do that twice."

---

"I want the argument against," he said, before anybody could speak. "Give
me it properly. I would rather lose to a good one."

"There are four," said Tsunade.

"Have them."

"One. It's a foreign holding. Not hostile — I want that said, and I'd
say it in front of the council and I'd lose the vote — but Myōboku is
not Konohagakure and it is not under my law and there is not one line of
treaty in the world that covers it.

"Two. Once there are two copies there are three. That is not a
suspicion, that's arithmetic. Every document that has ever gone to two
places has gone to a third inside a generation and I have watched it
happen to four things that mattered.

"Three. It is not mine to give twice. That book has five names in it and
two of them are yours and one is Katsuyu's and one is Shizune's, and if
it goes anywhere it goes with all five agreeing, and I am not putting
that to a vote in a room where four of the five want to say yes.

"Four." She put a hand on the book. "And this one is the real one.

"It's the only evidence in the world. Not the best. The *only*. If it
burns or it walks, then this village is back to a slug's word and
nineteen pages of argument, and a boy who has to spend the next thirty
years being politely disbelieved.

"So every additional place it exists is a place it can be lost from, and
every additional place it exists is a place it can be *taken* from, and
I have not got a way to weigh those against each other that comes out
clean."

Fukasaku sat with that.

"That's four good ones," he said.

"They're the four I've got."

"You've not said no."

"I've not said no," said Tsunade, "because I have been at this since two
o'clock this morning and I have not found the answer yet, and I am not
going to give you one before I have got it, and you are going to have to
sit in that basket for another eleven minutes."

---

Sazare had been standing at the window and turned round.

"Can I put a thing in," he said.

"Go on."

"It's not the document."

Fukasaku's head came round.

"Say that again, lad."

"You said you have not got a record and that in four hundred years the
only thing Myōboku has about last night is you. And you said it like
that was the weak version.

"It isn't. It's the strong one."

He came away from the window.

"A copy is paper. Paper burns and paper walks and paper can be taken off
a mountain by somebody who wants it, and the whole of your argument
against carrying it in your own head is that you are not for ever.

"You are more for ever than a sheet of pressed leaf, and you have just
proved it.

"You told us a thing last night from four hundred years ago that you had
never once said out loud, and you had it in order, and you had the
season and the rain and the wall and the fact that he laughed at you
twice. That is not a decaying record. That is better than every human
document I have ever handled and I have handled about nine thousand.

"And you did not lose it in four centuries of nobody asking."

---

"That's oral tradition," said Fukasaku.

"Yes."

"That's *theirs*."

"Yes," said Sazare.

There was a considerable silence in the long room.

"Say the rest of it," said Shima, from the table.

"You want it recorded because a record is what makes it honest at both
ends," said Sazare. "That's the same argument as the contract and it is
the same argument you brought through that door on the twenty-eighth,
and it is a good argument and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

"And it produced the wall in the rain.

"You had no contract with that man and no record of him and you have
carried him for four hundred years anyway, and the reason it went out
like a lamp is not that Myōboku failed to file it. It is that there was
nobody standing next to him.

"You were standing next to us. Five days, on foot, no contract, and you
watched the whole of it, and you asked the question at the front that
nobody else thought to ask, and it is *in* you now the same way he is.

"That is not a worse outcome than a sheet of paper.

"It is the only kind of record that cannot be stolen and cannot be
burned and cannot be read by somebody it would help."

He stopped.

"And I want to be honest that I have got a motive," he said. "I would
rather there was one copy in the world and I have had that position
since about half past two this morning, and everything I have just said
is true and I would still have said it if it weren't."

---

Fukasaku laughed.

It was one short noise again, like a board, and he put the stick across
his knees.

"Well," he said. "There it is. I have come four hundred miles to find
out whether the slugs have got something we haven't, and I have got my
answer, and it is not the one I was expecting and it has been handed to
me by a human.

"Four hundred years of *nothing goes out unrecorded*, and the one thing
I have got that nobody can take off me is the thing I never wrote down."

He looked at the sill.

Katsuyu had been there since eight and had not said one word all
morning.

"Have you got a view, lass?" he said.

"I have got one and I have been deciding for an hour whether it is mine
to say."

"Say it."

"We do not keep it because it is safe," said Katsuyu. "I want to be
accurate, because he has made a very good argument for you and it is not
quite our reason and I do not want you to go home with the wrong one.

"We keep it in the telling because a thing that is told has to be told
*to somebody*. It requires a second creature, every time, for ever. A
book does not. A book will sit in a drawer for four hundred years and be
perfectly well kept and nobody will have said one word to anybody.

"That is the whole of it and it is not a security argument and I have
never heard anybody make it out loud, including me, until this minute."

---

"Right," said Fukasaku.

He got up.

"I'll take the memory, then," he said, "and I'll not have the paper, and
I want that minuted before I go — that I asked for a copy, that I was
given four good reasons and a fifth one I did not expect, and that I
withdrew the ask myself and was not refused.

"And I want it minuted why.

"Because in about ninety years somebody on my mountain is going to find
that line and want to know why the old man came home empty-handed, and I
would like them to have it in the right order."

Shizune wrote it down.

"And I'll say the other thing to the room and then we're off," said
Fukasaku, "and it's for the boy and it will keep about four seconds.

"There'll be a second one of these.

"Not tomorrow and not this year. But you have got one instance and one
instance is a story, and there is a man somewhere in this village with
wood in him that was put there, and you have all been very carefully not
saying his name for five days and I have noticed every time.

"When you go and get him — and you will — I want to know.

"Not the paper. Come and tell me."

---

They went down at ten past.

Jiraiya took them to the western approach and Sazare went as far as the
tower steps and no further, because Fukasaku had said, on the landing,
without turning round, that a send-off past the door was a thing you did
for a summon and that he had not been one.

He stood on the steps in the cold.

Shima stopped at the bottom.

"Boy."

"Yes."

"You were wrong twice in five days and both times you found it off the
work and not off being told."

"Yes."

"Keep doing that," she said, "because you are going to be wrong about
four more things before the year's out, and the ones that get people
killed are the ones nobody puts a foot on a table about."

And then they went off down the hill, on foot, in the ordinary
morning, and nobody in the village turned round.
