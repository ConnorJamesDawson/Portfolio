# Chapter 23, Scene 2 — The Second Job

*Act 4, part A. Konoha General, second floor. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen.
POV: Sazare. ~2,350 words.*

---

Nurse Yagi was thirty-four and had a voice like a saw and had been on
that floor for eleven years, and on the first Monday she looked him up and
down and said, "Right. You're the one who did the window in six," and
Sazare said yes, and she said, "Good. There's four of them on this floor
and I've been asking for two years," and that was the whole of the
introduction.

She was tall and angular with her hair scraped up under a cap and a
permanent grey shadow under both eyes, and she moved down that ward like
somebody working a production line, and she talked continuously — to the
patients, to him, to herself, to the trolley — and did not once in three
months tell him anything twice.

He did dressings for six weeks.

Not interesting dressings. The ward had eleven beds and about half of them
at any time were the specific unglamorous population of a shinobi hospital
in peacetime, which is not what anybody imagines: an old woman with a leg
ulcer that had been open for four years; two burns off a kitchen fire in
the civilian quarter; a genin of thirteen who had put a training kunai
through his own foot and was extremely embarrassed about it; a jōnin of
about forty with a shoulder that had been rebuilt twice and would not
stay rebuilt; and, in the corner bed by the window, a man with a spinal
injury who was not going to get up and who everybody on that floor was
extremely kind to and nobody looked at directly.

Sazare did dressings, and did them well, and Nurse Yagi corrected him
eleven times in the first week and four in the second and once in the
sixth, and the once in the sixth was about the tension on a bandage over a
joint and he never got it wrong again.

He also, over those six weeks, mended: the window in six, the window in
nine, three trolley wheels, a drip stand, the catch on the dirty-linen
chute, a wheelchair with a footplate that had been wired on for two years,
a pair of surgical scissors that somebody had dropped and sprung, and the
handle of the ward door, which had been coming off in people's hands since
before Nurse Yagi arrived and which took him nine minutes.

"You know that's not what you're for," she said, at the door, watching.

"No," said Sazare, "but it took nine minutes and forty people a day use
it."

"Mm," said Nurse Yagi, and went and told Sister Kaburagi about it, and
Sazare found out about that four months later from somebody else.

---

The question about the chakra came up in the seventh week and it turned
into paperwork, because everything did.

It came up because of a boy. He was eleven and he had come off a roof and
had a compound fracture of the forearm, and he had been dealt with
properly by a medic on the ground floor and sent up to the ward to mend,
and on the fourth day the wound over the break was not closing and was
angry at the edges and everybody was watching it.

Sazare, who had been dressing it twice a day, said, "Can I ask something
technical?"

"Go on."

"When a medic closes something. What actually happens?"

Nurse Yagi looked at him for a moment over the boy's arm.

"You stimulate the tissue to do what it was going to do anyway, faster,"
she said. "That's it. That's the whole of it and every book makes it sound
grander. You put chakra in and you hold it *even* and the cells run at
about forty times, and if your control's bad you get a mess and a scar and
sometimes something worse, which is why they don't let people near it
until they can hold a leaf on their forehead for an hour."

"How even?"

"Very."

"How long can you hold it?"

"About twenty minutes on a good day," said Nurse Yagi, "which is
unremarkable, and I'd be a great deal further on if I'd had any control
worth speaking of at nineteen, and that is the entire story of my career."
She wound the bandage off. "Why?"

"Because I can hold still water for eleven hours," said Sazare.

Nurse Yagi stopped with the bandage in her hand.

"Say that again."

---

It went to Sister Kaburagi that afternoon, and Sister Kaburagi went white
about the mouth and said, "Absolutely not, he is on a combat restriction
and I am not losing this hospital's licence to teach because a porter is
clever," and then said, "and I want it in writing that I refused, and then
I want somebody to *ask*."

So it went up on a form.

Sazare saw the form. Ashida Kōhei brought it round to his room in the
evening, out of a mixture of goodwill and pure nosiness, and it was a
buff-coloured request-for-ruling with about nine boxes, and Sister
Kaburagi had filled it in herself in a small furious hand, and the
substance of it was:

*Query. Subject is a probationary resident under a total prohibition on
combat training in any discipline (ref. attached). Hospital wishes to
instruct subject in basic medical ninjutsu (Mystical Palm, foundation
level) in the course of ward duty. Medical ninjutsu is a shinobi
discipline. Is instruction in it "combat training" for the purposes of the
prohibition? Ward requires a ruling before proceeding and will not proceed
without one.*

It came back in four days.

There was one sentence written across the bottom of it in a hand that had
signed about forty thousand documents and had got faster than it was
legible, in the same black ink as the countersignature:

*Approved. Medical ninjutsu is not combat training and I decline to pretend
otherwise. Anyone who wishes to argue the point may come and see me. — S.*

And underneath, in a different pen, smaller, plainly added afterwards:

*Enter it in his book like everything else.*

---

He learned it in the sluice room, standing up, on Tuesday and Thursday
evenings after the shift, out of the hospital's own time, from a
thirty-four-year-old ward nurse who had never taught anybody anything and
was ferociously bad at it for about a fortnight and then got good.

And he was terrible at it, and he was terrible at it in a way that
surprised everybody including himself.

The control was not the problem. He got the emission steady inside two
sessions — flat, even, unvarying, held without effort — and Nurse Yagi
watched the meter-fish twitch in its dish and said a word Sister Kaburagi
would not have permitted on the ward.

The problem was that a body *moves*.

He had spent nine years putting chakra into things that stayed where they
were put. Sand in a tray. Clay. Grit. Wet stone. Render. The whole of his
craft — the whole of four hundred years of his family's craft — was
predicated on a medium that had no opinion, and the entire art was to make
the material do a thing and then to *stop*, and the material would still
be doing that thing in eighty years.

A forearm is not like that. A forearm has a pulse in it. It swells and
goes down over the course of an hour. It is warm at the surface and warmer
underneath and the warmth moves. The tissue at the edge of a wound is
doing something already, on its own, at its own rate, with its own
intentions, and it does not stop when you stop and it does not wait for
you.

For nine days he tried to make it hold still.

He knew he was doing it and could not not do it. Every instinct he had
went the same way: define the boundary, hold it, drive the growth to the
boundary, stop. And every time, the flesh under his hands did what flesh
does, which is to say something slightly different from what it had done
the minute before, and the whole of his careful architecture arrived at a
place that had moved, and the result was a closure like a bad seam and
Nurse Yagi opening it again with an expression of enormous patience.

"You're *building*," she said, on the ninth evening, in the sluice, at
about the eleventh hour of a long day. "I don't know what it is you do
with the stone but you're doing it here and it doesn't work here. Stop
telling it. Nothing in a body wants telling."

"Then what does it want?"

"Bloody hell," said Nurse Yagi. "I don't know. Nobody says it in words. It
wants — " she made a gesture, flat, both hands, palms down, moving apart
and settling. "*That.*"

Sazare looked at the gesture for about four seconds.

Then he sat down on an upturned crate in a sluice room and put his hands
over his face and said something in the Kiri lower-district dialect that
Nurse Yagi did not catch and would not have understood.

---

Because it was the *smoothing*.

That was the whole of it. That was the entire thing and it had been in his
hands since he was seven years old and it had a name in his family, and
the name was so ordinary that nobody had ever once thought of it as a
technique.

*A frame's copper web spreads the feed. It takes an uneven hand and evens
it, grain by grain, level as a glass. It doesn't shape anything. It's just
there, and everything comes out flat.*

Two jobs. The lattice did two jobs, and he had separated them on a bench
above a river at fifteen: **shape** and **smoothing**. And every single
thing he had done since then — the fixtures, the bar in the culvert, the
lip on the kerb-line at the bridge, four seconds of ugly one-course wall —
had been *shape*, because shape was what a fight wanted and shape was what
he had been trying to buy with four hundred thousand ryō.

And the other half — the half his mother could hold under a lattice for six
hours without stopping and had never in her life known was unusual — was
not a lesser technique that the frame did for you.

It was a *different discipline entirely*.

It was: put something in, at a rate, and hold the rate absolutely level,
and shape *nothing*, and let the material do its own work, and do not
stop, and do not correct, and above all do not tell it anything.

He had been raised inside it. He had scrubbed the copper that did it. He
had spent eight months on a bench trying to get better at the other half
and had never once thought about this one, because in the trade it was not
a skill, it was what the frame was for.

---

He closed his first wound on the eleventh evening.

It was two inches long, on the inside of his own left forearm, made by
Nurse Yagi with a scalpel with a great deal of muttering about paperwork,
and it took him four minutes and eleven seconds and left him grey and
sweating and unable to hold a cup steady for a quarter of an hour
afterwards.

Nurse Yagi did an identical one on her own arm in about nineteen seconds,
without looking at it, while talking.

"Right," she said. "Now. Before you get pleased with yourself, and you
are about to, so listen.

"That took you four minutes and it cost you a day's work. It cost me
nineteen seconds and about as much as walking up the stairs. I am an
ordinary ward nurse of no distinction whatsoever and there are four people
in this building who would do it in six seconds and one of them is
seventy-one.

"You have not learned medical ninjutsu. You have learned *one* thing, at
the foundation grade, on a clean cut, on a person who was not moving, in a
sluice, with all night to do it in. You cannot do a break, you cannot do
an organ, you cannot do a burn, you cannot do anything at all under
pressure, and if you tried any of that on a patient on my ward I would put
you through the window in nine."

"I know."

"You don't, but you will." She rolled her sleeve down. "The control's the
best I've ever seen and it will not save you, because control is about a
third of it and the rest is knowing what you're looking at, and *that* you
have not got, and it takes about nine years, and there is no way round it
and nobody has ever found one."

She put the scalpel in the tray.

"Same time Thursday," she said. "Bring your own arm."

---

He wrote it in the book that night, because Hiruzen Sarutobi had added a
line in a different pen and because Kanzaki Uta would have weighed the air
if she could.

*0187 | 4th, 1st month | nil — medical instruction, foundation, per
ruling | 0 g | n/a | S.R.*

He looked at it for a while with the persimmon tree bare outside the
window and the lamp going.

Every other line in that book was a *thing*. Three hundred numbered
lines, a description, a mass in grams, a disposition. It was a book for
objects and he had been filling it for two months with objects.

And here was a line with a nought in the mass column, because what he had
made that evening was two inches of somebody's forearm being slightly
sooner mended than it would otherwise have been, and there is no weight for
that.

Sazare Ruri sat at a low table in a foreign country at seventeen minutes
past the eleventh hour and looked at the first entry in the fourth ledger
of his life that did not describe a thing anybody could take away from
him.
