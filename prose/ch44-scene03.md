# Chapter 44, Scene 3 — What She Came Back For

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, a noodle house on the west lane. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,808 words.*

---

She asked on the fifteenth, at the end of a day, badly.

He had known it was coming for about four minutes, because she had
cleaned her brushes at ten past four instead of five and had then not
gone, and had stood at the far bench doing nothing with her hands,
which was a thing he had never once seen her do.

"The winter," she said, to the bench.

"Yes."

"I want—" She stopped and restarted it, which cost her something.
"I've been trying to ask you for three weeks and I keep getting to the
gate and going home, and I've decided the reason is that I don't know
how much to ask for, and if I ask for a bit you'll give me a bit and
I'll have to come back, and I'm not doing that twice."

"How much do you want."

"All of it."

Sazare closed the log.

"Then not here," he said. "Not because of Kanzaki. Because it takes
about an hour and a quarter and I'm not doing it standing up."

---

The noodle house on the west lane was empty at half past four, which
was why he had chosen it, and the woman who ran it took one look at the
two of them and put a pot down and went and stood at the far end by the
window and stayed there, which he noticed and was grateful for and
recorded.

Low room. A fan in the corner turning through about a hundred and ten
degrees and back. Steam and the brown smell of stock that had been
going since five in the morning, and outside, the fifth month getting
long and gold at the top of the lane where it met the main road.

He put the notebook on the table and did not open it.

"I'm going to do it in order," he said. "From the first day of the
eleventh month to the fourth of the second. Everything I have, in the
order it happened, with the conditions. I'll tell you what he said and
I'll keep my reading of it in brackets like the notebook does, and
you'll be able to hear the difference, and if you can't, stop me and
I'll say which is which."

"All right."

"There are things in it you won't want."

"I know."

"I'd rather say that now than get to one of them and have to decide in
front of you."

Kurihara put both hands flat on the table.

"Say all of it," she said.

---

It took an hour and twenty minutes.

He gave her the eleventh month: four evenings, the retainer laid first,
the reason said out loud and written down within the hour. The cold
room and the fire not lit and the wood in. Sitting down twice. Finishing
a sentence sitting. He gave her the twelfth: nothing, no sessions, a
note put under the hospital door on the ninth that said *not this week*
in a hand that was still perfectly steady.

He gave her the first month, which was three visits, one of them
useless, one of them the best hour of instruction he had ever had in
his life, and one of them a man in a chair with a blanket saying *ask
me the difficult ones now, I'm no good after four*.

He gave her the four questions. He gave her the last one, the one about
the seal on the seal, and the answer, and the fact that he had written
it up that night and had not gone back for eleven days because he had
had a shift pattern and had assumed there would be more evenings.

He gave her the second of the second month, when a neighbour sent to
the hospital.

He gave her the fourth.

And then he came to the funeral and he stopped, with the tea gone cold
in front of him and the fan going round, and he looked at the notebook
he had not opened.

There it was.

He could stop here. He could give her *four people, a clerk, an
intelligence specialist, a woman of sixty-one, and me,* which was true,
and complete as a list, and she would never in her life know there had
been anything else, because the only other person alive who had heard
it had said it once on wet gravel and gone home to the Land of Rivers.

He thought about it for about four seconds.

*A ledger with the flattering half in it is not a shorter ledger. It is
a different document and it is a lie.*

"There were four people at the funeral," he said. "A clerk from the
sealing office who hadn't known him and was there because somebody has
to be. An intelligence specialist. Me. And a woman of sixty-one who
came four days from the Land of Rivers."

"Who."

"A former student, from when he was still teaching at the Academy. She
didn't give me her name and I didn't ask, and that's a thing I'd change
if I could." He kept his voice flat, because flat was the only delivery
that would not make it worse. "He wrote to her in the autumn. He hadn't
written to her in eleven years. She came because of the letter."

The fan went round.

"He wrote to her," said Kurihara.

"Yes."

"In the autumn."

"Yes."

She sat completely still for a period he did not measure, and he did
not look away from her, because looking away would have been a decision
about her and he did not have the standing to make one.

"And not to me," she said.

"No."

"I was twenty minutes' walk."

"Yes."

"And you've told me that," said Kurihara, "when you didn't have to,
when you'd got to the end of the whole thing and you could have just
said *four people* and I'd never have known there was a fifth fact in
it."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Because you asked for all of it," said Sazare, "and because I've spent
eleven years finding out what happens to a person when somebody decides
which parts of the truth they can handle, and I've been on the wrong
end of it about nine hundred times, and I have never once been glad
about it afterwards."

---

She got up and went and stood by the door for a bit.

The woman at the far end by the window did not move and did not look
over and refilled nothing, which was the second correct thing she had
done in an hour and a half.

When Kurihara came back she was dry-eyed and furious about being
dry-eyed, which he could see in the set of the jaw and did not comment
on, and she sat down and pulled the tea over and drank a whole cup of
it cold in one go.

"I left because of a curriculum," she said.

He did not say anything.

"I wanted to write it down. All of it — the whole sealwright side, in
order, in stages, so it could be *taught*, so it didn't live in one
seventy-year-old man's hands in a cold room in the north lane." She
turned the cup. "He said no. He said the work is the work and you can't
put a ladder in a book, you can only put the rungs, and a man who
learns off rungs will fall the first time he needs to reach. And I said
— "

She stopped.

"I said that was the most convenient thing a man had ever said about
his own indispensability," she said, "and I said it in front of two
people, and he went absolutely white, and then he said something
considerably worse to me and I have never repeated it to anybody and
I'm not going to."

"And then it was a month."

"And then it was a month, and then it was a year, and then it was
stupid, and then it was too late to be stupid about." She put the cup
down. "It was about *filing*. Two years, over filing."

Sazare said nothing at all, because the thing was coming and he could
see it coming and there was no version of helping her to it that was
not a cruelty.

She got there on her own, about nine seconds later.

He watched it happen. Her hand went out flat on the table, exactly the
way it had gone flat on the bracket in his notebook in the yard, and
her face did nothing at all, and then she said, very quietly:

"He was compressing a syllabus."

"Yes."

"In the eleventh month. Four evenings. Retainer first, so it could be
taught in fewer steps." She was not looking at him. "That's the *book*.
That's my — that's the thing I said in front of two people, and he did
it, two years later, on his own, in a cold room, for somebody else, and
he never—"

She stopped.

"He never told me," she said.

"No."

"He wrote to a woman he hadn't seen in eleven years and he didn't write
to me and he was *doing what I asked*." Her voice went, once, on the
last word, and came back. "That's worse. Do you understand that that is
worse than everything else you've told me this evening put together."

"Yes," said Sazare. "I do, actually."

---

They sat there for a while and the fan went round and the light came
off the top of the lane and went orange on the paper screen.

"Right," said Kurihara, eventually.

She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, once, briskly, the way
you'd deal with a bench spill.

"Thank you for the funeral," she said.

"That's the part that hurt."

"That's the part that hurt, and it's the part I'd have spent the rest
of my life not knowing, and I'd have gone on thinking he simply stopped
thinking about anybody at the end, which is what I have been telling
myself for three months because it is the only version I could live
in." She stood up and got her coat off the hook. "You gave me a worse
man and a truer one. I'd rather have the true one. Most people won't do
that and I don't think you know that most people won't do that."

He paid, which she argued about and lost, and they went out into the
lane.

At the corner she stopped.

"I'll be in the yard on Thursday," she said.

And Sazare, who had spent eleven days doing arithmetic in a log about
nine visits with no work order, heard a tenth being announced out loud
in advance for the first time, and had absolutely no idea what to do
with it, and said:

"All right."
