# Chapter 23, Scene 3 — Ninety Seconds

*Act 4, part A. Konoha General, the receiving room. Sazare Ruri, age
sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

The man came in at the second hour of the morning on a wet Saturday in the
first month, off a barrow, with three of his friends and about half of the
civilian quarter behind him.

Sazare was on the night rota for the first time — Sister Kaburagi ran the
nights on a four-week rotation and had put him on it because everybody
went on it — and he was in the receiving room doing the least interesting
job in the building, which was restocking the shelves in the dressing
press, when the doors went.

There were six of them shouting at once. There was a barrow with a wheel
that squealed. There was a man of about thirty on it in a state Sazare
recognised at twenty feet across a lit room without having to think about
it at all: grey, wet with sweat, breathing in the wrong shape, both hands
over the left side of his own body low down under the ribs and his fingers
dark to the wrist.

There was, on the tiles, in the doorway and then in a line across the
floor to the trolley, the specific bright arterial trail of a man who has
been carried, and the drops were the right shape for a person moving at a
run.

Somebody had put a folded jacket over the wound and somebody else had been
holding it and had lost it in the transfer and there was a lot of blood
where there did not need to have been.

"Table," said Nurse Yagi.

They got him over. Sazare took the legs because he was standing at the
legs, and the man made a noise going onto the table, and the friends went
on shouting until a porter got them into the corridor.

Then a medic came down the stairs at a walk.

---

Her name was Michiba and she was about forty-five and she was the duty
medical shinobi that night, and she was still doing the tie on her apron
when she came through the door and she did not hurry once.

"What have I got?"

"Knife. Left, under the ribs, about here." Nurse Yagi had the shirt open
and the jacket off in one movement. "Went in about the tenth hour, they
carried him from the Nakadōri, he's been down about four minutes."

"Right." Michiba put two fingers on the wound edge and her palm flat and
held it for perhaps a second and a half with her eyes somewhere in the
middle distance.

"Spleen's intact. Gone in under and forward, missed everything that
matters and opened a vessel at the back of it about the width of a
pencil. That's all this is." She rolled her sleeves. "Yagi, pressure four
inches medial while I'm in there. You — hold that lamp where I put it and
don't move it, and if you're going to be sick be sick behind me."

Sazare held the lamp.

It took ninety seconds.

He counted, because he could not not, and it was ninety seconds and the
last twenty of them were her being thorough about something he could not
see. The green light came up off her hands and went into the wound and
stayed absolutely level, and she talked the entire time — to Nurse Yagi,
about the pressure; to the man, about how he was going to be very sore
tomorrow and would live to be extremely stupid again — and at the end of
it she wiped her hands and stood back and said, "He'll want watching for
bleeding overnight and he'll want a talk from somebody about knives, and
I'm going back to bed."

The man on the table was thirty and had been drunk at the tenth hour and
had said something to somebody in a bar about somebody else's sister.

He was going to be fine.

---

Sazare put the lamp back on its hook and went out through the doors into
the yard at the back where the incinerator was, and stood in the rain
against the wall with his hands flat on the brick behind him, and was not
sick, and did the arithmetic instead, because he had never once in his
life been able to do anything else.

*Ninety seconds.*

*Left side, low, under the ribs, going in under and forward. Vessel at the
back of it about the width of a pencil.*

*That is the same wound.*

*That is not a similar wound and it is not a comparable wound. That is the
same wound, in the same place, at the same angle, made by a shorter blade
into a heavier man, and it took a woman of forty-five ninety seconds with
her sleeves rolled while she talked about something else.*

He stood in the rain in a yard behind a hospital in the Land of Fire and
went through it properly, item by item, the way he did everything, because
there is no version of Sazare Ruri that gets to look away from a thing
once he has started laying it out.

*Nakabori Sōhachi. Fifty-one. Kanezaki, the salt gangway, the eleventh
month, about the sixth hour of the evening.*

*Knife wound low and to the back, into the meat above the hip and along the
bone — that one would not have killed him in six hours and I knew it at
the time.*

*And a crystal spur, out of my own palm, under the ribs on the left, going
in about four inches, under and forward.*

*Which was survivable.*

*Which I knew was survivable, because my hands told me the depth and the
angle in about four seconds in the dark and I have been able to read a
wound with my fingers since I was fourteen years old.*

*And I knew where the pressure went. I knew it exactly. High, under the
arch of the ribs, packed hard and held, for an hour if necessary, while
somebody ran — and it was a river port with eleven thousand people in it
and a physician on Kanmuri Street and a shout would have carried four
hundred yards.*

*And I put my fist three inches to the left, on the soft of the belly, and
held it there for eleven minutes, carefully, so that it would look from
any distance like a boy doing everything he could.*

*And he talked to me for eight of them about the harbourmaster's lamps.*

*Ninety seconds.*

Sazare stood in the rain with his palms on the brick and found that he had
been standing there for some time.

---

It was not remorse. He had had remorse; he had had it in a lime kiln with
two broken ribs and on a bollard at Kasado in the dark and every single
night since, and remorse was a weather he lived in and had made
arrangements about.

This was a different thing entirely and it was much worse, and it had a
shape, and the shape was *arithmetic*.

Because once you have watched a woman close that wound in ninety seconds
you cannot stop, and he did not stop, and he went through the whole of it
standing in a yard in the rain in the first month, in order, and there
were three lists.

**The ones he made.** Two, precisely, and he had never allowed himself to
call the number out loud before. A factor at Kanezaki. A chandler at
Kasado, who had gone into eighteen inches of cold water on a shelving
beach at the top of the ebb, and who had not needed a medic, because
nobody has ever invented the technique that gets a person out of the sea
once they have been held under for the second minute.

*That one nothing would have fixed. I did that one properly.*

He put his head back against the wall.

**The ones he was too late for.** Tose, at Yumoto, in the tenth month, who
had gone in eleven days and who Tōan had said from the second day would go
in eleven days, and had been right, and had known exactly what was wrong
and exactly what it would take and had not had it. *There is a thing they
can do for that,* Tōan had said, in the flat voice, with the lamp going.
*It is done in the capital and in three villages and it is not done in a
valley by a man with a copper pan.*

Sazare had thought about that sentence for two years as a fact about
Tōan.

He stood in a yard behind Konoha General and understood, at last, that it
had been a fact about *the world* — that the technique had existed the
whole time, in a building four hundred miles away, being used on Tuesdays,
by people who were not particularly remarkable and who went home
afterwards and had their dinner.

**And the ones he passed.**

This was the long list and he had never counted it and could not, and
standing in the rain he got to eleven and stopped, because eleven was
enough.

*The boat out of Nagihama, fourteen people in the fog, and the woman at the
stern with the baby that had been dosed quiet, and the old man who had a
cough that I would now put a name to inside four seconds and did not have
one then.*

*The loft above the net store at Shiranui, nine nights, and Emiko's
daughter.*

*Four families in an empty inn at Nami.*

*A boy of nineteen on a bridge with a gaff wound in the thigh who I packed
and told he was not going to die, and who was not going to die, and I did
not know that. I said it because he needed to hear it and I would have said
it either way.*

*Thirty-one people on a quay who had paid twice.*

*Every single town. Every single one, for two and a half years, and I
walked through all of them mending kettles.*

---

He came back in at about the third hour with his hair flat to his head and
found Nurse Yagi in the receiving room doing the floor, because at three in
the morning in a hospital everybody does the floor.

"You've been out there twenty minutes," she said.

"Yes."

"You going to be sick?"

"No."

"Right." She went on mopping. "First arterial?"

"No."

"Mm," said Nurse Yagi, and did not ask a second question, which he took at
the time for indifference and understood about a year later to have been
the exact opposite.

Sazare got the other mop.

They did the floor together for about four minutes, and then he said, in
a completely level voice, "Can I ask you a technical thing?"

"You always can."

"The vessel at the back of the spleen. If there's no medic. What's the
best a person with hands and a cloth can actually do?"

Nurse Yagi stopped mopping.

"Pack it and press high under the ribs and hold it," she said, "and get
somebody running, and it's about one in four, and if the packing's good
and the pressure's in the right place and they're young it's better than
that. Why?"

"Because I want to know the number."

"One in four."

"Thank you," said Sazare, and finished the floor.

He wrote it down when he got home, at the fourth hour, on the back page of
the audit book where the printed columns ran out — *spleen, posterior
vessel: pack, high pressure under the arch, hold. 1 in 4* — in a hand that
was not entirely steady; and underneath it, over the following four years,
he wrote about eleven hundred more.

That was the whole of his grief and the only shape he had for it, and he
knew it at the time, and it was completely inadequate, and it was the only
thing available.

---

He walked home along the north road at half past four in the morning with
the rain stopping and the sky going the colour it goes.

He was sixteen years and three months old. He had closed one wound in his
life and it had taken him four minutes and eleven seconds and had been on
his own arm.

He was, by any measure anybody in that building would have used, of no
medical use whatever, and would be for years, and Nurse Yagi had told him
so in the sluice with a scalpel in her hand and had been right about all
of it.

And he understood, walking up the north road in the dark, that he was going
to do it anyway, for as long as it took, and that the reason was not the
one he would have given anybody who asked.

The reason was not that he wanted to be useful, though he did.

It was not that he owed anybody, though he did, and he had a list.

It was that on the eleventh month of the previous year he had knelt on
cold stones in the dark and put his fist deliberately three inches from
the place he knew it should go, on a man who had been pleased with him
about a lantern; and there was no possible act, in any year of any life,
that could reach backward and put the fist where it belonged.

*Most dying is done waiting.*

He had been the thing that was waited for, twice, and had been the wrong
thing on purpose.

Sazare Ruri went up three flights of stairs to a room he could not
fortify, and lay down in his clothes on a mat with the ledger open on the
table and a single line on the back page, and worked out — flatly, with no
comfort in it anywhere, at half past four in the morning — that a man
cannot pay a debt to the dead, and that the only currency the world will
accept is somebody else entirely, and that this is the worst arrangement
anybody has ever been offered and there has never been another one.

Then he slept for three hours, and got up, and went in at the sixth.
