# Chapter 66, Scene 7 — We'll Wait

*Act 5, part A. The Tarugi bath house. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen.
POV: Sazare. ~2400 words.*

---

At the bath house door the day changed registers, because at the bath
house they were expected.

The hood came down, and the owner — a small upright woman of about
sixty with the forearms of a lifetime of wet linen — took one look at
the face the hood had been carrying across her town all morning, and
performed the whole sequence Sazare had watched four hundred people
perform in three years, recognition to disbelief to arithmetic, except
that she did it in a quarter of the time and landed somewhere he had
not seen anybody land: on *housekeeping*.

"The pool room's had its floor done since the letter," she said, as if
Hokage arrived on Wednesdays generally. "And the ledger's on the desk,
and there's a private room laid for after, and if either of you says
anything kind about the towels before you've used them I'll know it
for flattery. This way."

"I like her," said Tsunade, to the entrance hall at large.

The audit was real, so they did it really.

The four stones sat where the placement sheets said they sat, one at
each corner of the therapeutic pool, half-height in the wall, behind
slotted cedar screens the owner's son had built to a drawing Sazare
had posted fourteen months ago and never seen executed. Good screens.
Slightly proud fixings on the third. He said nothing about the
fixings.

The certificates hung framed by the door, four of them, each carrying
the programme's stamp, the placement date, and the certifying
signature: *Kurihara S.*, four times, in the hand he knew better than
any hand alive except two — small, disciplined, closing every loop it
opened.

He read the name four times and put it through and out, and if a
debt eleven seconds long and eight months old turned over in its
sleep somewhere under his sternum, that was between him and the
steam.

The stones themselves took eleven minutes, which he could have told
anybody they would.

Stone one, drawing and releasing to specification, rate steady, the
gradient exactly where he had laid it in the autumn. Stones two and
four the same, allowing for the second's position over the inflow,
which the protocol allowed for. Stone three was doing its work and
doing it a fraction lean, and the cause was not the stone: the room's
steam had found a path along the new floor's fall and pooled against
the east screen, and the stone sat above the pocket, drawing through
air that was already three parts water.

"Reposition," he said. "Two feet north, same height, same screen.
It's in the sheet's tolerance and the son can do it with the fixings
he's already got. The stone is right. The room moved."

Tsunade, who was conducting her audit from the warm end with her
sleeves rolled and an expression of profound institutional
satisfaction, signed the ledger where the ledger wanted signing.

*Field review, four placements, all performing. One repositioning
within tolerance, owner's labour, no cost to programme.*

And underneath, in the space the form left for the auditing officer's
remarks, in the hand that had signed his governor and his incident
report and the public notice on his paper, she wrote: *The village
should be sending stones to more towns like this one,* which was
true, and was policy, and made the owner stand up straighter for the
rest of the evening, and cost nothing, and he watched her do it and
thought: *that is what the office is for, and she has never once had
to be taught where to spend it.*

---

The sword took its usual toll of protocol at the changing room, where
the attendant — the owner's son, the builder of screens — looked at
the case and then at the rack meant for umbrellas and working
knives, and visibly encountered the entire absence of a shelf in his
world for *this*.

"It stays with me," said Sazare, "or it stays where I can see it. I
know how that sounds. There's a paper that makes it official, but the
paper's in Konoha, so tonight it's just a man being odd about a
sword."

"You made the stones?" said the son.

"I made the stones."

"Then you're allowed to be odd about a sword," he said, with the
whole reasonableness of a man who worked in cedar, and put the case
on two pegs he freed up at eye height, in clear view of the pool
room door, and that was the entire negotiation.

Tenzō was somewhere. There had been a fisherman on the sea wall of
the lake stage who had caught nothing all afternoon with total
contentment. There was presently a guest in the common room whose
tea had gone round twice. Sazare granted him the professional
courtesy of not existing, and got it back.

The water was the right kind of too hot. He sat in it up to the chin
in the men's side with the steam shutting the world down to four
feet of visibility, and did the accounting he had been putting off
since the gate, because some ledgers do not stay shut just because
the day is not for working.

*She has laughed nine times today. I have the count without meaning
to have it. Nine, and it is not yet six o'clock, and I do not have a
year's data but I have a strong prior, and the prior says nine is
not a Wednesday number. Nine is a number from before the office. She
left the office at the gate with the robes, and the person walking
round Tarugi all day pricing pears and ruining hat men is somebody
the village has not met.*

*And I am the only one who got the introduction.*

*File that where it goes,* he thought, chin-deep in a small warm
country, *which is nowhere. Some things you do not even keep. Some
things you just get to have been in the room for.*

---

The slab was in the corridor between the pool room and the private
dining room, and he had passed it twice already and taken it in the
way he took in everything, which is to say entirely, and kept
walking, which is to say the day was working.

Third time was after the bath, in the loose clean clothes the house
put you in, padding behind the owner's lamp toward dinner, warm to
the bone, guard as far down as it went. And the slab did what made
things do, at four feet, in lamplight.

It was the house charm. Grey local sandstone, about a foot square,
set into the wall at shoulder height when the house was built —
sixty-odd years, by the mortar — with the house's mark and a prayer
line cut into it in a mason's hand, and a crack clean through it,
corner to corner, through the mark and through the prayer. An old
crack. Somebody had painted over it once, and the paint had done what
paint does over a working crack, which is testify to it. The two
halves stood apart the thickness of a winter and were held in the
wall by habit.

His hand was out before there was any him in the decision at all.

He looked at his own hand, four inches off the stone, fingers already
spread to the load paths — read, priced, planned: a graded bridge
through the crack, keyed to the grain of both faces, four minutes,
invisible under the old paint, the mark whole again, done before
dinner — and he took the hand back, and put it at his side, and stood
there in the corridor with the whole repair sitting completed behind
his eyes the way the yard sat behind them at night.

*Not today. Today is not about a thing. It is the one rule the day
has, and I have kept it since the gate, and it costs four minutes and
I am not spending them. The house has lived with this crack longer
than I have been alive. It is not mine. Nothing here is mine to
mend.*

He had turned to go when her voice came from the doorway behind him,
where she had been standing for exactly as long as he had, holding
the lamp the owner had handed her, watching him read a wall.

"How long would it take you?"

"Four minutes."

"We'll wait," said Tsunade.

He looked at her. She stood in the corridor of a bath house in a town
of eleven hundred people, in house clothes, with her hair down and a
lamp in her hand, the fifth Hokage of the Land of Fire, and she moved
the lamp a few inches so the light lay flat along the slab, which was
the correct placement for raking light on a crack, because of course
she knew the correct placement, and she said the rest of it plainly,
the way the true things came out of her when she had decided to stop
armouring them.

"I didn't bring you here to stop being you for a day. That was never
the deal, and if it was working I'd be worried. I brought you where
nothing *needs* you. There's a difference, and the difference is the
whole holiday. Nothing here needs you. And you're standing in a
corridor wanting to mend an old woman's luck anyway, not because it's
a job, because it's *yours* — it's the thing your hands do the way my
idiot teammate's hands write. That one," she nodded at the slab, "you
don't put through and out. That one you keep. Four minutes. We'll
wait, and dinner will keep, and I am going to hold the lamp and watch,
because in three years I have never once got to just *watch*."

The owner, appealed to, gave the permission of a woman being offered
a free miracle with a floor she had just had done, which was
immediate, with conditions about dust.

There was no dust. There was no sound either, or nearly none — the
low workshop hum of an article being asked to exist, four minutes of
lamplight and a boy's spread hand on a broken charm, a bridge grown
corner to corner through the dark of the stone where nobody would
ever see it, graded fine at both faces so the old faces bore on it
without knowing they had been asked, and the crack closed under its
sixty years of paint like a seam being taken in.

He stepped back. The slab was one stone. The mark was whole. The
prayer line ran corner to corner unbroken, the way the mason had cut
it when the owner's mother was a girl.

"There," said Tsunade, quietly, to the owner, to the corridor, to
the winter, in the voice of a woman presenting the single most
ordinary thing in the world. "Now he's on holiday."

---

Dinner was in the private room, and was the owner's whole opinion of
the day expressed in food, and lasted two hours, and no part of it
was about a thing.

At the end of it he put the paper on the table.

Four lines, a date, her name. No reference number, because a
reference is a thing people can request against. It had lived in his
coat for nineteen days and it looked like it: carried, considered,
worn soft at one fold like the corner of a chapter somebody keeps
arriving at.

"I want it marked," he said. "Not destroyed — you said the terms in
front of a witness so it couldn't be un-counted, and I'm not going to
do to the paper what you built the paper to prevent. Marked. Because
it's spent, and I know what I spent it on, and I want the record to
show the price the way I show every price. This is the whole of what
it bought." He moved one hand, palm up, a small motion that took in
the room, the town, the lamp oil, the winter, the nine laughs, the
mended luck in the corridor. "It was the best purchase of my life.
Mark it paid."

She looked at the paper for a moment. Then she took the brush the
private room kept for the guest book, and wrote across the four lines
in the hand that closed hospitals and opened them:

*PAID IN FULL — received, one holiday. The seller confirms she was
overpaid.*

The date. Her name, under her own name, the second time smaller.

"Both names on it are mine now," she said, handing it back. "That's
how you kill a paper properly — you don't burn it, you *complete* it.
Keep the corpse. You keep everything else."

He kept the corpse.

---

The room they were given was the good room, the one over the garden,
and the futons had been laid out a courteous four inches apart by
somebody's blameless staff, and the four inches lasted as long as
they were ever going to.

He woke once in the deep of the night, out of no dream, for no
reason, the way he had used to wake in eleven lodgings to check a
door. There was no door to check. There was snow starting outside,
the first of it, going past the window without urgency. The sword
stood in its case in the corner, open, drawing its quiet sixteen-days'
wage. Somewhere below, a charm held a wall with a bridge in its heart
that nobody would ever see.

And her hands had found his shirt in her sleep, both of them, gripped
full, the way they had gripped it the first night and every night the
tower had allowed since — the strongest hands in the world, hands
that split ground and stopped hearts and signed his governor, holding
onto a fistful of cotton like the last handhold on a cliff nobody
else was permitted to know she was climbing.

He did not move. Moving was not in the protocol. He lay in the dark
of the small warm country and did the day's honesty in his head,
because the notebook was in his coat and the coat was across the
room and the rule had held since the gate and would hold till the
gate saw them again.

One entry, composed, dated, filed where the index could not be
stolen off his body.

*8/1. Nothing happened. It took all day.*
