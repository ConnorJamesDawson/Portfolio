# Chapter 19, Scene 2 — Two Numbers

*Act 3, part A. Nami. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,600 words.*

---

Haku came into the town on the Thursday with a basket, in the clear cold
weather that follows three days of drizzle, and Sazare knew he was there
about twenty minutes before he saw him, because the whole of Nami changed
tone.

That was not a figure of speech. He was sitting in the net-loft window
with a lock barrel in pieces on a cloth and the shutters back, and the
noise coming up off the plank walk altered: the flat grumbling
mid-morning register of a poor town went up half a step, and there was
laughing at the bread stall, and old Watari who mended nets and had not
been heard to say a complete sentence since the summer was audibly saying
several.

Then the boy came round the corner of the walk with a covered basket on
his arm and the sun on him.

He had put his hair up for the walk into town, twisted and pinned with two
plain wooden pins, which changed his face completely and made him look
about seventeen and unmistakably like somebody's daughter, and he was in
the same much-washed dark travelling kimono with the sleeves loose today
instead of tied, and he had bare feet in straw sandals, and there was a
smear of mud up one calf where he had come across the flats rather than
round.

He stopped at the bread stall. He stopped at the herbalist's. He stopped
at the well and helped a woman with a bucket without being asked and
without making anything of it, and the woman — Sazare watched this from
sixty feet — the woman said something to him and *touched his arm*, which
in that town, in that month, with strangers, was extraordinary.

He came along the plank walk to the net loft and stood under the window
and looked up, shading his eyes with a flat hand.

"I've broken a lid," said Haku.

---

It was a pot lid, and he had not broken it; it had broken about nine years
before he was ever near it, and it had been mended twice since by
somebody with more determination than skill.

"That's not broken," said Sazare, turning it over. "That's been mended
badly twice and it's been waiting for you to be the one holding it."

"That is very unfair."

"It's the truth, and it's what happens to lids."

The handle was a cast bronze mushroom on a threaded stud, and the stud had
snapped flush with the lid, which is the one way that fitting can go and
which every one of them does eventually. Somebody had drilled it out once
and put a wooden peg in, and the peg had charred; somebody else had wired
it, and the wire had cut through.

Sazare mended it in eleven minutes on the window bench with Haku sitting
on the boards outside with his back to the loft wall and his face in the
sun.

He drilled the old stud out properly, made a new one out of the shank of a
bent nail, threaded it by hand with the smallest die in the roll, and set
it with a shoulder washer cut from a strip of copper so the handle would
have something to sit down on instead of grinding on the lid itself.

"It'll get hot now," he said. "The bronze had a wooden peg in it, which
was somebody being clever — wood doesn't conduct. This will. Use a cloth."

"I always use a cloth."

"Then you'll be fine."

Haku turned the lid over in both hands, and looked at the underside of it,
where nobody looks, where the shoulder washer sat.

"You've made this better than it was new," he said.

"It was never any good new. That's a lid made for selling, not for using."

"How can you tell?"

And Sazare told him, because he was asked. He showed him the casting
line where the mould had been out of register by about a sixteenth and
nobody had dressed it. He showed him the wall thickness, thin on one side
and thick on the other, so the thing would always want to sit crooked. He
showed him the little chased ring round the boss that was there to be
looked at in a shop and did nothing whatever, and that had cost the maker
about four minutes and the buyer about a tenth of the price.

Haku listened to the entire thing with his head slightly on one side and
his eyes on the object and did not interrupt once, and at the end of it he
said, with what was unmistakably real interest:

"Who taught you to see all that?"

"My mother."

"Mine taught me to find things in a hedge," said Haku, "which has turned
out to be about as useful."

---

They walked up the hill together afterwards, because Haku had the
herbalist's list to finish and Sazare had a lock to deliver at the top of
the town and there was no reason not to, and it was the sort of morning
that makes a person say yes to a walk.

The rain had gone through and left everything rinsed. The mud on the flats
had a skin of light on it. Up the hill behind the houses the trees had
started turning — not the hard reds of a cold country, but a slow
yellowing in the broadleaves among the black-green of the pine, so the
whole slope looked like a piece of cloth with the weave going. Woodsmoke.
Wet stone. Somewhere a dog.

The herbalist's was a shop the size of a cupboard with a bead curtain and
a hundred and forty small drawers, and it smelled — Sazare stood in the
doorway and shut his eyes for a second without meaning to — it smelled
exactly like a treatment room up a valley in the Land of Hot Water at
about the fourth hour of a winter evening.

He came out of it with something on his face he had not arranged, and Haku
saw it, and said nothing, and took the parcel of yarrow off the counter
and put it in the basket.

"You know what this is for," Haku said, on the step.

"Yes."

"You knew when you came round the shore with the cart."

"Yes."

"That's why you came round with the cart." Haku settled the basket on his
arm. He was not accusing. He was checking a sum. "You'd already worked out
what was in that house and you wanted to be somebody who could be let in.
Somebody harmless. So you found the most harmless possible reason, and you
made it *true*, which is the part I keep thinking about — you didn't
pretend to bring the planks, you actually brought the planks and actually
did the work, for nothing, for two days."

"That's right."

"Why is that better?"

"Because a pretend reason gets checked," said Sazare, "and a true one
doesn't."

Haku laughed at that, out loud, and put his hand up in front of his mouth
the way he did.

"Zabuza-san said something like that about you," he said. "He said you're
a type. He says it about everybody; he's usually right." He came down off
the step. "He was wrong about you, though, and I'd rather he wasn't, and
I've been trying to work out how to say why since Tuesday."

"Go on, then."

"A type doesn't plane the edges of a board," said Haku, "so a bare foot in
the dark won't find the step."

---

They went along the top lane where the houses stopped and the hill began,
and below them the whole bay lay out flat and white-bright with the
unfinished bridge across the mouth of it, and neither of them looked at
the bridge.

The wind came round off the water and there was a smell in it and a cold
edge under the sun, and Haku stopped and put his head up and breathed it
and said, without thinking about it, the word for the fog that comes off
the shallows at the turn of the tide.

Not a general word. Not *mist* or *haar* or any of the things a foreigner
would say. The specific one — the one that exists in about four hundred
miles of coastline and nowhere else on earth, because nowhere else on
earth needs a separate word for the fog that comes at slack water as
against the fog that comes at the ebb.

Sazare heard it and answered before he had made any decision at all,
because it is not a word you answer, it is a word you *agree* with; and
what came out of him was the same word with the lower-district vowel in
it, the flat one, the one they said at the fish market end of Kiri where
the drying frames were.

Both of them stopped walking.

They stood on a lane above a foreign bay in the sun for a while, not
looking at each other.

"Seven," said Haku.

"Thirteen," said Sazare.

And that was the whole of it. Neither of them said the name of the
country, or the word for what had happened in it, or one single syllable
about who or where or how; and they did not have to, and both of them knew
they did not have to, and there is a kind of conversation that consists
entirely of two numbers and is more complete than anything either party
will ever manage again.

---

"Thirteen is worse," said Haku, after a while.

"I don't think that's a thing you can—"

"It is, though. It's the only part of it I've ever been sure about." He
had gone quiet, but not sad; he said it the way he had said *I always use
a cloth.* "At seven nobody explains it to you. It happens in your house
and then you are somewhere else and the whole of it is just — weather.
Something that came down. I found out what I was because of what was done
about it, and nobody ever once sat me down and told me why, and I have
never been able to decide whether that was mercy or whether they simply
didn't think of it."

He looked out at the water.

"At thirteen they explain it," he said. "Don't they."

Sazare stood in the lane with the sun on the side of his face and thought
about a district clerk with a warm hand and a wife he was pleased to tell
things to. About four names in block capitals on a schedule and a column
for *bloodline* and a column for *use*, and a boy of ten who had filled
his own line in correctly and been praised for the neatness of it. About a
man on a platform with a paper, reading out the reason, at volume, to four
hundred people, in the fog, so that everybody would know what the reason
was — because that was the whole point of the exercise, that was the
*service* being provided, that was why there was a platform at all.

"Yes," he said.

"There," said Haku, mildly. "Worse."

And then — and this was the thing Sazare took away from that hill and
turned over for the rest of his life — Haku said:

"I've thought about it a great deal and I would rather not have been told.
I know what that sounds like. But a person who has been told has to decide
what to do about it, and that's the whole of the rest of their life, and I
have watched what it does." He picked a seed head off the grass at the
side of the lane and turned it in his fingers. "Nobody ever gave me
anything to decide. I got a thing that happened, and then I got somebody
who wanted me, and I have been extremely happy, and I do not think you
have been happy for one day since you were thirteen years old."

Sazare said nothing at all.

"That wasn't unkind," said Haku, checking. "I didn't mean it unkindly."

"No," said Sazare. "It wasn't unkind."

---

They came back down the hill together and split at the top of the plank
walk, and the whole of the last two hundred yards was completely ordinary
and neither of them tried to make it anything else: the price of oil, the
herbalist's cat, whether the yellow in the broadleaves meant an early
winter or a wet one.

At the corner Haku shifted the basket and said, "Thank you for the lid."

"It's a lid."

"It's a good lid now." He was smiling again; the smile had come back at
some point on the hill and Sazare had not seen it arrive. "Will you go and
eat at Tazuna's again before you leave?"

The plank walk went on being a plank walk. The gulls went on doing what
gulls do. Sazare stood in a patch of sun on wet boards with his roll under
his arm.

"I might," he said.

"You should. Tsunami-san cooks about twice what four people can eat and
she's had four extra in the house since Friday and she's still doing it,
and you're the sort of person who'd notice that and go, and she'd like it."
He settled the basket. "And you'd be able to see them properly, which
would be useful for you, wouldn't it, rather than in a doorway on the
walk in the rain."

He said it kindly. He said everything kindly; it was the medium he came
in.

"Yes," said Sazare. "It would."

"Good," said Haku, and went away up the walk with his basket, stopping
once to say something to the woman at the well that made her laugh, and
once to pick up a child's dropped shoe and put it back on the step where
the child could find it.

Sazare stood where he was until the boy was out of sight.

Then he went back to the net loft and climbed the stairs and sat down on
the mat by the window with the lock barrel still in pieces on the cloth
where he had left it, and did the arithmetic, because he could not not.

*He knows I've eaten at that house. He knows which night. He knows how
many people are in it and how long they've been in it and what the woman
cooks and how much of it, and none of that is in a town's gossip, because
Tsunami's cooking is not gossip, it's a thing you would only know if
somebody had been sitting somewhere watching that house at supper time.*

*And he told me. On purpose. Warmly. As a piece of advice about a kind
woman.*

*He is fifteen and he is the politest person I have ever met and he has
been sitting in a tree above that household counting the bowls.*

The tide came up under the loft and the light moved round the room and
Sazare sat with his back against the wall and thought, for the first
time in six weeks, in the flat voice:

*And I like him. That's the problem. That's the whole of the problem, and
he hasn't done a single thing to make it happen, and I don't think he
could stop it if he tried.*
