# Chapter 5, Scene 2 — Even If It's Raining

*Act 1, beat 12. Sazare Ruri, age eleven. POV: Sazare. ~2,500 words.*

---

Kohaku changed over about seven months and Sazare would have said, if anyone had
asked him, that she had not changed at all.

She was home less. That was the only thing he could have pointed to, and he could
account for it: she had been made chūnin in the spring, which meant longer
postings, which meant she got the coast run and the two-week jobs instead of the
four-day ones, and everybody said that was good.

She slept when she was home. She had always eaten when she was home; now she ate
and then slept, sometimes for a day and a half, and their mother put a screen up
across the end of the room and nobody was to go past it.

She stopped doing the story.

That one Sazare felt without ever locating. There had always been a story — the
dog, the ferryman, the man at Kuromizu with the cart of chickens — and it had come
out over the second bowl, and everybody had stopped eating to listen. Now Ren
would ask his three questions and she would answer the first one, properly and at
some length, and then eat.

She was still funny. She was still exactly as funny. It was only that she was funny
about things that had happened in this house rather than things that had happened
on a road.

---

She snapped at him once, in the eleventh month.

He had gone past the screen. He had gone past it to put a cup of something down
where she would find it, which he had done nine or ten times before, and she had
come up off the mat so fast he did not see it happen and had him by the front of
the shirt with her other hand back, and the cup had gone across the boards.

They looked at each other.

"Sorry," she said. She let go. "Zare. Sorry."

"I was putting—"

"I know what you were doing." She sat down on the mat and pushed the heels of her
hands into her eyes. "Don't come past the screen. All right? Not for anything.
Don't come past the screen."

"All right."

"It's not you."

"I know."

"It genuinely is not you," said Kohaku, "and I'm going to go to sleep now and when
I wake up I'm going to have forgotten that happened, and so are you, and if you
tell mum I'll put you in the cutting."

He did not tell mum.

His mother knew by the following evening, because she wiped up what was left of the
cup, and looked at the mark it had left on the boards, and looked at Kohaku, and
said nothing at all — and Kohaku said "I know" to a sentence nobody had spoken,
and that was the end of it.

---

The errands got bigger.

They had always been the same errand. A package the size of a pack of cards, wrapped
in waxed paper, tied with string; a name; a street; *don't go in, she'll try and
give you something with anise in it*. He had done perhaps forty of them over four
years and he could have walked most of the routes with his eyes shut.

In the twelfth month she gave him one that was heavier.

He noticed the weight and thought nothing of it, and had got three streets before
he realised the second thing, which was that this one did not rattle *at all*.
The others had never rattled either, but they had had a give in them, a sense of
something loose inside the paper. This one was solid all the way through, and it
had a heft to it that he recognised from about a thousand mornings of picking
things up off his mother's trestles.

It was crystal. Not offcuts. A piece.

He stood on the corner of the rope-makers' street with it in both hands and
thought about that for a while.

Then he took it where he had been told to take it, because it was Kohaku, and
because if you thought about the reasons for things you would never do anything.

---

She sent him out in the rain in the first month, and he thought about that
afterwards for years, because it was the only one she checked twice.

"Kuromizu ford. The end house on the north side, the one with the boat in the
yard. Man called Bekku. He'll be expecting it."

"Kuromizu's *miles*."

"It's four miles."

"It's *raining*."

"You said even if it's raining," said Kohaku.

He had said that. He had said it four years ago, sitting on a wall, appalled that
she could think otherwise.

"I'll go," he said.

"Say the name back."

"Bekku."

"House."

"End of the north side. The one with the boat in the yard."

"And if he's not there?"

"Then I bring it back and I don't leave it with anybody."

"And if anybody asks you what you're doing four miles up the coast road in the
rain?"

Sazare stopped.

She had never asked him that before. In four years, forty errands, she had never
once asked him what he would say to anybody, because there had never been anybody
to say anything to.

"...I'd say I was going to Kuromizu?"

"No." Kohaku crouched down so she was at his height, which she had stopped doing
about two years ago because he had got too tall for it, and which she now did
badly, with one knee on the wet ground. "You say you're going to your aunt's. Your
aunt Sen, at Nagihama, and you've gone the coast way because the low road's under
water, which it is, because it always is. And that's true. She *is* your aunt.
She's not your aunt but she's called your aunt and she has been since you were
four, and she'll say so if anybody ever asks her, and nobody ever will."

"Why would anybody ask me anything?"

"They wouldn't."

"Then why do I have to—"

"Because I'd rather you had it and never used it," said Kohaku, "than the other
way round. Say it back."

He said it back.

"Good," she said, and stood up, and her knee had gone through at the seam. "Go on.
And Zare — if the ford's up, you turn round. Don't be clever. I mean it. If the
water's over the stones you turn round and come home and I'll take it myself
tomorrow."

"All right."

"*Turn round.*"

"*All right*," said Sazare, aggrieved, and went.

---

The ford was not up. It was high, and cold, and he took his boots off and went
across with them round his neck and his feet went numb somewhere in the middle,
and on the far side he sat on a stone and rubbed them until they hurt and felt
extremely capable about the whole business.

Bekku had the end house on the north side with a boat in the yard. He was about
sixty and enormous and had no interest in Sazare whatsoever; he took the package
and said "right," and shut the door.

Sazare walked four miles back in the rain and got in after dark and his mother did
not ask where he had been, which he registered at the time as a mercy.

Kohaku was on the step with her boots off.

"Well?"

"He said 'right' and shut the door."

"That's Bekku."

"He didn't even say thank you."

"He wouldn't say thank you if you saved his life," said Kohaku. "He'd say
'right'." She moved over two inches on the step. "Sit down. You're wet."

"I'm all right."

"Sit down, Zare."

He sat down.

They sat there for a while with the rain coming off the eaves in a sheet and the
frames going in the next room, and Sazare, who was eleven and had walked eight
miles and forded a river and was very pleased with himself, said:

"I could do more of them."

Kohaku did not answer straight away.

"Could you," she said.

"I'm faster than I was. I did the ford in — I didn't even *stop*, I just took my
boots off and went. And I know all the roads now, I know the coast one and the
low one and the way round the back of the tannery, and I could do two in a day if
you—"

"Zare."

"—if you wanted, because I don't do anything else, and Jirō's doing extra running
in the evenings because of his stream and I just sit there with a bowl—"

"*Zare.*"

He stopped.

His sister was looking out at the yard. She had her elbows on her knees and her
hands hanging, and there was a great deal of grey in the light and it made her look
like their mother, which he had never noticed before and would notice constantly
afterwards.

"Yeah," she said. "All right."

"Really?"

"Two a week. Not two a day, you'd be dead on your feet and mum would skin me."
She rubbed her face. "And you go the way I tell you, and you turn round when I say
turn round, and if I ever find out you've been clever about any of it we stop.
Straight away. That's the deal."

"That's the deal," said Sazare, delighted.

"Say it."

"Two a week, your route, turn round when you say."

"Good."

He sat on the step with his feet aching and his shirt sticking to him and a
feeling in his chest he did not have a word for and would not have wanted one,
which was that he had asked his sister for something for the first time in his
life and she had said yes.

Beside him Kohaku turned the earring round in her ear with her thumb, twice, and
looked out at the rain, and did not say anything else at all.
