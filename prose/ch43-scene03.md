# Chapter 43, Scene 3 — Kurihara

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,738 words.*

---

She was already working when he got there and she did not stop, which
he approved of before he had any other opinion about her at all.

Kanzaki's yard at the back of the requisition office had a bench under
the awning and two under the open sky, and on the good days in the
fourth month everybody worked outside and complained about the light,
and the whole of it smelled of the tar they had put on the roof in the
spring and of the sour dust that comes off dry stone under a file.
Somewhere behind the wall a pigeon was doing the same four notes it had
been doing since he had first come into that yard three years ago,
possibly the same pigeon, probably not.

She had nineteen of his stones laid out on a felt in four rows and a
short fifth, and a brush roll open at her right hip, and she was
working the second row with a lens on a stand and a taper going even
though it was two in the afternoon and the light was fine.

"Don't stand in it," she said.

He moved.

"Thank you." She did not look up. "Two minutes."

It was rather more than two minutes. He used them.

Leather apron, and the wear was wrong for a smith — nothing at the
chest, nothing at the belly, but the forearms polished dark and the
right hip gone soft and shiny in a rectangle the exact size of the
brush roll that lived there. Somebody who leaned on a bench with her
elbows and never leaned on anything with her body. Ink to the second
knuckle on the right hand, not a stain but a colour, the sort that goes
in over years and stops coming out. Nails cut square and short. A burn
across the heel of the left palm, old, healed pale, with a shape to it
— a straight edge and then a curve, which was not a splash and not a
tool and was the print of something that had gone off while she was
holding it flat.

Twenty, or near it. A squarish face with a permanent small frown in it
from close work, and the narrowed look of somebody who has been reading
things at eleven inches since she was a child and has not yet been told
to do anything about it.

Her hair was up on a lacquered pin.

He priced the pin in about two seconds because he could not help it and
then wished he had not, because the answer was that it was very good,
that it was at least thirty years older than she was, and that nobody
buys themselves a thing like that at twenty.

---

"Your seventh one's wrong," she said.

"Which seventh."

"All of them. Every seventh application, going back — I've done sixty
and it's every seventh, so it's not you drifting, it's you doing
something on a cycle." She put the brush down and turned the felt so
the row faced him. "Look at the third stroke of the retaining figure."

He looked at it for a while.

"It's closed."

"It's closed. On the other six it's open by about the width of the
brush." She sat back. "Do you know why it's open?"

"Because it's drawn fast."

"Because it's drawn *last*," she said. "The retaining figure is the
last thing you put on and by then the ground's taken all it's going to
take, so the stroke sits up rather than sinking, and the gap is the
tell that the ground is full. If it's closed, the ground wasn't full,
which means you charged it and then applied, instead of applying and
then charging."

"Every seventh."

"Every seventh. Which means once a week." She looked at him for the
first time. "You do a batch of six on the bench and the seventh
somewhere else, at a different time, in a different order, and I'd
guess at night."

"Thursday," said Sazare.

"Thursday," she agreed, and wrote it down. "It isn't dangerous. It
holds. It'll hold for years. It's just worse, by about the amount that
one stroke is worth, and over three hundred stones that's a number
somebody should have given you fourteen months ago."

"Nobody's been able to."

"No," she said. "There are four people in this village who can read a
retaining figure and one of them died in the second month, so that
tracks."

She said it entirely evenly, in the middle of writing, and went on
writing.

---

He worked the rest of it out over the following eleven minutes and did
not enjoy any part of the process.

They talked technique, because there was nothing else to talk about and
because it was, without any question, the best technical conversation
he had had in a year that had contained a four-hour night in the
Hokage's office. She was better than him. Not in the way Kanzaki was
better, which was thirty years of a different trade, and not in the way
Tsunade was better, which was a mind operating at an altitude he could
see and not reach. She was better *at the same thing*, four feet away,
in a way he could follow every step of and could not have got to, and
she gave him three corrections in eleven minutes and did not soften one
of them and did not seem to think there was anything to soften.

It was the most straightforwardly pleasant hour of his month.

Then he said, because it was relevant and because he had stopped being
careful about twenty minutes earlier:

"He changed the order in the eleventh month."

Her brush stopped.

"He had me lay the retainer first for the last four I did with him," he
said. "Retainer, then ground, then charge. He said the reason out loud
and I wrote it down and it's in a notebook in my room, and I can bring
it to you, because he was quite specific and I'd rather you had it
exactly than had my summary of it."

Kurihara did not move for about three seconds.

"He never laid a retainer first in his life," she said.

"He did in the eleventh month."

"He'd have argued about it for an hour. He'd have argued with *me*
about it for an hour, and then he'd have—" She stopped. She put the
brush down on the rest very precisely, the way people do when the
alternative is putting it down badly. "Right. Yes. Bring me the
notebook."

She went back to the row.

Her hands were fine. That was the thing he noticed and could not
account for at the time: her hands were completely steady and she
worked the next four stones without a fault, and it was everything
above the wrists that had gone somewhere else.

---

Kanzaki came out at four with the day book and did the introduction
eleven minutes after it had stopped being useful, which was how Kanzaki
did everything.

"You've met. Kurihara Saeko, she's certifying the batch and she'll be
in and out of this yard until she's finished, which will take longer
than she says. Kurihara, this is—"

"I know who he is."

"Then that's the whole of it." Kanzaki opened the day book. "Sazare,
she studied under Urushibara six years, which is five and a half more
than you managed, so when she tells you a thing is wrong the correct
response is *thank you* and not the noise you make."

"Six years," said Sazare.

"Six," said Kurihara, to the felt.

---

He did the arithmetic on the way out of the yard because he could not
stop himself doing arithmetic, and it came out badly, and he stood in
the lane outside for a moment with the pigeon still going behind the
wall.

Six years. Ending, by everything in the way she talked about the
retaining figure, about two years ago — because everything she knew was
two years old and she had not known about the eleventh month, and a
student who is still a student finds out about the eleventh month in
the eleventh month.

And there had been four people at the funeral.

He had counted them at the time, standing on wet gravel, because
counting was what he did instead of the other thing. A clerk from the
sealing office who had not known him. An intelligence specialist. A
woman of sixty-one who had come four days from the Land of Rivers on
the strength of one letter after eleven years of silence.

And him.

*She was not there.*

*She is twenty minutes' walk from that ground and she has been in this
village the whole time, because she was in this village when the
Division needed a seal read in the second month, and she was not there.*

*And I have just told her that he changed the order in the eleventh
month, in a yard, at two in the afternoon, in the middle of a technical
conversation, because it was relevant.*

He stood in the lane a while longer.

He had four things he could do with that and he could see all four of
them, and he did what he had learned to do in the last year, which was
to write it down and not say it.

*Do not ask her. Not this month.*

*She has been in that yard four days and she has corrected me eleven
times and not one of those corrections was unkind, and she has not once
mentioned him except as an item in a list of four people who can read a
retaining figure, and she said the word "died" in the middle of a
sentence about my work, which is where a person puts a thing they have
decided to be able to say.*

*I have exactly one item she wants and it is not the notebook.*

*The notebook is what she asked for.*

*What she wants is an account of the last winter of a man she stopped
speaking to, and she cannot ask for it, and I am the only person alive
who has it, and I know precisely what that is because I have been on
the other side of it my entire life.*

*Bring the notebook. Bring only the notebook.*

*She will ask when she can ask.*
