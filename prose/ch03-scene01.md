# Chapter 3, Scene 1 — Carriage

*Act 1. Kohaku Ruri, fifteen. POV: Kohaku. ~2,300 words.*

---

The rule was that you never carried anything you could not account for, and
Kohaku had spent four years getting extremely good at accounting for things.

The bag on her hip had in it: two ration bars, a spare wrap, four thousand one hundred ryō in
mixed coin, a whetstone, a letter from her mother to a cousin in Nagihama that
was two years old and had never been sent, and a twist of paper containing
eleven small crystal offcuts of no value whatsoever.

If a checkpoint went through it — and one had, at the Shirakawa cutting, in the
sixth month, by a chūnin with a cold who had wanted very badly to be somewhere
else — they would find a girl going to her cousin's with a bag of rubbish her
brother had given her.

Which was true. That was the good part. It was all true.

---

She came into Nagihama on the low road at about the ninth hour with the rain
coming sideways off the water, and stopped at the third house on the sea side and
knocked twice and once.

The woman who opened it was called Sen and was ninety and had opinions.

"You're wet."

"It's raining."

"You'd know," said Sen, and let her in.

The house smelled of anise, and fish oil, and the particular cold-damp of a place
where nobody had lit anything for a week. Sen went about putting a pot on with the
appalling slowness of the very old, and Kohaku sat down on the floor because there
was nowhere else, and got the twist of paper out and shook the eleven chips onto
the boards.

Sen came over and lowered herself down opposite in stages.

Then she picked up the chips one at a time and held each of them very close to
her eye, and put nine of them in a heap on her left.

The other two she put in front of her.

"Same hand," she said.

"That's what I make it."

"That's not what you *make* it, girl, that's what it *is*." Sen turned one of them
over. "That's Ishibe. Look at the tail on the third stroke. He's been doing that
since before your mother was born and he'll die doing it." She set them down.
"Ishibe's alive, then."

"Ishibe was alive nine days ago."

"Nine days ago," said Sen, "is a long time." She swept the nine worthless chips
back into the twist of paper and gave it to Kohaku, and Kohaku put it in her bag,
because she would need it going back the other way.

"What do they say?"

"They say he's alive," said Sen.

"Yes, but what do they—"

"Kohaku."

"I know. I know." She rubbed her eyes. "I ask every time."

"You do."

"I don't want to *read* them. I want to know what they're *for*."

"They're for exactly what you've just done with them," said Sen, "which is to
carry a fact eleven leagues in a bag of rubbish, and if you knew any more than
that you'd be worth taking, and you're not, and that's the whole reason you're
still walking about." She poured the tea, badly, spilling some. "Drink that.
You're grey."

---

There were four people in the country who knew Kohaku's name and what she did.
She had met two of them. She had never seen the face of the man she wrote to and
she was not sure he was a man.

This was, she had been told at the beginning, the entire architecture: not
secrecy, which was impossible, but *thinness*. Nobody knew enough to be worth
breaking. Sen knew hands. A man in Kirigakure knew routes. Somebody, somewhere,
knew what the marks meant, and Kohaku had decided years ago that she did not want
to meet them.

She had been recruited at ten, if that was even the word, and she was fairly sure
it wasn't.

What had actually happened was that a jōnin she had liked had asked her, on the
road back from a D-rank that had gone long, to drop a package at a house on her
way home. And she had. And two months later he had asked again, and this time
there had been a coin in it for her, which she had spent on food. And a year after
that, when he asked, she had said *what is it, though*, and he had told her the
truth in about eleven words, and she had thought about it for the length of one
breath and said *all right*.

She had been eleven. She had understood roughly a third of it.

They had not lied to her once. That was the thing she came back to, on the road,
when she had time to think and wished she didn't. Nobody had ever lied to her.
They had simply asked her for one thing that was easy, and then one thing that was
slightly less easy, and by the time anything was hard she had been doing it for
three years and had stopped being able to see where the line had been.

---

She stayed the night at Sen's because the low road after dark was for people with
nothing to lose, and lay on the boards under her wrap listening to the sea, and
thought about the register.

The extra line had gone on in the ninth month. It was the fourth item in the
household section, after *Name*, *Trade*, and *Persons resident*, and it read:

*Inherited technique present in household. Y / N. If Y, specify and give names of
all persons in whom manifest.*

That was it. That was the whole of it. Twenty-one words on a form that every
household in every district filled in once a year and had done since before the
Second War.

She had seen four hundred of them by now, in a room she was not supposed to have
been in, and about a third had *N* written in a hand that had not hesitated, and
about half had *N* written in a hand that had, and the rest had *Y*, and a list of
names, in the careful block capitals of people who had been told to be accurate.

Aoi Momoi had left it blank. He had left it blank and drawn a line through it and
written *not applicable* underneath, which was, Kohaku thought, about the most
Momoi thing a man had ever done, and he had then gone to a bar and explained his
reasoning at length to four people, one of whom had been in the room she was not
supposed to have been in.

Nobody had come for the Momoi because Aoi had a bloodline. The Momoi did not have
a bloodline. They did the barracks laundry.

They had come because he had noticed the line.

---

She got home two days later at lamp-time, and Sazare came across the yard at her
like something fired out of a tube, and she got her arm out in time.

"You're *wet*."

"It's raining, Zare."

"It's not raining *here*."

"Well, it's raining *there*," she said, and steered him inside by the head.

Her mother came out of the workshop and did the look — top to bottom, one pass —
and said, "Nagihama?"

"Nagihama."

"Any good?"

"Sen says the roof's holding."

"Sen's said the roof's holding for eleven years," said her mother. "One of these
days that roof's going to fall on her and she'll say it was holding right up
until it wasn't." She went back to the frames. That was the whole conversation.
It had contained, as usual, one piece of information that had been requested and
received, and both of them knew which one it was, and neither of them had ever
said a word about it.

Kohaku sometimes wondered how much her mother knew. She had decided, at about
thirteen, that the honest answer was *exactly as much as she has decided to know*,
and had left it there, because the alternative was a conversation and there was no
version of that conversation that ended well.

---

Iwao said she was thin. Her father asked her three questions about the road
without waiting for any of the answers. Sazare sat opposite with his chin nearly
in his bowl and watched her eat with the frank admiration of a nine-year-old for
a person who is allowed to do things, and she told them about the ferryman at
Shirakawa who had lost a boot, and did the ferryman, and did the boot, and got
Sazare laughing so hard he had to put his cup down.

There had been no ferryman.

There had been a checkpoint at Shirakawa that had not been there in the sixth
month, with four men on it instead of one, and a table for emptying bags onto,
and she had walked through it with eleven bits of rubbish and a two-year-old
letter and her heart going like a bird in a box, and it had taken ninety seconds,
and she had been three hundred yards up the road before she could feel her hands
again.

The ferryman was from a road four years ago and had actually happened to somebody
else.

She was extremely good at this. She was aware that being extremely good at it was
not a thing she had ever chosen to become.

---

Afterwards, on the step, with her boots off:

"What's an intake register?"

She had been ready for it since the table. He had a face like a window.

"It's a list," she said.

"Of what?"

She told him. She kept it to the true parts, which was easy, because almost all of
it was true — a form, a drawer, names and ages and trades, uncle's had ours for
years. She watched him take it in and go looking for the hole in it, which he
would find, because he always did.

"Then why wouldn't he put his name on it?"

"Because it changed."

"Changed how?"

And there it was.

Kohaku looked at her brother.

He was nine. He had a scab on one knee and grey sand permanently under two
fingernails and he had, that spring, made a piece of crystal that was hollow at
one end and solid at the other and had not understood why she had made a fuss
about it. He carried a cloth roll of Genzō's offcuts everywhere he went. He had
found four families out of five, first go, off twelve scraps, when he was seven.

She could tell him. He would understand about a third of it. He would say *all
right*.

That was the whole problem. He would say *all right*, and he would mean it, and he
would be eleven when it got hard, and he would not be able to find the line
afterwards either.

*Two more years*, she thought. *Three. Let him do the errands and think they're
errands. He's not carrying anything that isn't rubbish and nobody stops a child
in the lower district, and if it ever gets past that I'll stop.*

She had thought *if it ever gets past that I'll stop* four times now about four
different things.

"They put an extra line on it," she said. "That's all. One line at the bottom. And
Aoi Momoi didn't fancy it and said so in a bar, and there's your reason."

"What was the line?"

"Doesn't matter."

"But what *was* it—"

"Zare," she said, "it genuinely doesn't matter, because we're all going to fill it
in when they ask, and it'll be fine, and if you get any more interesting than that
I'll have to start lying to you and I'd rather not."

Which was true.

She knocked her shoulder into his and sent him for the chips, and they did the
game until it was too dark to see, and he got six families out of twenty-one,
which was two better than last time.

"Yeah," she said.

The earring was in her ear. It had gone dull at the hollow end where she kept
turning it with her thumb, which she had started doing on roads, and had not
noticed she was doing until Sen had asked her what was wrong with her hand.
