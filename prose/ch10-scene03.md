# Chapter 10, Scene 3 — Go Home

*Act 2, part B. Sazare Ruri, ages thirteen–fourteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,400 words.*

---

The work was exactly as sold: it hurt, and it was dull, and it was dull and it
hurt, for month after month, and it was the best thing that had happened to him
since the ninth frame took.

Not because of the hurt. Because it was *work* — because it had the shape of
work, which he had been missing without knowing what he was missing: a thing
you did every day, that was measured, that went forward by amounts too small
to see and too real to argue with. Tōan ran it like a trade. There were
sessions on the bench, twice a day, where the thread of light went in along
the old seams and the scar tissue was *argued with* — that was the only
description Sazare could ever manage; it felt like a cramp being talked out of
its position — and those hurt in a way that left him grey and sweated, and
they were the small half of it.

The large half was the count.

The ball, still — but now with rules, and rows in a book. Grip and release,
sixty of, morning. The five stones of graded sizes to be picked off the bench
edge and set down again, fifty of, noon, the little one last because the last
two fingers were liars. The bucket carried at arm's length along the porch
and back. The knots: reef, clove, surgeon's, done and undone until his
fingertips read the cord's lay the way they had once read a chip's marks.
Wrist circles against the band of cart-spring Tōan had had the smith make,
which Sazare privately considered an instrument of interrogation.

Numbers went in the book. Tōan wrote them without comment. When a number
stalled for a fortnight the exercise changed; when it stalled for a month
they went to the springs and started lower.

The springs were the part he never got used to, which is to say the part he
came to love with an intensity he told no one about. There was a pool up
behind the house, walled off from the bathhouse trade, that ran at the heat
of blood and a little over, and the arm went into it to the shoulder for an
hour at dusk while the minerals did whatever Tōan said they did — "less than
the bathhouse claims, more than nothing" — and the heat went into the mended
places like forgiveness.

A country that leaked hot water. He floated his ruined arm in the ground's
own warmth under the standing steam, in the blue evening, and thought — he
could not help thinking — of a frame at blood-heat in a cold north room, and
of being told the sand was warm, and of every good thing his life had ever
held being, in the end, some version of this: warmth, coming up from
underneath, asking nothing.

By midwinter he could carry the full bucket. By the second month of spring
the little finger stopped lying. The shoulder, untaught and retaught through
a course of exercises of stupefying tedium, sat back into its socket's old
honesty, and one morning on the woodpile he brought the axe down two-handed
without having decided to, and stood looking at the split billet for a long
moment, and then very carefully did it again.

---

The timber camp sent down for Tōan at the end of that spring — a man under a
rolled log, two others hurt getting him out — and Tōan looked at the runner,
and then at the size of the job, and then at Sazare.

"You're carrying and boiling," he said. "And if I say hold something, it gets
held."

They went up at the pace Tōan called a medic's run, which you could sustain
for an hour and arrive still useful, and the camp was a raw scar of mud and
stacked trunks up under the first saddle, full of men standing wrong — Sazare
knew the stance from the quartermaster's yard, men arranging themselves around
a bad thing — and the bad thing was in the sawyers' shelter on a board table,
and it was bad.

Sazare boiled water. He laid out the roll the way the roll went. And then
Tōan said "hold this," and for the next two hours he held — the lamp, the
leg, the man's shoulders through the worst of it, the bowl — and the shelter
was small and crowded and hot and there was a great deal of blood, more than
the square, more than he had ever—

and it did not touch him.

He noticed that from a small distance, the way he noticed most things about
himself now. His hands did not shake. His breath stayed low. The place he
went to was the place the bowl had taught him and the frames had taught him
before that: he found where things were moving — the lamp's flicker, the
man's breathing, Tōan's two-word requests — and he attended to them, and
nothing else got in, and the two hours went by in a single long carried
present in which he was, for the first time in over a year, of *use*.

The man kept the leg. They came down the saddle in the dark, gear shared
between them, and behind them the camp's fires burned in the new clearing
like a small disreputable constellation.

"You didn't ask," said Tōan, on the road.

"Ask what?"

"Whether he'd live. They always ask. You watched what mattered instead."
The gear clinked on his back, matching his stride. "There's medics I've
worked with who never learned that. Where'd you learn to hold a lamp?"

"A man came to our house once to write the inventory," said Sazare. "I held
the lamp." — which was not where he had learned it and both of them knew it,
and Tōan let it stand, because the third law held on roads as well as in
houses.

But something about the dark, and the shared weight, and the smell of blood
and resin, had unlocked a door somewhere in Sazare's chest, and half a mile
on, without deciding to — the way his hands had come up in the square without
deciding to — he heard himself say:

"I met the one who did it. Your exam."

Tōan's stride did not change. That was itself an answer: a man who needed to
ask *what exam* would have broken step.

"On the night of the coup. I was in a doorway on the hill, in the fog,
because I was stupid, and a building was about to come down on the doorway,
and he threw me out of it. Not saved me. Moved me. Like luggage." The road
went on under them. "He asked whose I was. I said the name. And he looked at
me for — for about a quarter of a second there was something, and I've never
worked out what it was. And then he told me to go home, and called me an
idiot, and went up the hill, and lost, and left the country."

They walked.

"I know what he did," Sazare said. "In that room. I know it was — I'm not
saying he's—" He gave up on the sentence. "But he's the only person from
that whole year who told me to go home. Like there was a home. Like I was a
child. Everyone else was very kind and nobody else said that."

Tōan walked for a while, the gear keeping his slow time.

"I was outside that room," he said at last, "the year he did it. That's mine;
you don't have to carry it. I'll tell you the one thing I know about him
that's worth anything, and it's a medic's fact, not a story." The steam of
his breath went back over his shoulder in the cold. "Whatever they made in
that room, they made it out of a child who walked in off the street. He
wasn't in the class. Nobody made him. That's the piece everyone tells for
horror, and they tell it wrong. A child *volunteers* for a thing like that
in the exact same way you volunteered your arm in that square — because
everything in the world has already gone so far wrong that it looks like the
only move left on the board. Nobody's born a demon. They're cornered into
it, one reasonable-looking move at a time."

"He wasn't cornered. He chose—"

"So did you," said Tōan, without heat. "Four hundred people watched you
choose. And here you are holding lamps, because a fat horse walked you up a
different road than the one they walked him down. That's the whole
difference I've ever been able to find between the ones I patch and the ones
I hear about, and I've been looking for thirty years: the road. Not the
child. The road."

Sazare carried that the rest of the way down the mountain, and it did not
comfort him, and he did not think it had been meant to. It went into the
collection — with the shrine, and the wall of names, and the back of the
door — into the growing heap of things said to him by adults in this
country, plainly, at night, that he was going to spend the rest of his life
unpacking.

*Like there was a home. Like I was a child.*

Far below, the lamps of Yumoto came up through the trees, and the steam
above the town stood silver in the moonlight, going up, and up, and up.
