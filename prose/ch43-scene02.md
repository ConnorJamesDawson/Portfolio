# Chapter 43, Scene 2 — What Was in the Drawer

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the Intelligence Division. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,748 words.*

---

They sent for him on the fourteenth, which was two months and six days
after he had stood in Sister Kaburagi's empty office with a charge
nurse as a witness and written two slips in his own hand.

The Intelligence Division was cooler than the hospital and always had
been, being half underground and built into the hill's north face, and
it smelled of the particular combination — paper, lamp oil, damp
plaster and the ghost of tobacco somebody had given up four years ago
and had not managed to get out of the furnishings — that Sazare had
come, over three visits, to associate with being told things he did not
want to know.

Ibiki had the envelope on the desk in a wrapper with a case number on
it. Beside it were four sheets of somebody else's handwriting, and a
tray, and in the tray a very small quantity of grey dust.

"Sit down," Ibiki said. "Two months of work, and I'm going to give you
all of it, because you'll be no use to me holding half."

---

The paper was from a mill in the Land of Fire, one of nine, and had
been sold in eleven countries.

The ink was ordinary.

The hand was right-handed, unhurried, and — this was the four sheets —
had been analysed by a man who had spent nineteen years doing nothing
else, whose conclusion was that the writer had been taught to write by
somebody who had been taught in a medical school, and who had then
spent a considerable amount of effort making the hand plainer than it
naturally was.

"Plainer," said Sazare.

"He writes down. It's the only vanity in the whole document and he'd
have been better off leaving it alone." Ibiki turned a sheet. "The dust
is the seal. There was one — an inert one, on the flap, not a trap,
just a tell. It went when the envelope opened and it left about that
much." He indicated the tray. "Kurihara took it apart for us. Her
finding is that it's a two-part indexing seal, meaning the writer keeps
a matching half, meaning he wanted to know if you'd opened it and when.
He does not know that you did, because you reported it inside eleven
minutes and it never went back out of this building."

"Kurihara."

"You'll meet her. She's certifying your stones." Ibiki put the sheet
down. "Here's the whole of what we have. He is a medically trained
right-handed man with access to hospital paper, sufficient standing to
read a materials schedule and understand it, and sufficient patience to
wait two months for a reply from a boy he has never met. That is not a
description. It's a shape. I can put about four hundred people in it
and I can eliminate none of them."

"That's less than I expected."

"It's considerably less than I expected, and I have been doing this for
twenty-two years." Ibiki laced his hands. "Which brings me to the part
I came for. I want you to answer it."

---

Sazare had known that was coming since the second sheet.

He had constructed it on the walk down the hill, four different
versions of the conversation, and had got most of the way through
deciding which of them to have when the fourth version had arrived
uninvited and reminded him of the thing in his drawer.

"Before you make the offer," he said, "there's something you don't
have."

Ibiki said nothing.

"I wrote a reply. In the third month, on a Tuesday. Four drafts. Eleven
hundred words on the medium finding — the whole of it, better written
than anything I've given the hospital. I didn't send it. I put it in
the drawer of the low table in my room and it has been there for six
weeks and I did not file a slip on it."

The room was quiet.

"That last part is the part that matters," Sazare said, "and I'd like
it noted that I'm telling you rather than you finding it, and also that
I'm aware that telling you now is worth considerably less than telling
you in the third month, which is when I should have done it."

"Why didn't you."

"Because a slip on a letter I received is a report. A slip on a letter
I wrote is a confession." He kept his hands still on his knees.
"Reporting the envelope cost me nothing. Everybody in that hospital
thought better of me for it inside a day. Reporting that I'd written
eleven hundred words to him and kept them in a drawer costs me the
thing I got for reporting the envelope, and I worked that out inside
about four seconds in the third month and then spent six weeks not
looking at it."

"And today."

"Today I made two mistakes in front of eleven people and one of them
turned into a volunteer." Sazare heard how that sounded and did not
soften it. "I'm not offering that as a reason. It's just the thing that
happened immediately before I stopped being able to leave this
standing."

Ibiki got up.

He went to the cabinet, and came back with a blank slip and a pen, and
put both down in front of Sazare, and sat.

"Write it," he said. "Date it today. Note the six weeks. Note that you
withheld it and why — and put the *why* in, the real one, the one about
what it cost you, not a tidy one — and then sign it, and then I'm going
to tell you what I think."

Sazare wrote it. It took four minutes and it was the worst document
he had ever produced about himself, and he did not soften any part of
it, and he had to stop once, at the sentence about the four seconds,
because writing it down made it a different size than it had been in
his head.

Ibiki read it twice.

"Do you know what I had before this?" he said. "I had a young man who
reported a letter in eleven minutes and had been extremely correct ever
since, and I could not use him for anything, because a man who has only
ever been correct is a man whose floor I have not found."

"You wanted me to have done something wrong."

"I want to know what you do *after*." He put the slip in the wrapper
with the envelope. "You've now told me, unprompted, at cost, about the
one thing in this business that could have ended you. That is worth
more to me than the eleven minutes. The eleven minutes told me you were
frightened. This tells me what happens when you're not."

---

"Now. The letter."

"You want a controlled correspondence."

"I want three exchanges over about nine months, of which the first two
are entirely yours and true, and I'll tell you now that the true part
is not generosity, it's the only thing that works." Ibiki turned the
tray of dust with one finger. "He has read your schedules. He has
already established that he can tell your real work from a decoration
at a distance of four hundred miles. The first false sentence you write
him, he'll have it, and the correspondence will end that afternoon and
you will never know it ended."

"So I send him the eleven hundred words."

"You send him the eleven hundred words," said Ibiki, "and here is the
part you're going to hate, and I want you to hate it out loud now
rather than in the ninth month: it will help him. Whatever he is doing,
your finding will make it go faster. That is not a risk of this
operation, it is the fee, and I am telling you the fee before you agree
rather than after, which is a courtesy I do not extend to most people
and which I am extending to you because you just filled out that slip."

Sazare looked at the tray.

"What do I get."

"Three letters in his hand, a delivery route, a fishmonger's Thursdays,
and about nine months of a man who thinks he is running you." Ibiki
leaned back. "And one other thing, which is the actual reason, and it's
yours and not mine. In nine months he will have told you what he wants.
Nobody tells you what they want in the first letter. They tell you in
the third, when they've decided you're worth the risk of asking."

"And if the answer is that he wants the stone."

"Then we'll have found that out in the ninth month rather than the day
he takes it." Ibiki stood. "Go home. Don't answer me today. And Sazare
— the letter in the drawer stays in the drawer until I tell you, and
you do not rewrite it. Whatever's in there is the honest version. If
you go back to it now you'll write it for me, and he'll read it and
know."

---

He walked back up the hill in the last of the light with the ledger
running.

*Withheld a material fact for six weeks out of self-interest, knowing
inside four seconds that I was doing it and knowing exactly why.*

*Disclosed it today, unprompted, at cost, and the disclosure bought me
something I could not have bought any other way, because the only thing
that could establish what I do after a failure was an actual failure.*

He stopped on the hill and stood for a moment with his hand on somebody
else's wall.

*That is the second time today.*

*I am now going to write down the thing I was careful about this
morning, because it has arrived twice in one day and I would be lying
by omission if I left it out.*

*A fault has an output. Not always. Not reliably. But the column exists
and I have been keeping books with it ruled off for eleven years.*

*And the exact reason I have to be careful is sitting in the same
paragraph: both of today's outputs came from faults I did not choose.
The moment I choose one it stops being a fault and becomes a tactic,
and a tactic that looks like an honest failure is the single most
useful thing a dishonest man can own.*

*I know that because somebody four hundred miles away is currently
using one on me and it is working.*
