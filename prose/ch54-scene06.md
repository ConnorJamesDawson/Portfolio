# Chapter 54, Scene 6 — What I've Got

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the Hokage's office. Sazare Ruri, age eighteen.
POV: Sazare. ~2180 words.*

---

"I don't know what that word means," said Sazare.

Tsunade stopped with the cup at her mouth.

"I want to be exact about this, because you've just told me I've
mistaken a thing for another thing, and the honest position is that I
can't tell you you're wrong, because I have never had the second thing
described to me by anybody I'd trust."

He was still sitting down. He had his hands on his knees.

"I've got two sources. That's the whole of it. There's nine pages in a
book I borrowed out of the ward library when I was sixteen because it
had a chapter on Kiri, and the nine pages are about the etiquette of
the approach, and they're a hundred and forty years old and they're
about a class of people who don't exist any more, and even at sixteen I
could tell the man who wrote them had never done any of it.

"And there was a girl on the staircase at the east end of the second
floor in the eleventh month when I was fifteen, who said something to
me that I did not understand at the time and worked out about two years
later, and I have never seen her again and I don't know her name.

"That's it. That's the corpus."

"Ruri—"

"So when you say *you don't know what that word means*, I can't fight
you on it. You're right. I've got nine pages and a staircase. I'd fail
an examination on it."

He looked up.

"But I'm not going to say the sentence you're expecting, because I've
never said a thing I couldn't support and I'm not starting tonight at
midnight in your office."

---

She put the cup down.

"Then what are you doing here?"

"I'm going to give you what I've actually got," said Sazare, "and
you're going to tell me what it is, because you know and I don't."

"That's not—"

"You asked for the rest of it eleven times in three years. Every single
time. I put a thing on the table and you took it off and put it on the
floor and said *where's the rest*. So." He sat forward. "This is the
rest."

"I gave you three on Tuesday. Those were the three I could stand
saying. There are more than three."

"How many more?"

"I don't know. It isn't a closed list. I'll do the ones I can get out."

"You haven't got your book."

"No."

"You've got it in a drawer."

"I've got it in a drawer at home," said Sazare, "because Shizune told
me on the stair in the eighth month that I wasn't to put anything about
you on that desk, and she was right, and I've never written a word of
this down anywhere, and it's the only thing in my life that isn't
written down."

He stopped for a second, and found the thing, and did not like it, and
said it.

"So it isn't a document. It's just me."

---

"One," he said.

"You told me to say the end of a sentence out loud.

"It was the twelfth of the fourth month, in the small room off the
records floor, and I'd stopped in the middle of one, which is what I do
— I get about two-thirds through and I can see it landing wrong and I
stop and I go and build a better one and the better one is always
worse. And you didn't wait and you didn't help me and you didn't finish
it for me, which is what Yagi does and I'm grateful to him for it and it
has made me nine years worse.

"You said *say the end of that sentence out loud*.

"And I did, and it was bad, and you said *right, that's what you
think*, and we carried on.

"Nobody has ever done that. Everybody in my life has either finished my
sentences or waited politely for me to stop having a go. You made me
finish one badly and then treated the bad one as the actual content."

"That's not a reason to—"

"I'm not finished. Two."

---

"You come in at the fourth hour.

"Not sometimes. I've got it since the third month of last year, and
it's about four days in five, and it's before the guard changes and
about two hours before anybody who wants to be seen coming in.

"I know because of the ink. When I come up at the seventh hour the top
sheet on the left-hand stack is dry all the way to the edge and the
third or fourth one down is still tacky at the fold, and dry to the
edge is about three hours in this building in winter and about ninety
minutes in the summer, and I checked that with a nib and a spoiled
sheet in my own room because I wanted to know if I was inventing it.

"And it's always the worst paper. Always. The casualty returns and the
compensation files and the ones with a family name on them go first,
before anyone's in, and the supply and the appointments and the things
people watch you do go at eleven.

"You do the unbearable ones alone in the dark and the visible ones with
an audience, and I don't think you've ever told anybody that you do
that, and I don't think you know that you do it."

Tsunade did not say anything.

"Three.

"You say *I don't know* in front of people.

"Twice on the record. Ninth month, the Tanzaku road briefing, in front
of eleven people including two of the fifteen, and you said *I don't
know, and I'm not going to guess with that in the room*. And once to me
in the ward in the second month, about a boy with a spinal, and you
said it and then you went and got Yagi.

"There is not one other person above the rank of chūnin in this
building who does that, and I've watched all of them, and what they do
instead is produce a shape that has the volume of an answer. You cost
yourself something every time you do it and you do it anyway.

"Four."

---

"You were wrong about me and you said so to somebody else."

She moved.

"You told Kanzaki, in the eleventh month, that you'd read the first
surplus report and decided I was building a case for a bigger budget,
and that you'd been wrong about that, and you told her — not me — that
you'd been wrong about it. And she told me, four months later, in about
one and a half sentences, because she thought I already knew.

"That's the item. Not that you were wrong. That you fixed it in the
place where it had happened, with the person you'd said it to, and
never once collected the credit off me."

"Five.

"You read a casualty figure at exactly the same speed as a supply
figure.

"I thought that was coldness for about a year and a half. It isn't.
I've watched you do it about sixty times and the tell is the hands —
they go flat on the desk about a second before, both of them, every
time — and what it actually is, is that you decided a long time ago that
changing your voice for the dead is something you do for the living
people in the room, and you don't do it, because you think it's a
performance and you think a performance in that room is obscene.

"You are wrong about that, and I've thought about telling you so about
forty times, and I never have, because it's yours."

---

"Six.

"In the first four months, you did not ask me about crystal.

"Not once. I have got a record of the first thing every single person
in this village said to me and it's the same thing, ninety-four times
out of a hundred and six, and it's not even rude, it's just that
there's a thing about me that's interesting and people go to it.

"You asked me about a supply figure. You asked me about a supply figure
for four months, and you were wrong about the supply figure, and I
corrected you three times before I worked out that you knew.

"Seven.

"You let me be wrong in front of the council in the sixth month and you
did not save me.

"I had the throughput number the wrong way round and you sat four feet
away and let it happen and let Ariake Fumi take it off me in front of
eight people, and I have never in my life been so — "

He stopped, and found the word, and it was not the one he had been
going for.

"— *treated as an adult*," he said.

---

"Eight.

"You use my name.

"That's the whole item. You say *Ruri*. It took me eleven months to
notice, and I noticed because Kanzaki's minute for that month has *the
fabricator* in it four times and the Corps board paper has *the
Kirigakure asset*, and I read all three the same afternoon."

He stopped.

He sat with his hands on his knees in a chair he had sat in four
hundred times and got to the end of what he had.

"That's eight," he said. "There are more. That's the eight I can get
out at midnight."

---

And then he said the thing he had not planned, and it was the only
thing that mattered, and it took him a second to work out why.

"None of those are on your list."

Tsunade looked at him.

"You gave me six," he said. "The name, the story, the henge, what you
can do, the mothering, the post. Six, in order, revised. And I have
just given you eight and not one of mine is one of yours. Not partly.
Not a version of one. There's no overlap at all."

"Ruri—"

"That's not me being clever. That's the finding. If I'd built this out
of the thing you say I've built it out of — if it's a boy who's
mistaken kindness for something, if I've taken the only adult who was
decent to me and made a beautiful wrong thing out of her — then my
eight would be *your six*, in different words. That's what that failure
looks like. I'd have made a shape out of the name and the legend and
the fact that you fixed me, because those are the things a boy would
have.

"They're not what's on it. What's on it is ink drying at the edge of a
sheet and a woman putting both hands flat on a desk before she reads a
number.

"Nobody makes that up. Nobody could. I'd have had to *watch you for
three years*."

The room was very quiet.

"So I don't know what the word means," said Sazare. "I've told you
that. You can have that. But whatever it is that I've got, it isn't the
thing you just accused me of, because I have got the wrong list for it,
and I have never in my life been able to fake a list."

---

She had gone back to the window somewhere in the middle of six.

He had not noticed her do it. That was itself remarkable and he noted
it and let it go.

"Is that all of it," she said. Not as a question.

"No."

"Then finish."

He got up.

He had been meaning to say something entirely different, and he had it
ready, and it had four clauses in it and a bracket, and it was
excellent.

He did not say it.

"I don't know what I'd do without you," said Sazare.

---

It went across the room and there was nothing under it.

He knew that as it left him. He knew it more clearly than he had ever
known anything about his own work: that there was no measurement in it,
no date, no count, no observation, nothing on a felt, nothing he could
put on a table and ask a person to weigh.

It was a claim with no evidence attached.

He had never made one before. Not once, not in eleven years, not in
four hundred books, not on a single line of a single page — he had put
*unknown* in ink three hundred and eleven times rather than write down
a thing he could not stand behind.

And it was true, and he could not show that it was true, and he said it
anyway.

That was the moment, and he did not know it was the moment.

Tsunade did not turn round.

She stood at the window with her shoulder against the frame and the cup
in her right hand and the village dark underneath her, and she looked
out of it at the horizon, at the place where the hills were when there
was light, at nothing at all.

And then she started to cry.
