# Chapter 74, Scene 10 — Book One

*Act 5, part A. Konoha, the tower and the north fields. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,920 words.*

---

He got up at four on the Friday and did it at the desk in the upper
room with the lamp down and all four books out in a row, which was the
first time in his life they had ever been out of the case together for
any purpose except moving house.

Book one was smaller than the others.

He had forgotten that. It was a cheap thing — grey board covers gone
soft at the corners, a spine he had restitched himself at seventeen
with waxed thread and no skill, and the paper inside was the bad
absorbent kind that took ink the way a sponge takes water, so that
every line he had written in it at sixteen had a slight bleed on it
and looked, to a man who had spent three years being certified,
faintly incompetent.

He opened the front.

*I am not going to get stronger. Find out how it works.*

The hand was terrible.

That was the thing he had not braced for. He had read this page
perhaps four hundred times and never once looked at it as an object,
which was the entire finding of Monday and he had needed a Sannin to
give it to him: the strokes were laid too hard, the pen dragged where
a hand that was afraid had pushed it, and the second sentence was
smaller and neater than the first because the first had been written
by somebody with something in his chest and the second had been
copied off a boat.

*Copied,* he thought. *That is the other thing.*

*This is not the original. The original was written at fifteen, on a
boat, on the back of something, with a hand that had healed wrong.
That sheet is gone. It went the way everything from that year went.*

*So the sentence I am striking this morning has been a copy since I
was sixteen, and I have treated it for three years as the founding
document.*

*Which is the correct description of the entire mechanism, and I am
going to enter it, and it is the last thing the old creed ever tells
me.*

---

He had had four days.

He set them out first, in the fourth book, in the small exact hand,
because he could not build to a specification without listing the
loads.

*Monday. It is a governor, it fires on capacity, and it was laid over
a going concern to shut it. It was written by a boy whose hands had
come up on their own once, in a square, a hundred and forty seconds
early, and who had correctly established that you cannot put a clause
on a decision that never occurs — and had therefore governed the only
variable left, which was how far the hands could reach. It was the
best available engineering with the materials in the room, and it did
not work, and I have the incident report.*

*Also Monday, from a tray, in seventeen minutes: don't write what you
are. Write what you are FOR, and make it something you can be caught
failing at.*

*Tuesday. Hyūga Hinata commissioned, in writing, with a reference
number, the thing that is going to hurt her most, because she had
worked out that the only version worth having is the one she is not
protected from. A woman who has been holding one sentence for eleven
years ordered the ambush herself.*

*Also Tuesday: I became a man who keeps something from Uzumaki
Naruto, and the only thing that made it survivable was telling him
that I was doing it and putting an undertaking in a diary that he can
hold me to.*

*Wednesday. I found the decision, cold, in four minutes, and spent it
on a no, and it cost me the cheapest and best layer of my own style,
permanently, by my own hand. And the man it was about took the same
decision independently twenty minutes later and his was better than
mine, because mine was made about him and his was made by him.*

*Thursday. Where the fabricator is unavailable, the article waits.*

*And: the day somebody could take this hat off me on a Tuesday, and
the lists would still go up on the Wednesday.*

He looked at the list for a long time in the lamplight.

*Every one of these is the same object.*

*Every single one. Four days, five people, and not one of them is
about strength. They are all about a thing HELD — a secret, a name, a
technique, an answer, an office, an article that cannot be replaced —
and every one of them turns on whether the person holding it can say
who it is for, and to whom, and by when.*

*I am about to become a man who holds a very great deal.*

*The spring. The sword. A boy's answer. A woman's name. Whatever she
has not said about the mountain. And now, as of Monday at eleven
minutes past seven, my own capacity, with no ceiling on it and
nothing laid over it to shut it.*

*The old clause protected everybody by ensuring I held nothing worth
taking. That is finished, it was finished before it was struck, and
it was probably finished the afternoon I grew a thing in a yard that
drinks.*

*So the replacement is not a bigger promise. It is the same promise
with the correct variable in it.*

He wrote three drafts and burned them in the lamp, which was
theatrical and he did it anyway, and the fourth one came at about
half five and took under a minute, the way the true ones always did.

Then he wrote it into the front of all four books.

He did book four first, because book four was the working one and his
hand was not steady; then three; then two; and by the time he got to
book one the hand had come back, and he was glad of it, because the
page mattered.

He drew a single rule through the first sentence — one line, ruled,
not scratched out, the way you strike a superseded clause in a minute
book so that the record shows what was there — and dated the strike
in the margin, *16/3, 07:11, witnessed*, and wrote underneath it:

*I am going to be strong enough that nobody standing near me has to
be lucky.*

*Acceptance test: anything I hold, I can say who it is for — out
loud, to them, on the day they ask. Where I cannot, I say that I
cannot, and say when I will be able to, and I am held to the date.*

*Find out how it works.*

The second sentence he did not touch. It was eleven years old and had
spent three of them underneath the other one, and it had never once
needed help.

---

Jiraiya read it out of book one at six sharp, standing in the ruined
field with the sun not up, holding the little grey book the way you
hold a thing that is somebody else's.

He read it twice. The second time he read it out loud, to the frost,
and Fukasaku listened to it from the cloth with his arms folded and
his eyes half shut.

Then he shut the book and gave it back.

"Say the test again," said Jiraiya.

"*Anything I hold, I can say who it is for — out loud, to them, on
the day they ask.*"

"Right." He scratched his jaw. "You know I'm going to use that on
you."

"I am aware."

"No, kid, I mean I'm going to use it *this year*. There's a thing in
that yard and there's a boy in this village and there's about four
months before somebody makes you answer the question in a room where
you can't pick the furniture, and you have just handed me the stick
to hit you with, in writing, in four books, on purpose." He said it
without any pleasure at all, which was how Sazare knew it was the
serious version. "That's what a specification is for. Most men write
one they can live inside. You've written one that can catch you."

"That was the instruction."

"That was the instruction," Jiraiya agreed, "and I've given it about
eleven times in my life and two people ever did it." He picked up the
practice swords and speared one into the soft ground. "Footwork.
You're four days behind and the ground's still soup."

That was all. Sazare had run four projections on the walk down the
lane, and three of them had a speech in, and the one that had turned
out to be correct was the one where a man read a page twice and said
*right* and picked up a sword — and he found, standing in the churn
with his coat on and his hands cold, that he preferred it by a
distance he could not measure.

"Boy," said Fukasaku, from the cloth, without opening his eyes.

"Fukasaku-sama."

"Said out loud, in a field, before witnesses, on a stated day, and
entered where it can be found." The old toad's arms stayed folded.
"That's the rite. Four hundred years, and I have sat through
perhaps six hundred of them, and they are all exactly that dull, and
the dullness is the entire point.

"It'll want doing again. Not soon — you're eighteen, that one should
hold you a good while. But it'll change, and when it changes you say
the new one out loud with somebody listening and you date it and you
put the old one under a rule and you don't tear the page out. That
last part is the part everyone gets wrong. You don't tear the page
out, boy. The one you were is the reason the one you are came out in
this shape."

"Understood."

"Mm," said Fukasaku, and went back to sleep, or to whatever four
hundred years does instead.

---

He wrote the entry on the walk home, as always, at the top of the
lane, standing up, with the sun properly over the hills.

*20/3. Amendment entered, all four books. Rite conducted. Struck
clause preserved under a rule and dated.*

*Book one has a line through the front page and my own worse hand
underneath it and the two do not match at all, and I looked at that
for some time before I closed it, because there is no version of the
sixteen-year-old who copied that sentence off a boat who would have
believed one word of the replacement.*

*He would have said it was a boast. He would have been wrong, and I
am not going to be able to explain to him why, because he is not
available for comment.*

*I am not going to tear the page out.*

He went to put the notebook back inside his coat, and it caught.

The button — third down on the left, a sixteenth proud of the row,
four holes off square, nine passes of a knife round the chamfer,
eighteen years of catching on things — had snagged the notebook's
corner, exactly the way its maker had promised him it would, and he
stood in the lane with the two objects hooked together and a
completely idiotic expression on his face that he was extremely
grateful nobody was there to survey.

It would take four seconds to thin the back.

He left it.
