# Chapter 52, Scene 2 — Ame

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the quartermaster's yard. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~2,495 words.*

---

There were two of them on the twenty-fourth.

He came into the yard at about eight with the core under his arm and
there was Katsuyu on the corner of bench three where she always was,
and beside her, taking up rather more of the bench than seemed
reasonable, something the size of a large dog.

Paler. Almost no colour in her at all, and the two dark stripes that
ran back over Katsuyu's mantle were on this one as well and had gone to
a sort of grey-brown, the way black cloth goes when it has been in the
sun for forty years.

Sazare stopped in the gateway.

"This is Ame," said Katsuyu. "She asked to come. I have said nothing to
her about you that was not about the work and I want that said first
and in front of her, because I have been thinking about what I say to
people all month and I did not know how to raise it with you in
advance, so I have not, and I hope that was right."

"...Yes. It was."

"Good evening," said Ame.

Her voice was Katsuyu's voice and it was slower, by about a quarter,
and there was something in the pacing of it that took him nine or ten
sentences to identify and which turned out to be that she did not
appear to be going anywhere afterwards.

"Ame," he said. "Is that—"

"It is not a name in your sense. It means rain." A long settling. "It
is what I was doing when somebody first needed to refer to me, which
was a very long time ago, and it stuck, and I have never minded it. I
am told your people choose them in advance, which I find extraordinary
and have thought about a great deal."

---

He got the bloodlines out of her in about eleven minutes and it was the
single most anticlimactic conversation of his life.

He had not planned to. He had planned to ask about natural energy and
capacity and the thing that happened past eleven, and he had four
pages of questions in his coat, and at about the fourth minute she said
something in passing about a family in the Land of Rivers who had been
good at finding water, and he heard the word *family* and stopped.

"Say that again."

"They were good at finding water. It is not interesting, I only
mentioned it because you asked what changes and that is one of the
things that does not."

"You said the grandchildren were better at it."

"They usually are."

"Why?"

"Because the grandmother was in the room," said Ame.

Sazare put the file down.

"That's it?"

"I am not certain what you are asking."

"They were better at it because she was there. Because they watched
her, and she corrected them, and they started earlier than she did and
had somebody to compare themselves against." He was writing now. "It's
not in them. It's the *room*."

"Yes."

"And after four or five generations of that, somebody says it is in the
blood."

"Somebody always says that," said Ame. "I do not know why. It happens
every time and it takes about that long, and afterwards they are
extremely proud of it and stop letting anybody else watch, and then in
a hundred years or two hundred they are not especially good at it any
more and nobody can work out what went wrong."

She said all of it in the voice a person uses for the weather.

"Ame," said Sazare. "Are kekkei genkai real?"

"I do not know the word."

"Bloodline limits. Crystal, wood, ice — techniques that only certain
families can do, that pass down, that are supposed to be in the body."

"Ah," said Ame. "No."

---

He sat down on the yard stone.

"Say the whole of it," he said, and his voice did something he did not
attempt to correct.

"There is not very much of a whole. Chakra was given — once, by
somebody, a very long time ago, and I was not there and neither was
anybody I have met, and what I have is what I was told by things that
were told by things." A slow settling. "The eyes are different. The
eyes came down a body from before that, from the one who gave it, and
those are in the blood in the way you mean and there is no argument
about it.

"Everything else is people working things out. And then their children
watching them work things out, and being better at it, and their
children better still. And then a name, and then a wall round it, and
then it goes away."

"Nobody knows this."

"A great many of us know it," said Ame, sounding faintly puzzled. "It
is not a secret. It is only that nobody has asked."

"For how long?"

"I could not tell you. I am poor with numbers of that size." A pause.
"I have been summoned by eleven people in my life and instructed by all
eleven and asked a question by none of them, and Katsuyu tells me hers
is nine and none, and I would have said any of it at any time and so
would she."

---

He worked out what he had inside about four minutes and it was
considerably less than nothing.

"Ame — would you say that in front of a room of people?"

"If somebody asked me."

"Eleven physicians and four clan representatives, in a hall, in front
of a clerk taking a record."

"...I would say the same thing," said Ame. "I do not know how to say
anything else. Is that what is required?"

"No," said Sazare. "It's not. That's the problem."

He got up and walked to the end of the bench and back, which he did
about four times a year.

*It is not evidence.*

*I have been given the answer by a creature who has held it for longer
than there has been a village here, and it is worth nothing whatsoever
in a room. Not because she is a slug. Because it is testimony, and it
is testimony about something that happened before there were records,
delivered by something that was told it by something else, and there
are four clans in this village who would take it apart in about nine
minutes and every one of the nine would be technically correct.*

*And the Hokage has forbidden the paper anyway, on grounds I agreed
with in her office on the first of the eighth month and have not
stopped agreeing with.*

*So what I have got is: I am right.*

*I was at eight in ten in the eighth month and I am at something above
nine now, and it has changed nothing at all except that I can no longer
be uncertain.*

*I preferred the uncertainty. I want that written down because it is
the honest half and I do not entirely understand it yet.*

---

She talked for a while about other things, and he got about four pages,
and roughly a page and a half of it was useful.

She was a poor witness in exactly the way Katsuyu was a poor witness,
and worse, because she was older and more digressive and had opinions.
She could not tell him where natural energy came from — *it is in
everything and I do not know where it begins, I have said this to
somebody before* — and could not say whether it was finite, and became
extremely animated about a particular kind of moss.

But she gave him two things.

The first was that what happened past eleven had a shape and she knew
what it was called, and the name was not a name, it was a description,
and she gave it in about nine words and he wrote all nine down and did
not fully understand any of them and knew he had something.

The second was the thing about the room.

*The grandmother was in the room.*

*I have four hundred thousand courses laid alone at benches, and thirty
articles a night at a third over with twice the grip, and a boy of
eleven who wrote "easy" in a margin because somebody was standing
behind him.*

*It was never a difference in the hands. It has been a difference in
the room, the whole time, for me and for the Land of Rivers and for
four hundred years of the Sazare and for whatever family the six hands
on that scroll belonged to.*

*And the reason the Sazare kept it for three hundred years and did not
lose it is that we kept putting the grandmother in the room, and the
reason it stopped is that somebody put a sword through the last two
people who could have stood behind me.*

He did not write the last part of that down.

He wrote it four days later, and struck it out, and wrote it again.

---

She mentioned the Hokage at about ten o'clock, in passing, in the
middle of something about the moss.

"Katsuyu says the Hokage is not pleased with you."

Sazare stopped with the file halfway to the bench.

"...Does she."

"I hope that is not a difficulty. She did not say it as a complaint —
she was explaining why she has been coming here more than usual and
she said the Hokage is not pleased with him at present, and I said ah,
and that was the whole of it." A slow settling. "I am told I ought to
have asked before repeating it. Katsuyu has said so twice this evening
and I do not entirely follow the reasoning, but I am sorry."

"Don't be. It's true."

"Yes, she was quite clear."

He stood in the yard with a file in his hand.

*They talk to each other.*

*That is what that is. It is not that Katsuyu has been reporting me
anywhere — she has never reported anything to anybody and said so in
this yard on the fourteenth in front of the Hokage and it went through
that woman like a knife. It is that there is a network, and it is
horizontal, and it discusses her.*

*I assumed a channel existed and it does. It has existed the whole
time. It runs sideways and it has never once gone up.*

*And she is in it as a subject and has never been in it as a party, and
she does not know, and there is no version of this where I tell her.*

"Ame," he said. "Have you known her long?"

"Tsunade? I have not met her." A pause. "I met her grandfather twice.
He was quite young. He asked me a great many questions about a river
and I found him tiring and I have thought about him rather often since,
which I did not expect."

Sazare did not move.

"She is like that as well, they say," Ame went on comfortably. "Katsuyu
says she has always been like that."

---

He had four questions.

He had them assembled and ordered and every one of them was a good
question, and the first of them was *what did he ask about the river*,
and he could have had the whole of it in about nine minutes from a
creature who would have answered without any idea that she was
handing over something that did not belong to her.

He looked at the bench for a moment.

"That's not mine," he said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"What you've just said, and the four things I want to ask you next.
They're hers." He put the file down. "You'd tell me. You wouldn't mind
and you'd think it was an ordinary conversation about a river, and it
would be, from where you're sitting.

"And I'd have something about her that she didn't give me, out of
somebody who didn't know it was hers to keep. I've had that done to me
about four hundred times. I know exactly what it's worth and I know
exactly what it costs, and I'm not doing it."

There was a pause.

"Katsuyu," said Ame, "you did not tell me he was like this."

"He is like this about twice a month," said Katsuyu. "I have never
worked out when."

---

The mate question came at about eleven and it came out of nowhere, in
the middle of nothing, in the flattest voice of the evening.

"Why have you no mate?"

Sazare, who had been writing, stopped.

He waited for about four seconds for the rest of the sentence and there
was no rest of it.

"...Sorry?"

"You have no mate," said Ame. "You are — I am poor at your ages, and
Katsuyu tells me eighteen, and I am told that is late-ish and not very
late, and you have not got one, and I have been here three hours and
nobody has come, and there is nobody in your entries."

"You've been reading my entries?"

"They are on the bench and you leave them open," said Ame, entirely
without embarrassment. "I did not know that was not permitted. I shall
stop."

"That's — no. That's not the—"

"So why have you no mate?"

---

He sat on a stool in a yard at eleven o'clock at night and found that
he had nothing at all.

Not a deflection. He had four of those and every one of them was a
sentence about being busy, and he heard all four in his head and
discarded all four, because they were the answer you give a person and
this was not a person and she would simply have said *yes, but why*.

And underneath the four was the thing he had actually noticed in the
first second and a half and had not been able to put anywhere.

*Nobody has ever asked me.*

*Not once. Yagi has known me four years and Kanzaki three and Ashida
three and Handa two, and Emiko asks me every single day whether I have
eaten, and not one of them has ever asked me this, and the reason is
not that they have not wondered.*

*It is that they are people, and people do not ask, and I did not know
until eleven o'clock this evening that there was a question there at
all that was going unasked, because the not-asking is so complete that
it does not leave a hole.*

*And the first thing in the world to ask me is a slug who has been in
this yard three hours and thought it was a reasonable follow-up to
some moss.*

"I don't know," he said.

"Ah," said Ame. "Then you should find out. You are eighteen and you
have a great deal of time and it is the sort of thing that gets away
from people."

And she went back to the moss.
