# Chapter 37, Scene 3 — The Specification Problem

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the room over the persimmon tree. Sazare Ruri,
age eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,141 words.*

---

He got stuck on Tsunade for eleven weeks and the reason he got stuck
was professional.

He established this at the bench, on paper, in the way he established
everything, and the analysis was sound and complete and he went back to
it four times looking for the error and did not find it, because there
was not one.

*Requirement: an object, made by me, wearable, that she would actually
wear.*

*Constraint 1: rings are out.* And they were, comprehensively, and it
had taken him about four seconds to see it and eleven weeks to accept
it. Her technique landed on the heel of the right hand. A ring on
either hand was, at best, a thing that would be destroyed inside a
month; at worst it was a hard object between a chakra strike and the
bone behind it, which is how a person breaks their own finger. He had
watched her hands for two years. There had never been anything on
them. There was a reason and the reason was arithmetic.

*Constraint 2: bracelets are rings with a longer lever.* Same problem,
worse. Anything rigid on a wrist that moved like that was a fracture
waiting for a bad angle.

*Constraint 3: she will not wear a second necklace.*

He wrote that one and looked at it and did not need to write the
reasoning, because the reasoning was eighty years of gold and two
repairs and a thumb-worn patch on the lower left of a stone that no
polisher had ever made.

That left ears.

*Constraint 4: her ears are not pierced.*

---

He checked that four separate times over three Thursdays before he
allowed himself to write it down, because it was the kind of
observation that could be wrong and because being wrong about it would
make everything downstream nonsense.

He was not wrong.

No holes, either side, either lobe. No scar tissue, no thickening, no
sign of a hole that had closed — and a closed piercing leaves a mark
for decades and he had seen four hundred of them.

Which was, professionally speaking, remarkable.

A woman of her age and position, in that country, in that century.
Every civilian woman over about thirty in the market row had pierced
ears. Kaburagi had them. Emiko had them. Yagi had one side done and one
not, which was a story he had never asked about and now intended to.
Mei Terumī had worn a single large blue drop on the left at the
delegation and had worn it, Sazare had noted at the time without
knowing why he was noting it, in a hole that was old and slightly
elongated from years of weight.

Tsunade had nothing. Never had.

He wrote:

*Finding: she has never in her life owned a piece of jewellery she
chose.*

*One necklace, inherited, worn every day for approximately forty
years, which she cannot remove and would not.*

*Nothing else. Ever. Not in the drawer, not on the shelf, not once.*

*She is one of the four wealthiest individuals in this country by
salary and has been offered, at a conservative estimate, several
hundred objects by people who wanted something. And she has no holes in
her ears, which means that at no point in fifty years has she wanted a
thing badly enough to sit down for four minutes and let somebody do
it.*

He sat back.

*That is not an absence of taste. That is a decision, made once,
probably young, probably about the necklace, and kept.*

*And I have spent eleven weeks trying to design her an object and the
entire problem is that she has already got the only one she will ever
wear, and it was given to her by somebody who died.*

---

He tried, anyway, for four more evenings, and produced three designs
and destroyed all three.

The first was a hair ornament, which solved the wearability problem
completely, and which he abandoned on the second evening when he
worked out that she put her hair up and took it down again at least
once a day and would lose it inside a fortnight and would then feel
bad about having lost it, which is the single worst thing a gift can do
to a person.

The second was a brooch, like Ōtaki Sue's, and he got nine evenings
into it before he understood that he was making the same object twice
because it was the object he knew how to make, and that giving the
Hokage a variant of a thing he had already given his neighbour was
either a compliment or an insult and he had no way whatsoever of
predicting which.

The third was a repair.

He drew it out fully before he noticed what he was doing: a new bail
for the necklace, properly matched to the eighty-year-old work, to
replace the one that had been re-soldered in the Second War by a field
medic with an instrument torch and four minutes.

He looked at the drawing for a long time.

Then he wrote, underneath it, in the flat hand he used for findings:

*This is not a gift. I have read the page. A repair creates an
obligation and this one would create a very large one, and I would be
proposing to take a mount off a stone that she has refused to have
taken off the stone for thirty years by four separate people, and I
would be doing it because it is the one thing I know how to do that
she cannot do for herself.*

*I have caught myself doing chapter four wrong four times now and every
single time the error has been the same error.*

He burned the drawing in the stove, which was theatrical and which he
did anyway.

---

Which left him, on the eleventh Thursday, with no design, no object,
eleven weeks of work in the failure column, and one remaining course of
action, which the book had told him about on page nine and which he had
skipped because it belonged to a chapter he could not do.

*Where an object is intended for a particular person and the maker
cannot determine what would suit,* said chapter four, *there is no
disgrace in inquiry. It is generally supposed that a gift must be a
surprise. This supposition is responsible for a very great quantity of
unworn cloth.*

*Chapter three,* thought Sazare Ruri. *It's chapter three again. It's
been chapter three for eleven weeks.*

He put the pencil down and looked at eleven weeks of drawings, and at
the tin of things nobody had asked for, and at a book from the domestic
economy shelf of a civilian library, and worked out that he was going
to have to walk into a bar and ask a woman a question about herself.
