# Chapter 24, Scene 4 — What You Owe a Street

*Act 4, part A. Konoha, the east side. Sazare Ruri, age sixteen. POV:
Sazare. ~2,700 words.*

---

He knew the exact morning he became a local, because somebody asked him
for something in the street without saying please.

It was the eleventh of the fifth month, a Tuesday, hot already at the
seventh hour with the light coming flat down the block between the
washing-lines and the dust going gold in it, and the whole east side
smelling of drains and hot tile and somebody's breakfast fish; and a woman
he had spoken to perhaps four times leaned out of a first-floor window
with her hair in a cloth and shouted, "*Oi.* Crystal. My shutter's
dropped," and went back inside without waiting for an answer.

Sazare stood in the street with a bag of rice under his arm.

Four years in the fog, he had been *the Sazare boy*. A year and a half on
the road, he had been *the mender*, which is what a town calls a man it
has decided not to ask about. Sixteen days in a cell he had been *the
subject*. In the round room at the top of the tower he had been *item
six*.

Nobody had ever once shouted at him out of a window.

He fixed the shutter. It had dropped because the top pintle had worn oval
in a soft pine stile — nine years of a woman with a heavy hand, and the
hole gone from round to egg-shaped, and every time she shut it the sag got
a fraction worse. Twenty minutes with a plug of hardwood and a rasp and a
new screw an eighth over. She gave him three plums and did not thank him
and did not ask him where he had learned it, and he walked to the hospital
with the plums in his hand feeling something he did not have a word for
and did not get one for another four months.

---

Ōtaki Sue lived on the second floor, directly under him, and was
seventy-one, and had been stone deaf since a fever at nine.

She was the one who had banged on her ceiling with a broom handle on the
Sunday he laughed on the floor at six seconds; and he had gone down the
next evening to apologise, which was a piece of pure social incompetence,
because he stood in her doorway apologising to a deaf woman in a flat
voice with his hands at his sides for the better part of a minute before
he understood what was wrong.

She let him do it. That was the thing. She stood there in the doorway —
four feet eleven, in a grey cotton wrapper worn white at the elbows, with
her hair cut off short and level at the jaw and a face like a folded paper
that has been opened and refolded along the same lines for seventy years —
and she watched his mouth and let him get all the way to the end of it.

Then she held up one finger, went inside, came back with a slate and a
piece of chalk, and wrote on it in a fast, square, beautifully controlled
hand:

*I FELT IT. FLOOR. WHAT WAS FUNNY*

And Sazare Ruri, who had lied by omission to every single person he had
met since he was fourteen years old, took the chalk and wrote:

*A THING I HAVE BEEN TRYING FOR NINE YEARS WORKED*

She read it. She looked up at him. And her whole face came apart into an
enormous gap-toothed grin, and she thumped him on the chest with the flat
of her hand, hard, twice, and took the slate back and wrote:

*THEN BANG ALL YOU LIKE*

They wrote to each other twice a week for eleven years.

He worked out why it mattered the third time he stood at her table with
the chalk in his hand and her kettle going, and when he did work it out
he had to put the chalk down for a second.

*She cannot hear my accent.*

That was all. She had never heard it and never would. Every other person
in that village — the kind ones, the incurious ones, Ashida who did not
care, Kanzaki who had stopped noticing, the ones in the market who were
perfectly civil about it — every one of them got four words into a
conversation with him and something in the back of their head filed him
under *Water*, and did nothing with it, and filed it anyway, every single
time, for the rest of his life.

Ōtaki Sue got his hand. His hand was nothing. His hand was a clerk's hand,
because his mother had made him write out stock lists at seven years old
and had made him do it *properly*.

He was, to exactly one person in the Land of Fire, from nowhere at all.

---

The market taught him the other half of it, and taught it to him the way
markets do, which is by short-changing him for eleven weeks.

The fish trestles were on the Nakadōri end where it widens, under an awning
of blue-and-white striped canvas gone bald along the folds, and the ground
there was permanently wet and permanently silver with scale, and it smelled
of ice and salt and the drain, and the noise of it at the sixth hour was
four men and about nine women all shouting numbers at once.

Tsuchida had the second stall. He was fifty, bald, red-faced, with forearms
like a docker and a hand missing most of a thumb, and he was not a
malicious man; he was a man with a scale that lived a little bit light and
a habit of using it on people who would not make a scene.

Sazare knew from the second week. He knew to the gram — he had a jeweller's
eye and eleven years of buying material by weight and he could feel four
hundred grams in his palm as easily as he could feel a flat — and he said
nothing at all for eleven weeks, and paid it, every time, because a
foreign national on probation does not have an argument on a market row
about eight per cent.

He priced it out one evening and it came to about nine hundred ryō a
month. He entered it in his own book under *rent*.

What ended it was O-Shige, who had the vegetable trestle opposite and was
about sixty and had a voice that carried to the bathhouse.

She watched the transaction on a Thursday in the sixth month. He put his
coin down; Tsuchida weighed; Sazare took the parcel and said thank you and
turned away.

And O-Shige said, across the row, at volume, to nobody, while sorting
beans:

"*Tsuchida*. That's the boy off the second floor at the hospital that did
my sister's husband's leg. Weigh it *again*."

The row went quiet in the way a room does.

Tsuchida looked at O-Shige, and then at Sazare, and then went red from the
collar up and weighed it again, and put a fish and a half more on the
paper, and did not say a word, and Sazare did not say a word either
because he had absolutely no idea what the correct thing to say was and
every instinct he had was screaming at him to get off that row.

He got off that row.

He sat on the wall by the bathhouse for about twenty minutes afterwards
with a parcel of fish going warm in his lap, in the sun, with the steam
coming out of the bathhouse vent and the smell of it, and had to work out
what had just happened to him, because he could not get it to fit
anywhere.

He got there eventually and it was this:

*I have been in that village for seven months. I did not ask her for that.
I did not know she knew who I was. I could not have engineered it — I have
spent my whole life engineering things and there is no version of that I
could have set up, because the entire content of it was that somebody who
owed me nothing decided in public, at a cost to her own trade, that I was
one of theirs.*

*I have no procedure for this at all.*

*The only thing I have ever known how to do with a person is be useful to
them first.*

---

Nemoto had the ground-floor front and had taught weapons at the Academy for
twenty-nine years and had been retired for nine, and he frightened Sazare
more than anybody else on that street.

He was sixty-eight and sat out on a stool by the door from about the fifth
hour in any weather that was not actually rain, in a faded blue jinbei, with
his stick across his knees and the last two fingers of his left hand gone at
the second joint, taken off — as Sazare established over four months and
about nine conversations — not in any war but by a lathe, in the fourth
year of his retirement, out of pure inattention, which Nemoto regarded as
the single funniest thing that had ever happened to anybody.

He had watched Sazare go up and down those steps two hundred times.

And in the fourth month he said, without any preamble, as Sazare came past
with his tool roll: "Here. Do that for me."

It was a kitchen knife. Cheap, worn back nearly to the spine at the heel,
with a split handle.

Sazare sat down on the step and did it. He had the stone out and wet in
about forty seconds and he took it right through — coarse, fine, and the
strop off the back of his own belt — and it took him nine minutes and it
came off the strop with an edge on it that would take the hair off a
forearm, which he did not demonstrate, because he did not do that.

Nemoto took it, and turned it over, and looked at the bevel down the light
for about four seconds.

"Who taught you to hold a stone?"

The street was very hot and very quiet and there was a cicada going
somewhere up in the elm at the top of the block and Sazare Ruri sat on a
step with his hands on his knees and did not move a muscle in his face.

"My mother," he said. "She was a fitter."

"Hm," said Nemoto. He put the knife down on his own knee. "It's the wrist.
You lock the wrist and walk the whole arm. Nobody does that unless
somebody stood behind them and made them do it about four thousand times."
He scratched his jaw with the two-fingered hand. "I made about six hundred
children do it and I got it into perhaps nine of them."

"She was very hard to please."

"They're the only ones worth anything," said Nemoto, "and you spend the
rest of your life wishing you'd told them so, and you don't, and then
that's that." He handed the knife back handle-first. "Do the other one and
I'll give you a plum."

"Everybody on this street gives me plums."

"It's a plum street," said Nemoto Genji. "Get used to it."

---

The Kurihara family had the two rooms at the back of the third floor and
had been in them for nine years, which is a thing that happens to
refugees: the temporary arrangement outlives the incident, and then it
outlives the memory of the incident, and then somebody's son is nine years
old and has never lived anywhere else.

The boy was called Tetsu and had decided, on no evidence, that Sazare was
worth following, and followed him — at a distance of about eleven feet,
with enormous transparent stealth — for most of the sixth month.

Sazare let him. Then in the seventh month he turned round on the stairs
and said, "If you're going to do it, carry something," and after that the
boy carried the tool roll, badly, with both arms, like a log.

Kurihara Nao stopped him on the landing in the seventh month with a bowl
of something in her hands and her sleeves still tied back, and she was
about thirty-eight and had a burn scar up the inside of her right wrist
and the flat, level, entirely unhurried gaze of a woman who has been
processed by two administrations.

"You're the crystal one," she said.

"Yes."

"Amnesty or asylum?"

Nobody in Konoha had asked him that. Not one person in seven months —
because the shinobi did not need to, and the civilians did not know there
was a difference.

"Amnesty," said Sazare.

"Mm." She nodded slowly. "That's the better one. Ours is the other. Ours
renews." She held the bowl out. "He's not to go near the Academy fence,
and if he asks you about it you're to tell him it's boring."

"Is it boring?"

"It's *the fence*," said Kurihara Nao. "Everything is boring from the
fence." And she gave him the bowl and went in and shut the door.

---

He did the accounting on a Sunday night at the end of the seventh month,
the way he did all his accounting, at the low table with the lamp and the
persimmon in full leaf outside the window and the block going quiet around
him: the tap on the landing dripping, somebody's radio two floors down,
Nemoto's stick going along the passage at about the tenth hour, every
night, exactly.

He wrote out both columns, because that was the only way he knew.

**What he had done for the street:** a shutter, four windows, eleven
knives, O-Shige's trestle leg, the bathhouse door closer, the stair-nosing
on the second flight, a mangle, three chairs, a kettle handle, the tap on
the landing twice, and Ōtaki Sue's window catch, which he had done four
times because she opened it with her foot.

**What the street had done for him:** plums; a bowl; a fish and a half;
two hundred grams a week of not being cheated; a slate and a piece of
chalk; a boy carrying a tool roll like a log; and a stool by a door where
an old man sat every afternoon and had never once asked him a second
question.

He looked at the two columns for a long time.

They did not balance. That was the entire finding and it took him about
half an hour to see it, and when he saw it he sat back on his heels.

*They don't balance and nobody is trying to make them balance.*

*Nobody on this street is keeping a total. O-Shige has no idea what
mending a trestle leg is worth and would be insulted if I told her.
Nemoto gives me a plum for nine minutes of work worth two hundred ryō in
any town in four countries and both of us know it and neither of us has
ever mentioned it. Kurihara Nao gave me a bowl of aubergine and a piece of
advice about the fence and she gave me the advice deliberately and the
aubergine was the excuse.*

*I have spent nine years making myself owed. It is the only technique I
have that works on people. You do the lantern, you do the lid, you do the
veranda, and then when they come for you there is a town of people who
each owe you one thing, and you get out of the district alive.*

*This is not that. I cannot get this to be that. I have been trying to
make it be that for seven months and it will not go.*

He put the pencil down.

Outside, the tenth hour, Nemoto's stick went along the passage below,
tap-drag, tap-drag, all the way to the front door, and the bolt went
across, the way it did every single night, for the whole street, without
anybody having asked him to.

*A ledger you cannot balance is not a debt,* thought Sazare Ruri, in a
rented room in a foreign country, at sixteen years and seven months old.

*It's an address.*
