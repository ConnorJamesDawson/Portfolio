# Chapter 37, Scene 1 — Six Chapters

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the civilian library. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,484 words.*

---

He asked Ochiai for it in the eighth month, at the desk, out loud, in a
building with four other people in it.

That was the hard part and it took him three visits. He had come in on
the first two, taken books out, and left, and on the second one had got
as far as the desk with the sentence assembled and had asked instead
whether the mineralogy shelf had been reordered.

The third time he said it.

"Is there a book about how to make friends?"

Ochiai was seventy-eight by then and had stopped mending spines because
his hands would not do it any more, and had moved to accessioning
instead, which he did slowly and with enormous care and which took him
about four times as long as it had taken the girl before him. He was
in the brown cardigan. It had been re-elbowed since the spring, badly,
in wool that did not match.

He put the accession slip down.

"Yes," he said.

Sazare had constructed nine responses to that question over three
visits and had not constructed that one.

"There is?"

"There are eleven, and nine of them are rubbish, and one of them is
rubbish in a way you'll find annoying rather than useful, so I'll give
you the other one." Ochiai got off the stool, which took a moment.
"Social conduct. It's shelved under domestic economy because the 1961
scheme thinks anything about people goes with the cookery. Second bay,
bottom shelf, and you'll have to kneel."

"You knew where it was."

"I know where everything is, I've been here since before your mother
was born." He was already moving. "I also know that shelf gets taken
out about four times a year and that in thirty-one years not one
person has ever asked me for it at the desk in a voice anybody else
could hear."

"...Should I not have?"

"You should absolutely have," said Ochiai. "It cost you something and I
watched it cost you, and I have been waiting three years to see what
you'd eventually come and ask for out loud, and I want to say that
this was not on my list."

---

The book was called *On Ordinary Acquaintance and the Forming of
Friendships*, it had been printed forty-one years earlier in a town
Sazare had never heard of, and it was one hundred and eleven pages
long.

He read the whole of it in one sitting at the furthest table with his
back to the wall.

It was, he decided by page nine, an extremely good book, and the reason
it was good was that it did not once tell him to be himself.

It told him what to do.

*The forming of a friendship,* said the introduction, *is generally
supposed to be a thing that happens to a person rather than a thing a
person does, and this supposition is the cause of a great deal of
unnecessary loneliness. It is a practice. Like any practice it may be
learned late, learned badly, and improved by attention.*

He copied that onto the back of a requisition form and kept it for
eleven years.

There were six chapters.

*I. Of Presence, and the going where people are.*
*II. Of Speech without Object.*
*III. Of Inquiry, and the asking after a person's affairs.*
*IV. Of Gifts.*
*V. Of the Hand and the Shoulder.*
*VI. Of Occasions, and the marking of them.*

Sazare read the contents page four times.

Then he got out a fresh sheet and ruled it into six rows, and put a
column beside it headed *STATE*, and filled it in.

---

*I. Of Presence.* — *Adequate. Possibly excessive. See Yagi, eleven
unrostered attendances.*

*II. Of Speech without Object.* — *Cannot do at all.*

He wrote that one and then wrote it out again longer, because it was
not accurate enough:

*Cannot do at all. Have never in my life sustained a conversation that
was not about a subject. Every exchange I have had with another human
being since I was thirteen has had an object in it — a wound, a joint,
a price, a fault, a form. Have observed other people doing this
constantly and had assumed it was a lesser activity. Have now read
eleven pages arguing that it is the load-bearing one and I am not able
to dismiss the argument.*

*III. Of Inquiry.* — *Cannot do. See Ibiki: "you have never once asked
me a question about anything that was not directly in front of you."
This is the second time somebody has told me this and the first time I
have written it down.*

*IV. Of Gifts.* — *Unattempted.*

*V. Of the Hand and the Shoulder.*

He stopped on that one for some time.

The chapter was about touch. He read the whole of it twice, standing up
at one point without noticing he had stood up, and what it said — in
the flat forty-one-year-old prose of a man who had plainly thought
about it a great deal — was that ordinary friendly contact is a
language of about nine words, that most people acquire it before they
can speak, and that a person who has not acquired it will find it
almost impossible to learn from description.

*I put my hands on eleven people today,* Sazare wrote.

*I have taken four pulses, held a shoulder still, moved a man's leg,
supported a woman under both arms while she stood for the first time in
nine days, and put two fingers on a throat to count. I did all of that
without thinking about it once. I have been touching strangers
professionally for four years and I am good at it and nobody has ever
flinched.*

*And I do not know how to put a hand on Ashida's shoulder.*

*I have thought about doing it perhaps forty times. I have never done
it. If a man came into my ward with a broken collarbone I would have my
hands on him inside four seconds and if that same man is standing at a
gate telling me his daughter has been ill I stand at a distance of
about three feet with my arms at my sides.*

*Two complete systems. Neither of them is the one everybody else has.*

*VI. Of Occasions.* — *See over.*

---

*Of Occasions* was about the marking of days: birthdays, name days, the
anniversary of a death, the day a person got a thing they had wanted.

He read it and thought about his ledgers.

*I know the following about Ashida Kōhei:* he wrote. *That his flak
jacket has been buckled wrong across one loop since at least the
spring. That his wife corrects his spelling when it matters and not
otherwise. That he re-sorted ninety-one newspaper clippings into date
order at some point rather than simply adding to the top. That his
second child arrived in the winter and took about four stone off his
sleep. That his boots are worn at the outside heel on the right, which
means he stands on that leg, which he has done since before I met him.*

*I do not know his birthday.*

*I do not know his wife's name. I have called her "your wife" for three
years and she has written a note in a folder that was for me.*

*I do not know how old either of his children are, or their names, and
one of them is nearly four.*

He sat back from the table.

*I have the most detailed file on that man of anybody alive including
his wife and it does not contain one single fact he would want me to
have.*

---

He took the book out on his card, which Ochiai stamped without comment,
and which brought his total to thirty-nine books out of the forty on
the natural history shelf and one from domestic economy.

At the door he stopped.

"Ochiai-san."

"Mm."

"What's your name?"

The old man looked up from the accession slips.

"Chapter three," he said. "You've started already."

"I've read it twice. I'm on the fourth page of chapter four and I've
just realised I've known you for four years and I call you by the name
on the desk plate."

"Sumiko," said Ochiai. "Ochiai Sumiko, which is a woman's name and was
my mother's, and my father registered it wrong at the office and never
corrected it, and I have been explaining that to people for
seventy-eight years and have long since stopped minding."

"That's—"

"It's a good story and you've never heard it because you've never asked
and I have never volunteered it, because volunteering it is a thing
old men do to trap the young into conversation and I have always
thought it beneath me." He went back to the slips. "Chapter three,
Ruri. It's the shortest one and it's the one that works."
