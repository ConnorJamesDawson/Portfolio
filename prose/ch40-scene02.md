# Chapter 40, Scene 2 — Four Bays Over

*Act 4, part D. Konoha, the civilian library. Sazare Ruri, age
eighteen. POV: Sazare. ~1,439 words.*

---

He took the book back in the third week of the second month, six months
after he had asked for it, which was five and a half months past the
lending period and which Ochiai had said nothing about on any of the
eleven occasions Sazare had been in that building since.

He put it on the desk.

*On Ordinary Acquaintance and the Forming of Friendships.*

Ochiai looked at it and then at him.

"Finished?"

"Five of six."

"Which one didn't you do?"

"Two. *Of Speech without Object.*" Sazare put both hands on the desk
edge. "I want to be accurate about this because I've written it up
twice and got it wrong both times. I did not fail chapter two. I
attempted it about forty times over six months and produced nothing,
and I have concluded that I am not going to be able to do it, and I
have stopped calling that a defect."

"That's a considerable distinction."

"It took me four months to arrive at and I got there in a stairwell
because a fourteen-year-old told me that four seconds is nothing." He
turned the book round on the desk so the spine faced Ochiai. "I can't
hold a conversation that isn't about a subject. I've tried. What I can
do is find the subject, and it turns out that if you find the subject
first, the other thing happens on its own underneath it, and nobody
except me has ever noticed that I was doing it the long way round."

Ochiai took the book.

"Somebody told you that."

"Ashida's wife. In a passage, holding two bowls." Sazare took his hand
off the desk. "She said I'd been standing up for two hours like a man
waiting to be told he could leave, and she gave me a bowl, and the
whole of the following hour was about a roof."

"And that was a conversation."

"That was three hours and I did not once know what to say and it did
not matter, because there was a roof."

---

Ochiai stamped the book in and did not put it on the returns trolley.

He held on to it for a moment, which he did not do with books, and then
he said:

"Thirty-one years I have had that on the bottom of the second bay. It
goes out about four times a year, and it comes back, and in thirty-one
years not one person has ever told me what happened."

"That's—"

"They bring it back and they put it on the desk and they say nothing and
they go, and I stamp it, and I have never once known." He put it down.
"So thank you. That's not politeness. I've wanted to know for
thirty-one years and I did not think I was going to."

Sazare stood at the desk.

"There's a second thing," he said.

"There generally is."

"I need another book and I don't know how to ask for it without you
drawing a conclusion, and I have decided that the conclusion doesn't
matter, and I would like to say that out loud first so that when you
draw it I don't have to pretend."

Ochiai's face did not move at all.

"Ask for the book, Ruri."

---

He asked for the book.

He asked for it badly, at length, and starting from the wrong end, in
approximately the manner of a man delivering a technical brief.

"I know anatomy. That's — I want to establish the baseline. I've done
four hundred hours of it under supervision and I sat a paper on it and
scored badly and then re-sat it and scored adequately, and I can tell
you what happens in a body and in what order and I am not asking about
that."

"No."

"I'm asking about the other thing. The one everybody else has." He got
it out in one piece, which cost him about four seconds and a
considerable amount he did not name. "There is a category of thing that
every person in this village does constantly and that I have been
watching for six years and filed under *behaviour I do not understand
the purpose of*, and I found out this year that it is not a lesser
activity and that I have never once run it, on anybody, ever, and I do
not know whether that is damage or whether it is late."

Ochiai waited.

"I've been told it's load," said Sazare. "By somebody who would know.
That nothing in my life has ever been slow enough or safe enough for
it, and that it turns up when there's room and that pushing it produces
nothing. And I believe her and I've stopped pushing at it."

"But."

"But I would like to *understand the mechanism*, and I have been told
that I cannot, and I have never in my life accepted that about anything
and I am not going to start with this one." He stopped. "There's a
shelf. There must be a shelf. There's a shelf for everything in this
building."

Ochiai got off the stool.

"There is a shelf," he said. "It is smaller than the shelf for rocks and
considerably worse, and about two thirds of it was written by people
who had never done it either. Third bay, second shelf up, and you will
not have to kneel."

---

The one he came back with was called *A Consideration of the Regard of
Persons, with Some Observations upon Courtship*, it had been printed
forty-four years earlier, and it was two hundred and eleven pages.

Sazare read the contents page at the desk while Ochiai wrote out the
card.

"It says courtship."

"It does."

"Is that the current term?"

"It was current when it was printed and it was already old-fashioned
then," said Ochiai. "I would not use it in front of anybody under
fifty."

"What would I use?"

"I have no idea. I am seventy-eight and my wife has been dead for
nineteen years and the last time I had cause to think about it Sarutobi
Hiruzen had brown hair." He stamped the card. "Ask somebody young.
That's chapter three, and you're apparently good at it now."

---

He took it, and thanked him, and then he turned round and walked four
bays across the same floor to the natural history shelf, and stood in
front of it with the courtship book under his arm.

Forty books. Thirty-nine of which he had had out at least once, most of
them three or four times, one of them — a schoolbook for
fifteen-year-olds with a diagram on page eleven — four times in a
single year.

He had not been in that bay since the ninth month.

He had been in that building eleven times since the ninth month and
every single one of those visits had been to the third bay or the desk
or the domestic economy shelf at the bottom of the second, and he had
walked past the rocks eleven times without turning his head, and he had
not noticed doing it.

*Five months.*

*I have not opened a book about crystal in five months.*

*I have written up eleven patients, four failures, a degradation curve
and about nine hundred observations. I have not asked one single new
question about what I am.*

He stood in front of the bay with the light coming down between the
stacks and the whole of it in front of him — the 1961 shelving, the
badly translated monograph from the eastern provinces, the argument
with a man called Warashina who was not in that library and never would
be — and put one hand flat on the spine of the schoolbook.

Then he took out three, because three was what the card allowed, and
carried them to the desk with the courtship book on top, and Ochiai
stamped all four without comment and without looking up.

"Ochiai-san."

"Mm."

"What's on the other side of the third bay?"

"Anatomy, veterinary, and eleven volumes of a legal digest nobody has
opened since the second war." Ochiai squared the cards. "Why?"

"Because I have just worked out that I have been in this building
eleven times without going four bays to my left," said Sazare, "and I
would like to know what else is in here that I have walked past."

Ochiai Sumiko put the stamp down.

"Everything," he said. "That is what a library is. Come back Tuesday
and I will walk you round it, and it will take about two hours, and it
will be the best afternoon I have had in nine years."
